# Practice above exam difficulty: does harder-than-test practice improve test performance in math, physics and engineering problem solving?

Tag key. Every finding carries [EVIDENCE TYPE | HOW READ]. Evidence types: META = meta-analysis; RCT = field randomized controlled trial; QUASI = quasi-experiment (incl. regression discontinuity, pre/post with comparison group); LAB-EXP = randomized laboratory experiment (usually small N, short delays); THEORY/SIM = derivation or simulation; REVIEW = narrative review or framework; OPINION = expert or practitioner opinion; VENDOR = vendor-authored study or vendor claim; HISTORICAL = memoir, official document or history. How read: FULLTEXT = full text extracted and searched by me; ABSTRACT = database or publisher abstract only; FETCH = page summarised by the fetch tool, numbers not re-checked against the PDF; SECONDARY = reported by a different paper than the original. Nothing below recommends a design.

## 1. Direct comparisons: practice harder than the test vs practice at test level (effect on the test, on retention, on transfer)

### Takeaway
I found no study in math, physics or engineering that varies practice difficulty relative to a fixed test while holding practice amount constant, so the direct comparison is unmeasured in the literature I could retrieve. The closest evidence is one quasi-experiment (N = 201) and one RCT (N = 620) of olympiad-level training, plus small cross-domain lab studies whose direction is mixed.

### Cited Findings
**Math training above curriculum level (closest analogs)**
- [QUASI | FETCH] Rebholz et al. (2022): third and fourth graders (N = 201; trained n = 50, controls n = 151) took a course preparing for the German Mathematical Olympiad (ten 90-minute modules; "challenging open tasks", structured writing, cooperative learning, games). Olympiad performance (third, most difficult level, z-standardised): Grade 3 B = 1.15, p = 0.048; Grade 4 B = 0.55, p = 0.041. Curriculum-oriented math test (DEMAT): Grade 3 B = 0.69, p = 0.164 (not significant); Grade 4 B = 0.45, p = 0.010. Caveats: trainees were teacher-nominated and self-selected, scored higher at pretest and were younger (covariate-adjusted); missing data 5% at pretest and 12% at posttest; B values are unstandardised as reported. — [Springer, Z Erziehungswiss 25(5)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11618-022-01106-y)
- [RCT | FETCH] Agarwal & Gaule (IZA DP 18381, Feb 2026, working paper): 620 olympiad-nominated students from 44 countries, randomized within matched quadruplets (2 to an 18-week Art of Problem Solving advanced combinatorics course, 2 to self-study e-book and plan with no live instruction or feedback). Final exam about 20 weeks in: intention-to-treat 0.165 SD (strata fixed effects, significant at 5%); about half of assigned students participated minimally and about 15% stayed fully engaged; extrapolated gain for a fully engaged student about 0.66 SD; IMO subsample (n = 39) combinatorics score +2.83 points against a control mean of 5.96 (p < 0.05). The exam was aligned to the course, i.e. the test was at training level, not below it. — [IZA](https://docs.iza.org/dp18381.pdf)

**Practice demand level vs test demand level (retrieval practice, other content)**
- [LAB-EXP and classroom | ABSTRACT] Agarwal (2019): middle-school and college students did retrieval practice with fact questions, higher-order questions, or a mix; "higher order and mixed quizzes improved higher order test performance, but fact quizzes did not." Retrieval practice of any type beat rereading or no quiz on delayed tests. — [DOI 10.1037/edu0000282](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000282)
- [LAB-EXP | FETCH] Same program, author's summary page: two college-lab experiments and one 6th-grade classroom experiment; fact-based retrieval improved a final fact test, higher-order retrieval improved a higher-order test, and mixed quizzes benefited both test types (Experiments 2 and 3). The page gives no sample sizes or effect sizes and does not report the effect of higher-order quizzes on the fact test. — [retrievalpractice.org](https://retrievalpractice.org/blooms)

**Hard vs easy training, including cells harder than the test (other domains)**
- [LAB-EXP | FETCH] Garcia, Kuai & Kourtzi (2013), perceptual learning of Glass-pattern forms, 36 participants over four experiments (16, 8, 5, 7). Abstract: "Hard training produced larger behavioral gains than easy training"; within-session learning stronger for hard training, F(1,14) = 10.7, p < 0.01; between-session learning stronger for easy training, F(1,14) = 7.7, p = 0.02; Exp 2: post-training performance after longer easy training was lower than after hard training, t(14) = 2.8, p = 0.02. Hard training was matched to the test difficulty and easy training was easier than the test. Exp 3 (n = 5) trained at 40 vs 50 degrees, harder than the 35 vs 55 degree test; no test-performance comparison for that cell was extracted (learning-index by experiment interaction ns, F(1,11) = 2.6, p = 0.14). — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00110/full); [Cambridge repository abstract](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/handle/1810/273524)
- [LAB-EXP | FETCH] Bao & Cowan (2023, J Intelligence): 70 children (ages 10.08-12.39), randomized to start a working-memory recognition task with the easier material (line orientations) or the harder material (irregular shapes), then switch. Children who began with the easy material transferred to the harder shapes better: first shape block M = 0.72 (easy-first group) vs 0.64 (hard-first group), BFincl = 3.44 (modest evidence); by the final blocks the groups were "almost identical"; training lasted about 30-40 minutes. — [PMC10057205](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10057205)
- [LAB-EXP | ABSTRACT] Spiering & Ashby (2008): difficult perceptual categorization; easy-to-hard vs hard-to-easy vs random order. Rule-based categories: "the three training procedures were equally effective." Information-integration categories: participants who began with the most difficult items "performed much better than participants in the other two conditions." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02219.x)
- [LAB-EXP | FETCH] Edmunds, Milton & Wills (Q J Exp Psychol, 2017): four experiments reported "substantial evidence that Spiering and Ashby's conclusions were in error"; the authors regard the hard-first benefit as likely a false positive and report that training on easy items speeds later learning of a harder discrimination (transfer along a continuum). Sample sizes not shown on the retrieved page. — [Exeter repository](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/28456)
- [LAB-EXP | ABSTRACT] Pashler & Mozer (2013), 7 experiments: adaptive or linear "fading" (exaggerated, easy examples first) "dramatically improved performance in training (unsurprisingly) but did not enhance learning as assessed in a final test" when the discrimination was on a single sensory continuum; when the relevant variation was embedded among category-irrelevant variation, fading "dramatically enhanced not only performance in training but also learning as assessed in a final test" (Experiments 4 and 5); the color-saturation benefit "proved transitory after feedback was withdrawn" (Experiment 7). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031679)
- [LAB-EXP, animal | FETCH] Turney (1976): rats trained on an easy discrimination learned a related hard discrimination faster than rats trained only on the hard one when transfer was "compatible" (Experiment II), with persistent negative transfer when incompatible; no easy-to-hard effect in Experiment I. N and statistics not on the retrieved page. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03214422)
- [OPINION/practitioner summary of a QUASI or LAB study, SECONDARY | FETCH] Tim Lee's practitioner article summarises Liu et al. (2018): SWAT trainees did three one-hour shooting sessions with or without a person in safety gear near the target (pressure); post-test under high pressure was "much better than on the pre-test" for the pressure group but "as poor" as pre-test for controls, while low-pressure performance improved similarly in both groups. N and numbers are not in the article (only in a figure). Not verified against the original. — [Force Science](https://www.forcescience.com/2022/09/desirable-difficulties-in-training-improve-skill-retention/)

**Practice performance vs learning**
- [REVIEW | ABSTRACT] Soderstrom & Bjork (2015): "improvements in performance can fail to yield significant learning" and "certain manipulations can have opposite effects on learning and performance"; people "often mistakenly interpret their performance during acquisition as a reliable guide to long-term learning." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000)

### Inferences
- In the retrieved math studies the outcome test was at, or aligned to, the training level; the only math cell with a test below the training level is the curriculum test in Rebholz et al., which was significant in Grade 4 and not in Grade 3, in a self-selected, non-randomized sample.
- Where a harder-than-test cell exists in the lab literature it is tiny (Garcia Exp 3, n = 5) or second-hand (Liu et al.).
- The lab evidence points in different directions: hard-first helped in one category-learning study and did not replicate in four experiments (Edmunds et al.); easy-first favored generalization to harder material in children (Bao & Cowan); fading helped final-test learning only with irrelevant variation present (Pashler & Mozer).
- In the retrieval-practice experiments, practice at lower demand than the test (fact quizzes) did not raise higher-order test performance, so matching of practice demand to test demand mattered; the reverse direction (higher-demand practice, lower-demand test) is not reported in the sources read.

### Gaps
- No RCT or quasi-experiment in math, physics or engineering comparing equal-amount practice at above-exam vs at-exam difficulty with a delayed test at exam level. Searches used: "training on harder problems than test transfer to easier problems", "practice with more difficult problems than test improves performance easier test problems", "asymmetric transfer training difficulty hard-to-easy", plus targeted searches on category learning and olympiad training.
- Not retrieved: the specificity-of-practice and transfer-appropriate-processing literature (the main theoretical argument for practice matching the test), motor "overload" training studies, Ahissar & Hochstein (1997) and Jeter et al. (2009) on easy vs hard perceptual training and specificity, hardest-case simulator training studies, Pyc & Rawson (2009) on retrieval effort.
- Agarwal (2019) sample sizes, effect sizes and the higher-order-quiz effect on fact tests were not retrieved. Physics-specific work (for example Hsu, Kalyuga & Sweller 2015 on when guidance should come in physics instruction) was not retrieved.

## 2. Overlearning: practice beyond criterion (Rohrer, Taylor, Pashler, Driskell) and how its benefits fade

### Takeaway
Extra same-session practice after criterion gives a retention boost that shrinks or vanishes over weeks in the best-controlled experiments (verbal facts, math procedures, and a large adaptive-system dataset), while the meta-analytic d of about 0.75 rests mostly on short retention intervals. All of this concerns amount of practice at constant difficulty, not harder items.

### Cited Findings
- [META | ABSTRACT] Driskell, Willis & Copper (1992): "overlearning produces a significant effect on retention of moderate overall magnitude and ... the effect ... is moderated by the degree of overlearning, type of task, and length of retention period." — [DOI 10.1037/0021-9010.77.5.615](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.77.5.615)
- [META | SECONDARY, FULLTEXT of citing papers] Rohrer & Taylor (2006) describe Driskell et al. as examining "51 comparisons of overlearning versus learning-to-criterion in experiments using cognitive tasks" with "a moderately large effect ... (d = .75)"; "only 7 of the 51 comparisons relied on a retention interval of more than one week, and the longest was 28 days"; "the largest effect sizes were observed for retention intervals lasting less than one hour." Rohrer et al. (2005) describe "11 overlearning studies with cognitive tasks" with d = .753, a majority with retention intervals of one week or less, none longer than four weeks, and a smaller overlearner advantage at longer intervals. Original paper not accessed; study counts differ by description (comparisons vs studies; the full meta-analysis may include non-cognitive tasks). — [Rohrer & Taylor 2006 (ERIC)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED505642.pdf); [Rohrer et al. 2005 (ERIC)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED505637.pdf)
- [LAB-EXP | FULLTEXT] Rohrer et al. (2005), 218 college students, geography facts learned by test-with-feedback trials. Experiment 1 (130 students; 10 city-country pairs; 5 vs 20 trials; tests at 1, 3 or 9 weeks): high learners recalled 70% at week 1 and 24% at week 9; low learners 31% and 17%. Experiment 2 (10-word list overlearned vs 20-word list underlearned): 64% at week 1 and 22% at week 4 vs 38% and 18%; "no such benefit remained at week four," and the underlearners recalled more words in total at both intervals (7.5 vs 6.4 at one week; 3.5 vs 2.2 at four weeks). Abstract: overlearning "(and its concomitant demand for additional study time) is an inefficient strategy for learning material for meaningfully long periods of time." — [ERIC full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED505637.pdf)
- [LAB-EXP | FULLTEXT and FETCH] Rohrer & Taylor (2006), 216 college students learning to count permutations of letter sequences with repeats. Experiment 2: 3 vs 9 practice problems in one session (the extra six are overlearning); on the first three practice problems (identical for both groups) accuracy was 90% (nine-problem group) vs 88% (three-problem group), and on the additional six problems given only to the nine-problem group it averaged 95%; the paper states the extra problems "had virtually no effect on subsequent test scores at either retention interval", with test scores at 1 and 4 weeks nearly identical (figure labels 69% and 67% at one week, 28% and 27% at four weeks; which bar belongs to which group is inferred), and "the extra effort devoted to additional problems produced no observable benefit." Experiment 1: splitting 10 problems across two sessions a week apart left the one-week test unchanged but "virtually doubled" the four-week score (figure labels 64% vs 32%, assignment inferred). The authors note that to their knowledge all earlier overlearning experiments used verbal memory tasks. — [ERIC full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED505642.pdf); [USF abstract](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1770/)
- [SECONDARY | FULLTEXT of citing paper] Reynolds & Glaser (1964), as quoted by Rohrer & Taylor: high studiers recalled 100% more than low studiers after 2 days but just 7% more after 19 days. — [Rohrer & Taylor 2006 (ERIC)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED505642.pdf)
- [META | FETCH] Arthur, Bennett, Stanush & McNelly (1998), skill decay and retention: only 30 of 178 independent data points reported degree of overlearning; the authors call its effect on decay relatively weak but caution that "this may be misleading" given few data points and a "fairly limited range" of overlearning; overlearning was not analysed by retention interval or task type. — [Arthur et al. 1998 PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/1998-arthur.pdf)
- [LAB-EXP | ABSTRACT] Rawson & Dunlosky (2011), 3 experiments, 533 students, conceptual material, items recalled correctly 1 to 4 times in the first session and relearned 1 to 5 times later: "The effects of initial learning criterion and relearning were subadditive, such that the effects of initial learning criterion were strong prior to relearning but then diminished as relearning increased"; their summary is initial criterion of 3 correct recalls then 3 widely spaced relearnings. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023956)
- [QUASI (regression discontinuity), VENDOR | FULLTEXT] Matayoshi, Cosyn & Uzun (EDM 2022), ALEKS: a mastery threshold of 5 vs 3 correct (score) gave a retention advantage of "about 0.03" at week 1 (95% CI 0.025 to 0.036), "dropping below 0.02 for week 2"; abstract: "after several weeks this difference has largely disappeared." — [EDM 2022 PDF](https://educationaldatamining.org/EDM2022/proceedings/2022.EDM-short-papers.27/2022.EDM-short-papers.27.pdf)
- [RCT-style randomized A/B, VENDOR | FETCH] Matayoshi et al. (JEDM 2025), ALEKS: April 2023 to November 2024, each topic start assigned 5% high threshold (score 5), 5% low (score 3), 90% default; learning outcomes from about 33 million data points (16,441,259 high; 16,474,845 low); retention from 1,404,725 data points from 806,279 students. High threshold: mean 6.6 vs 5.1 minutes, 7.4 vs 5.4 actions, learn ratio 0.789 vs 0.809. Retention differences start slightly below 0.03-0.04 and fall to "less than 0.02" after several weeks; topic-level mean and median difference 0.021 (5th-95th percentile 0.011 to 0.032) across 4,175 topics; correlation between topic retention and threshold gain -0.86; students who started with errors showed larger early gains (above 0.04) that decreased. Authors: "not a given that the same pattern would appear on other data sets and with other learning systems." — [JEDM 2025](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/900/254)
- [QUASI, classroom control | FETCH] Cen, Koedinger & Junker (2007), Cognitive Tutor geometry (page without authors or N): reducing practice on skills flagged as over-practiced saved about 22 minutes (12%) of tutoring time; post-test no condition effect (p = 0.772) and no interaction (p = 0.56); retention test two weeks later p = 0.602; "over practice does not lead to a significantly higher learning gain." — [LearnLab wiki](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Using_learning_curves_to_optimize_problem_assignment)
- [META | ABSTRACT] Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990): 108 controlled evaluations; mastery learning programs have positive effects on examination performance; stronger for weaker students; may increase time on task. (Mastery thresholds, not overlearning per se.) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543060002265)

### Inferences
- The fade-out pattern recurs across verbal facts (Rohrer 2005), a math procedure (Rohrer & Taylor 2006) and an adaptive-system dataset of 806,279 students (Matayoshi 2025), with the large benefits concentrated at short delays.
- The meta-analytic d of about 0.75 comes largely from short-interval comparisons, so it does not contradict the null long-interval math result.
- Matayoshi's -0.86 topic-level correlation suggests any extra-practice benefit sits where initial learning was fragile.
- None of these designs varied the difficulty of the extra practice, so they cannot say whether above-criterion practice at higher difficulty fades differently.

### Gaps
- Driskell et al. full text (comparison counts by task type, degree and interval) was not accessed; "benefits disappear around 38 days" appears in the project's earlier notes (quoted there from a 2025 paper) but I did not verify it.
- No overlearning experiment in physics or engineering problem solving, and none varying difficulty, was found. Pyc & Rawson (2009) on retrieval effort had no retrievable abstract.
- Arthur et al.: the project's earlier notes cite 189 data points and r = .08; my read found 178 independent data points and no numeric r in the text (the Table 4 values were illegible), so those two figures are unconfirmed.

## 3. Desirable difficulties and their boundary conditions (low prior knowledge, element interactivity, expertise reversal)

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence for "desirable" conditions concerns practice schedules (interleaving, spacing, retrieval) that lower practice performance but raise delayed performance on tests at the same difficulty. Documented limits include learner expertise (expertise reversal), unguided discovery (negative pooled effect), and very high processing load. Productive failure (attempting problems beyond current knowledge before instruction) has a moderate pooled effect with reversals for young learners.

### Cited Findings
**Schedule difficulties with test at practice difficulty**
- [RCT (cluster, preregistered) | ABSTRACT] Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung (2019/2020): 54 seventh-grade classes over 4 months, interleaved vs blocked assignments, then an interleaved review for both groups; unannounced test one month later: interleaved 61% vs blocked 38%, d = 0.83; teachers implemented it without training. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000367)
- [LAB-EXP | ABSTRACT] Taylor & Rohrer (2010): children practiced four kinds of math problems interleaved or blocked with spacing fixed; "interleaving of practice impaired practice session performance yet doubled scores on a test given one day later." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1598)
- [REVIEW | SECONDARY via Wikipedia] Wikipedia summarising Marsh & Butler (2014): "to be desirable, the tasks must also be achievable"; "Too difficult a task may dissuade the learner and prevent full processing"; easier methods can give "better temporary performance effects" that are mistaken for lasting gains. Bjork's own boundary-condition statements could not be retrieved (see Gaps). — [Wikipedia: Desirable difficulty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desirable_difficulty)

**Expertise and element interactivity**
- [REVIEW | ABSTRACT] Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003): "Instructional techniques that are highly effective with inexperienced learners can lose their effectiveness and even have negative consequences when used with more experienced learners." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4)
- [REVIEW/THEORY | ABSTRACT] Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller (2017): "the expertise reversal effect constituting a particular example of the element interactivity effect"; both effects "rely on equivalent changes in element interactivity with the changes induced by different factors" (changes in material type vs changes in learner expertise). The abstract makes no prediction about practice-task difficulty. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-016-9359-1)

**Guidance and difficulty**
- [META | ABSTRACT] Alfieri, Brooks, Aldrich & Tenenbaum (2011): 164 studies; unassisted discovery vs explicit instruction, 580 comparisons, d = -0.38 (printed CI "[-.44, .31]", presumably a typo for [-.44, -.31]); enhanced or assisted discovery vs other instruction, 360 comparisons, d = 0.30, 95% CI [.23, .36]; "unassisted discovery does not benefit learners, whereas feedback, worked examples, scaffolding, and elicited explanations do." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021017)
- [META | ABSTRACT] Sinha & Kapur (2021), problem solving then instruction (PS-I) vs instruction then problem solving (I-PS): 53 studies, 166 comparisons, Hedges g 0.36 [95% CI 0.20, 0.51] favoring PS-I; g 0.37 to 0.58 when implemented with high fidelity to Productive Failure; grade level, intervention time span and (quasi-)experimental nature contributed; "contrasting trends ... for younger age learners (second to fifth graders) and for the learning of domain-general skills, for which effect sizes favored I-PS"; publication-bias-adjusted estimate g 0.87 (as printed). The abstract does not state how test difficulty compared with the problem-solving phase. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105)
- [REVIEW | FETCH] Wulf & Shea (2002), complex motor skills: "situations with low processing demands benefit from practice conditions that increase the load and challenge the performer"; "practice conditions that result in extremely high load should benefit from conditions that reduce the load"; results from simple tasks may not generalize to complex skills. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03196276)

### Inferences
- The best-supported "desirable" manipulations change the schedule (spacing, interleaving, retrieval, order of instruction) while keeping item difficulty at the level of the test; none of the retrieved sources manipulates item difficulty above test level.
- The boundary conditions are stated in terms of learner knowledge and available guidance: benefits reverse or vanish with high expertise (guidance redundant), turn negative with unassisted discovery, and turn on when problems exceed current knowledge only when instruction follows.
- Productive failure is a case where tasks exceed the learner's current knowledge and still help, but the evidence base is for conceptual understanding and transfer after follow-up instruction, with reversals in young learners.

### Gaps
- Bjork & Bjork's own wording on when difficulties are undesirable was not retrieved: their lab-site PDFs were blocked to the fetch tool by robots.txt and I did not work around that. McDaniel & Butler (2011) was not retrieved.
- Sinha & Kapur moderators (math vs science, prior knowledge, problem type) and Hsu et al. (2015) physics results were not accessible. The project's earlier notes quote a worked-examples meta-analysis (g 0.43 or 0.48) that I did not verify.

## 4. Difficulty progression and fading: easy-to-hard vs mixed, the challenge point framework, and mastery-learning systems (ALEKS, Math Academy, Cognitive Tutor)

### Takeaway
Meta-analytic evidence favors performance-contingent (adaptive) increases in difficulty over fixed-step increases, with larger benefits for novices. The "optimal difficulty" claims (challenge point, 85% rule) are a conceptual framework and a simulation-based derivation, respectively, not tested in human math learning. Mastery systems target a "ready to learn" frontier and roughly 80% success; independent outcome evidence is modest, and none of the sources read documents assigning beyond-exam problems as a mastery criterion.

### Cited Findings
**Increasing-difficulty training**
- [META | ABSTRACT] Wickens, Hutchins, Carolan & Cumming (Human Factors, 2012/2013): 37 transfer studies on increasing-difficulty (ID) and part-task training; "ID training was successful when the increases were implemented adaptively but not when increased in fixed steps"; "Both strategies provided evidence that experienced learners benefited less, or suffered more." In ID training, early trials are simplified and difficulty rises toward the full transfer task, so practice starts below test difficulty and ends at it. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720812451994)
- [META | FETCH, document identity unconfirmed] A DTIC document attributing the analysis to Wickens et al. (2013) reports: "Overall, there was neither a significant cost nor a benefit to the increased difficulty training method (TR = 1.22, g = +0.03)"; non-experienced trainees TR = 1.10, g = +0.75 ("There were no data for experienced trainees"); immediate transfer g = +0.46 vs delayed transfer g = -0.36; adaptive schedules a "substantial" 36% relative benefit vs a 23% transfer cost for fixed or increasing schedules; the task-difficulty moderator was "inconclusive due to few primary study data points." A "transfer difficulty" moderator (g = +0.21 for "similar to training difficulty" vs g = -0.56 for "near transfer") appears but its category definitions were not verified. Numbers should be re-checked against the journal paper. — [DTIC ADA602128](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA602128.pdf)
- [LAB-EXP/quasi | ABSTRACT] Salden, Paas & van Merriënboer (2006), computer-based air traffic control training: four task-selection methods; each of two personalised conditions compared with a matched yoked control; the hypothesis that personalised adaptive selection was more efficient than non-adaptive selection was "only partially confirmed" and personalised efficiency-based selection did not beat preference-based selection; the transfer results were not reported in the retrieved abstract. — [Erasmus repository](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/31798)

**Optimal-challenge theory**
- [THEORY/SIM | FULLTEXT] Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen (2019): for stochastic-gradient-descent-based learners on binary classification, "the optimal error rate for training is around 15.87% or, conversely, that the optimal training accuracy is about 85%"; "training at the optimal accuracy proceeds exponentially faster than training at a fixed difficulty." Demonstrated by simulation (a one-layer perceptron, a two-layer network on MNIST digits, and the Law and Gold model of monkey perceptual learning), not by new human data. Assumptions: Gaussian noise (optimum 82% for Laplacian noise, 75% for Cauchy noise); a Bayesian learner with perfect memory "would learn equally well if hard or easy examples are presented first"; for batch learning "if learning is fast, then mixing in more difficult examples may be best"; "no formal theoretical work has addressed the effect of training accuracy on learning, a test of which is an important direction for future work." The paper notes staircasing procedures are commonly designed for about 80-85% accuracy and that participants given free choice "spontaneously choose tasks of intermediate difficulty." — [PMC6831579](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579)
- [REVIEW/framework | ABSTRACT] Guadagnoli & Lee (2004), challenge point framework: learning relates to information arising from performance, "which should be optimized along functions relating the difficulty of the task to the skill level of the performer"; it distinguishes task difficulty and skill level and "specific testable hypotheses" are listed. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3200/JMBR.36.2.212-224)
- [REVIEW | FETCH] A 2021 extension for coaching says the framework was "developed based mostly on empirical knowledge garnered through research on practice organization and the contextual interference effect"; its learning curves are conceptual, the optimum is "theoretical", and the paper gives no count of supporting studies or effect sizes ("a work in progress"). — [UBC preprint](https://msl.kin.educ.ubc.ca/sites/default/files/publication-files/PrePrint_ChallengeFrameworkforCOACHING_dEC2021_JSS.pdf)
- [LAB-EXP | ABSTRACT] Metcalfe & Kornell (2003): with free choice people "devoted most time to medium-difficulty items and studied the easiest items first"; when study time was manipulated, "best performance resulted when most time was given to the medium-difficulty items"; easy items showed steep early learning with little further gain; results "favored the region of proximal learning framework." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.132.4.530)
- [LAB-EXP, large-scale online | ABSTRACT] Lomas, Patel, Forlizzi & Koedinger (2013), math game, two experiments with 10K and 70K subjects: "in almost all cases, subjects were more engaged and played longer when the game was easier"; "the most engaging design conditions produced the slowest rates of learning." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2470668)

**Mastery-learning and tutoring systems**
- [RCT | ABSTRACT] Pane, Griffin, McCaffrey & Karam (2014), Cognitive Tutor Algebra I ("personalized, mastery-learning, blended-learning"): school pairs randomly assigned for 2 years; "no effects in the first year ... evidence in support of positive effects in the second year"; significant for high schools, not middle schools; "improve the median student's performance by approximately eight percentile points." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3102/0162373713507480)
- [RCT | FETCH] What Works Clearinghouse page for the same study: "Meets WWC standards without reservations"; 5,738 students in grades 9-12 at 73 high schools; outcome CTB/McGraw-Hill Algebra Proficiency Exam; the per-cohort table lists significance as "No" for both cohorts while a summary line says a statistically significant positive effect was found, and the page does not reconcile the two. The project's earlier notes cite 147 schools and more than 17,000 students from a news article; I did not reconcile this with the WWC sample. — [WWC study 82197](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/study/82197)
- [META | FETCH] Fang, Ren, Hu & Graesser (2019): 15 empirical studies (2005-2015), 24 independent samples; "ALEKS was as good, but not better than, traditional classroom teaching"; no significant differences by schooling level, principal vs supportive use, or outcome measure type; effect sizes "greater, when ALEKS was used for shorter periods of time rather than longer periods" (reported as surprising). — [Memphis repository](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/facpubs/7254)
- [VENDOR | FETCH] ALEKS knowledge-space paper (McGraw Hill/ALEKS authors): the outer fringe is "the set of items q not in K such that K ∪ {q} is also a knowledge state", "the set of items the student is 'ready to learn'", and the student's entry point to learning mode; an item usually needs a target score of 5 to be learned; learning-attempt success rates 0.809 (Sixth-Grade Math), 0.841 (Algebra I), 0.943 (College Algebra); five consecutive incorrect answers is a failed attempt. The paper does not discuss assigning problems above course level or over-practice and presents no external outcome data. — [ALEKS KST paper](https://www.mheducation.com/content/dam/mhe/research/prek-12/essa-tier-4/aleks-practical-perspective-knowledge-space-theory.pdf)
- [VENDOR claim | FETCH] Math Academy "how our AI works" page: quizzes are "most valuable ... when they are challenging yet not overwhelming"; quiz difficulty is tuned so "the student is expected to score about 80% on average" and a student above 80% gets "slightly more difficult question variations on the next quiz"; the knowledge graph stores "the easiest and hardest variations of problems within each topic"; when a student misses too many questions "we halt the lesson because further struggle is an ineffective use of the student's time." The page cites no studies and gives no outcome data, no numeric mastery threshold, and does not mention going beyond course or exam level. — [Math Academy](https://mathacademy.com/how-our-ai-works)

### Inferences
- Adaptive, performance-contingent ramps are the only difficulty-progression design with meta-analytic support in the retrieved material; fixed-step ramps showed no benefit, and the ID literature ramps from below test difficulty up to test difficulty, not above it.
- The 85% (simulation) and about 80% (Math Academy design target; ALEKS learning-attempt success rates 0.81-0.94) figures are the same order of magnitude but come from different kinds of evidence (derivation, vendor design, vendor logs); none was tested against delayed exam outcomes in math.
- Lomas et al. and Metcalfe & Kornell indicate that learner-chosen difficulty skews easy and that the most engaging condition was not the one with fastest learning.
- Independent outcome evidence for mastery-based systems is positive but modest and delayed (Pane year-2 effect; Fang "as good, but not better").

### Gaps
- No experiment found that varies the success-rate target or the ramp speed in math problem solving and measures a delayed exam; no easy-to-hard vs mixed-difficulty ordering study in math was found.
- Salden et al. transfer results, Renkl/Atkinson fading-of-worked-examples studies and Kulik-style critiques (for example Slavin) were not retrieved. "The Math Academy Way" draft was not retrieved; the project's earlier notes quote it as saying quizzes are deliberately harder than typical classroom quizzes and calibrated to 80-85% accuracy, which I did not verify. Cognitive Tutor's internal mastery rule (for example the 95% knowledge probability criterion) was not verified.

## 5. Competition and elite training practice (olympiad training, Cambridge Tripos example sheets, Landau's Theoretical Minimum, Soviet and Russian math circles): documented vs anecdote

### Takeaway
The documented material I could read describes how hard the exams or supplementary problems are, not controlled comparisons of practicing above test level. Cambridge Faculty documents call some example-sheet questions extras for good students but never rank them against Tripos difficulty; Landau's exam is documented mainly by one first-hand memoir; math-circle sources are descriptions without outcome data. Controlled evaluations exist only for olympiad-level training (one quasi-experiment, one RCT).

### Cited Findings
- [HISTORICAL/official document | FETCH] Cambridge Faculty of Mathematics, supervision guidance: lecturers' sheets "are supposed to cover the schedule and relate closely to their own treatment of the material"; the ideal is "basic questions followed by some supplementary questions intended as extras for the good or well-prepared students"; some lecturers add extra questions "for enthusiasts" or "for practice", usually starred; students are usually expected to attempt all questions; sheets per course match supervision count (four for 24 lectures, three for 16, two for 12); for revision, eight past Tripos questions is "about the right number for a third year course", chosen to be "pedagogically valuable, but not pure bookwork." The document does not say how sheet difficulty compares with the Tripos. — [Supervision in Mathematics (PDF)](https://www.maths.cam.ac.uk/internal/teaching/files/supervision_in_mathematics.pdf)
- [HISTORICAL/official document | FETCH] Cambridge Faculty "Advice on Preparing for Exams 2022": cover "around 3-5 years of past questions before the exams"; revision supervisions look in detail at "about two years' worth"; "Short questions are intended to be accessible to any student who has studied the course material conscientiously whereas long questions are intended to be more challenging"; says nothing on example-sheet difficulty relative to exams or on working beyond what is examined. — [Advice on Preparing for Exams 2022](https://www.maths.cam.ac.uk/undergrad/files/Advice%20on%20Preparing%20for%20Exams%202022.pdf)
- [HISTORICAL/memoir, first-hand | FETCH] Ioffe's memoir of Landau's Theoretical Minimum: Landau "gave me the programs of eight courses on theoretical physics" plus a separate mathematics exam; at each exam Landau posed a problem and returned every 15 to 20 minutes; problems were "sometimes very complicated" with about an hour per problem and two or three per session (example: a rotating dielectric sphere in a constant electric field); Ioffe says "only a dozen persons" passed in 1948 and that it took him "almost two years" (summer 1947 to June 1949); second-hand, a previous examinee told him which problems he got and Landau "reused a limited set of problems" while judging students mainly by how they solved them; Ioffe's reading list included English and German papers of around 100 pages. The memoir describes the exam's difficulty and preparation, not practice above the exam's level. — [Ioffe, arXiv hep-ph/0204295](https://arxiv.org/pdf/hep-ph/0204295)
- [HISTORICAL/encyclopedia, SECONDARY | FETCH] Wikipedia: Landau's exam "covered all aspects of theoretical physics" and "between 1934-1937 in Kharkiv and 1937-1961 in Moscow only 43 candidates passed" (cites Blundell and Ioffe); the page says nothing about difficulty relative to other standards or about how problem solving was taught. — [Wikipedia: Kharkiv Theoretical Physics School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharkiv_Theoretical_Physics_School)
- [HISTORICAL/survey, pilot | FETCH] Karp (2005, CIEAEM 57) on Russian specialised math schools: no discussion of circles; the "stagnation" period (1970s to mid-1980s) emphasised "a kind of skill at solving problems on college entrance exams" that were "indeed very difficult" but "oriented more toward speed, knowledge, and skills, than toward interest and contemplation"; the only data are a pilot questionnaire in one St. Petersburg school (18 of 25 eighth-graders and 19 of 27 eleventh-graders responded) that "does not claim to provide any kind of statistical proof." The paper does not say whether students practiced beyond the exams. — [Karp 2005](https://sites.unipa.it/grim/cieaem/cieaem57_karp.pdf)
- [OPINION/publisher description | FETCH] Publisher and translator blurb for Fomin, Genkin & Itenberg, Mathematical Circles (Russian Experience): the book targets topics "beyond the limit of school curriculum" ("extracurricular mathematics"); exams are not mentioned; the page presents no data or outcomes. — [cut-the-knot](https://www.cut-the-knot.org/books/circles/back.shtml)
- [QUASI and RCT] The only controlled olympiad-training evaluations retrieved are Rebholz et al. (QUASI, N = 201) and Agarwal & Gaule (RCT, N = 620), summarised under question 1. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11618-022-01106-y); [IZA](https://docs.iza.org/dp18381.pdf)

### Inferences
- In the sources read, the "elite practice" traditions are documented as hard exams (Landau), optional harder supplementary problems (Cambridge starred or "for enthusiasts" questions), and extracurricular problem material (circles); none gives data comparing outcomes of practice above exam level with practice at exam level.
- Landau's 43 passers and the dozen in 1948 describe a selective exam and a selected population, so they cannot be read as an effect of the preparation method.

### Gaps
- No official Cambridge statement that example sheets are harder than Tripos questions; forum claims (for example Student Room, Physics Forums, which surfaced in search) were not read and would be anecdote.
- No outcome data for Russian or Soviet circles, Kolmogorov or Gelfand schools; Karp's paper did not address circles, and the arXiv history of the Kolmogorov reform (1911.06108) surfaced but was not read. No longitudinal study of IMO or Putnam training was found.
- Fetch-tool extractions of the Cambridge, Ioffe, Wikipedia, Karp, Rebholz and IZA pages were not checked against the PDFs; the IZA summary only covered the first 100,000 characters.

## 6. Effect sizes, sample sizes, populations, and whether the outcome test was at or below practice difficulty

### Takeaway
The best-designed studies of delayed performance (Rohrer, Pane, Matayoshi) tested at the same difficulty as practice; the few cells with practice harder than the test are small, non-randomized, or secondhand. Schedule manipulations show large effects (interleaving d = 0.83; spacing roughly doubling a 4-week score), whereas amount-at-criterion manipulations show null (math) or small decaying effects (+0.02 to +0.03 retention).

### Inferences
- Across these rows, the test equals the practice difficulty in almost every case; only Rebholz et al. (non-randomized) and the small or secondhand lab cells have practice harder than at least one test.
- Magnitudes differ by manipulation type: schedule (interleaving d = 0.83, spacing roughly 2x at 4 weeks), guidance (discovery d = -0.38 vs 0.30), problem-before-instruction (g 0.36), amount at criterion (null in math at 1 and 4 weeks; +0.02 to +0.03 retention in ALEKS), adaptive vs fixed ramp (meta-analytic direction only in the abstract).
- The standardized effect of the olympiad-style interventions is available only for the RCT (0.165 SD ITT); Rebholz reports unstandardised B.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved: N for Matayoshi 2022, Taylor & Rohrer 2010, Spiering & Ashby, Edmunds et al., Cen et al.; standardized effects for Rebholz et al.; per-experiment Ns and effect sizes for Agarwal (2019).
- Items based on fetch-tool summaries (every row marked FETCH) were not re-checked against the PDFs. Rohrer & Taylor figure percentages were read from figure labels and their assignment to conditions is inferred.
