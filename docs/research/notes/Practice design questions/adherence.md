# Adherence in Self-Directed Spaced-Repetition and Problem-Practice Apps

Research date: 2026-11-02. Scope: what keeps students practicing consistently in self-directed practice apps, and which engagement features help learning versus only engagement. Facts only; no design recommendations.

**Reading guide.** Every finding carries a tag of the form `[DESIGN · STRENGTH]`.
- Design: `META` meta-analysis or systematic review; `RCT` randomized experiment or A/B test; `QUASI` non-randomized comparison; `OBS` observational, correlational or log data; `SURVEY` self-report survey; `CO` company-published, not peer-reviewed, sample sizes and methods usually undisclosed; `DOC` product documentation; `SEC` secondary source or aggregator.
- Strength: Strong, Moderate, Weak, Very weak. `UNVERIFIED` means not confirmed in a retrievable primary text.
- Numbers were pulled from the cited pages and PDFs through a fetch-and-summarize tool that limits verbatim quoting. Numbers are reported as returned; where the tool flagged an internal inconsistency in a source, it is noted. Some sources were retrievable only as abstracts, replies or secondary summaries (flagged).
- The target context (free, offline, no-account desktop app, deliberately hard unassisted practice) was used only to judge transferability.

## 1. What predicts continued use in spaced-repetition and practice apps (Anki/FSRS, Duolingo, Khan Academy, ASSISTments, MOOCs)

### Takeaway
Retrievable evidence on what sustains use is dominated by company-reported A/B tests (Duolingo) and descriptive usage data; no published dropout or survival analysis of Anki/FSRS users was found. Across Khan Academy, MOOCs and Anki surveys, most users fall short of recommended or intended usage (for example about 9% reach Khan Academy's recommended 30+ minutes per week; edX MIT/Harvard all-participant completion was 3.13% in 2017-18), and stated reasons for disengaging center on time and workload.

### Cited Findings

#### Anki and spaced-repetition users (survey and dataset evidence)
- `[SURVEY · Weak]` First-year medical students at one US school (UCF College of Medicine, Class of 2026): 89 of 120 responded (74.1%; the Discussion cites 78%, an internal inconsistency). 94% (84 of 89) used Anki; 67.9% used it at least 5 days per week (Table 2 lists 56 students, which does not match the text); 75% (63 students) "never or rarely" kept up with daily reviews from previous modules; 82.1% found Anki overwhelming; 67.9% felt anxious and 68.3% felt frustrated when reviews went unfinished; 34.5% lost sleep, 36.9% forgot to eat, 52.5% skipped exercise. No discontinuation rate, time-per-day figure or exam scores reported; the authors note the 94% usage may reflect response bias. — [Nour & Harris, Cureus 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12662189)
- `[SURVEY · Weak]` Same school, earlier cohort: 60 of 120 first-year students (50%) surveyed at Weeks 1, 7 and 13 of a 16-week module. "About 70% of participants utilized Anki"; the non-user share stayed at 28-29%; usage rose significantly from Week 1 to Week 7 (P = .001) but not from Week 7 to Week 13; the largest group used Anki seven days a week. Stopping, reasons for stopping and time per day were not measured; self-reported, single school. — [Harris & Chiang, Cureus 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9045547)
- `[SURVEY/OBS · Weak]` 165 survey respondents; 92 (56%) were daily Anki users. Daily use was associated with higher USMLE Step 1 scores (P = .039), not Step 2; better sleep quality (P = .01); no differences on other wellness measures (the survey covered stress, sleep and burnout risk) or extracurricular involvement. No adherence, continuation or reasons-for-stopping data. — [Wothe et al., J Med Educ Curric Dev 2023](https://doaj.org/article/7aaf357db5d54ad39fea3f672ad5aae4)
- `[DOC · n/a]` FSRS community dataset anki-revlogs-10k: "10k collections from Anki", "a random sample of collections with 5000+ revlog entries"; page metadata lists 938M rows; fields include day offset from the user's first review, rating (1-4), duration, elapsed days. The card says nothing about skipped days, review delays or limitations. — [Hugging Face dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/open-spaced-repetition/anki-revlogs-10k)
- `[RCT (A/B) · Moderate]` Duolingo half-life regression (ACL 2016, peer reviewed, company-run). Experiment I (HLR vs Leitner), six weeks, "just under 1 million students": daily retention for any activity +0.3%, new lessons +0.3%, practice -7.3% (p < 0.001). Experiment II (HLR-lex vs HLR), two weeks, 3.3 million students: any activity +12.0%, new lessons +1.7%, practice +9.5% (all p < 0.001). The abstract's "improve Duolingo daily student engagement by 12%" corresponds to Experiment II any-activity; the paper itself measures "daily retention". — [Settles & Meeder 2016](https://aclanthology.org/P16-1174.pdf)

#### Duolingo (A/B tests and company-reported correlations)
- `[CO · Weak-Moderate]` A/B test separating the daily goal from the streak (previously a streak required meeting the daily goal) versus keeping them combined: Day-14 retention +3.3% (relative); daily active learners +1% (relative); share of daily learners on a streak +10.5% (relative, in 20 days) and +19% among new learners; number of learners on a 7+ day streak up "over 40%"; "just over half" of daily learners now have a streak of at least 7 days versus "about a third" a year earlier. Stated tradeoff: fewer learners reached their daily goals. No sample sizes given. Published 2020-11-19 (author Anton Yu). — [Duolingo blog, "Improving the streak"](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)
- `[CO · Weak (correlational)]` Same post: "learners who reach a streak of 7 are 2.4 times more likely to continue using Duolingo the next day" (compared with learners without a streak); "almost 40% of learners active two days in a row with no streak had the 'intense' daily goal". Selection effects are not addressed. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)
- `[CO · Weak]` Streak Wager A/B (spend in-game currency to bet on keeping a streak 7 more days): Day-1, Day-7 and Day-14 retention increases described as "statistically significant", Day-7 largest at "+14%" (relative or absolute unspecified). Weekend Amulet: learners offered it were "4% more likely to come back a week later and 5% less likely to lose their streak". DAU "fall by as much as 5-10%" on weekends (observational). "Binge learners" were "much more likely to abandon the app" than paced learners (correlational, no figures). No sample sizes, p-values or baselines. Published 2017-05-10 (author Kai Herng Loh, Growth PM). — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals/)
- `[SEC · Very weak]` Third-party Vietnamese translation of J. Mazal's "How Duolingo reignited user growth": CURR (current-user retention rate) +21% (relative or absolute unclear), DAU 4.5x in 4 years, leaderboards total learning time +17% after launch, share of DAU with a 7+ day streak "nearly tripled"; the author reportedly says statistical significance for CURR had not been worked out and that lower churn among 10-day-streak users may be correlation and selection bias. Not checked against the original (paywalled) Lenny's Newsletter text. — [Mazal translation](https://substakvietnam.substack.com/p/duolingo-a-khoi-day-su-tang-truong)

#### Khan Academy
- `[OBS (longitudinal, vendor-published) · Weak-Moderate]` About 350K students in grades 3-8 with fall and spring MAP Growth scores in 2022-23 (about 221K with two or more years); students compared with their own prior years; not an RCT. 30+ minutes per week (18+ hours per school year): "~20%" greater-than-expected gains, effect size .36 (as stated in the post; metric definition not retrieved; the post also says "20-30%"). 9-18 hours per year: about 7% greater-than-expected gains (about 15% of sample, about 55K students). "Approximately 9% (~33K) of students in the sample reached the recommended dosage" of 30+ minutes per week. Published 2024-12-06 (Yamkovenko & Bergstrom). — [Khan Academy Efficacy Results, Nov 2024](https://blog.khanacademy.org/?p=18194)
- `[OBS · Weak (secondary summary of a peer-reviewed paper)]` Khan Academy post on a PNAS paper (DOI 10.1073/pnas.2507708123): roughly 200,000 students across hundreds of US school districts; "a statistically significant positive relationship" between usage and MAP Growth gains; average use "about 10-15 minutes per week" versus the recommended 30; the analysis "explicitly accounts for" unmeasured factors including student motivation but the post does not say how; no effect sizes in the post. The PNAS paper itself was not retrievable. — [Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/national-study-in-top-journal-finds-khan-academy-learning-gains-after-accounting-for-key-unmeasured-factors/)

#### ASSISTments and mastery-practice persistence
- `[RCT · Moderate-Strong]` Maine randomized field trial: 2,850 seventh-grade math students in 43 schools; ASSISTments online homework (timely feedback and hints, plus teacher information and training) significantly increased end-of-year standardized math scores; "Students with low prior mathematics achievement benefited most." Effect g = 0.18 SD, t(20) = 2.992, p = 0.007. Usage and completion statistics are not on the retrieved pages. — [SRI abstract](https://www.sri.com/publication/education-learning-pubs/online-mathematics-homework-increases-student-achievement/); [SRI "How big is that?"](https://www.sri.com/publication/education-learning-pubs/digital-learning-pubs/how-big-is-that-reporting-the-effect-size-and-cost-of-assistments-in-the-maine-homework-efficacy-study/)
- `[OBS · Moderate]` Skill Builder (3-correct-in-a-row mastery) log data: 23,896 students from 298 middle schools, 619 problem sets. Under the authors' definition 9.1% of 75,481 student-problem set pairs were wheel-spinning (worked 10+ problems, reached 3 correct in a row on or after problem 10, but failed the delayed ARRS retention check); under Beck & Gong's definition (10+ opportunities without mastery) 46.9%. Of non-persistent pairs (fewer than 10 problems), 42.7% quit without mastery. A 15-feature decision tree reached AUC = 0.684; top features were minimum hints requested in any problem, maximum bottom-out hints in the last 8 problems, and the standard deviation of time between problems; students were more likely to wheel-spin when they "do not request any hints in at least one problem". The authors cite that "A student who persists unsuccessfully may face eventual reduced motivation" and describe all findings as correlational. — [Kai et al., JEDM](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/ShiminJEDM.pdf)

#### MOOC dropout and attrition
- `[OBS · Moderate]` Three Coursera computer science MOOCs; active learners: 46,096 (course labelled high-school level, CS101), 26,887 (undergraduate level), 21,108 (graduate level). Trajectory shares (Auditing / Completing / Disengaging / Sampling): HS-level 6% / 27% / 28% / 39%; UG-level 6% / 8% / 12% / 74%; GS-level 9% / 5% / 6% / 80%. Most prominent survey reasons for disengaging: "personal commitments, work conflict, and course workload". Completing learners had the highest forum activity (mean 0.788 HS, 1.71 UG, 7.18 GS versus 0.189, 0.238, 1.98 for Disengaging); Disengaging and Sampling learners reported lower overall experience in the HS and GS courses. — [Kizilcec, Piech & Schneider 2013](https://web.stanford.edu/~cpiech/bio/papers/deconstructingDisengagement.pdf)
- `[OBS · Moderate (abstract); SEC for numbers]` "The vast majority of MOOC learners never return after their first year"; completion rates are low and have not improved over six years. Secondary coverage: edX MIT/Harvard 2012-2018, 5.63 million learners and 12.67 million registrations; all-participant completion 3.13% in 2017-18, about 4% in each of the two prior years and nearly 6% in 2014-15; verified-track completion 46% in 2017-18 versus 56% in 2016-17; share of first-time MOOC users enrolling in a MOOC the following year fell from 38% (2012-13) to 7% (2016-17). — [Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente abstract (MIT TSL)](https://tsl.mit.edu/research/the-mooc-pivot/); [Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/node/7744)
- `[OBS · Weak-Moderate]` Two MOOCs: completion-based success 6.5% and 5.6%; success judged from learners' own intentions plus subsequent behavior 59% and 70%. — [Henderikx, Kreijns & Kalz 2017](https://oerknowledgecloud.org/record1748)

### Inferences
- The only large-scale (hundreds of thousands to millions of users) experimental evidence on scheduling and retention comes from Duolingo and is peer reviewed only for the HLR (ACL) and notification-bandit (KDD, see Section 2) studies; streak results are company blog posts with undisclosed sample sizes.
- Anki-related adherence evidence is limited to small single-site medical-student surveys where Anki use is tied to high-stakes licensing exams, so the 94% usage figure likely reflects external pressure rather than free-choice adherence (inference).
- The public FSRS dataset is a sample of collections with 5000+ review-log entries, so it structurally excludes people who quit early; it cannot by itself describe dropout (inference from the dataset card).
- Reported "predictors" of continuation in company data (7-day streak, 2.4x next-day continuation) are correlational and confounded by selection; the same company states the tradeoff that separating streak from goal reduced goal completion while raising streak holding.
- "Dropout" depends on the success definition: completion-based rates (5.6-6.5%) versus intention-based rates (59-70%) differ by an order of magnitude in two MOOCs.
- Stated reasons for disengaging in the one MOOC survey retrieved are time and workload rather than difficulty (survey-based, MOOC population).

### Gaps
- No published retention, survival or dropout analysis of Anki or other flashcard-app users was found (including FSRS community material); the dataset card has no skipped-day or delay information.
- No peer-reviewed analysis of what predicts continued use in Khan Academy or ASSISTments beyond dosage associations and the wheel-spinning logs; ASSISTments usage and completion rates in the Maine trial were not on the retrieved pages.
- The Khan Academy PNAS paper itself, Kizilcec, Pérez-Sanagustín & Maldonado (2017) on self-regulated learning strategies predicting MOOC goal attainment (abstract not retrievable), and Lenny's Newsletter/podcast Duolingo figures (paywalled) were not retrieved.
- Anki's default daily limits and the Load Balancer, Postpone and Advance documentation were not confirmed from the retrieved Anki manual page.

## 2. Which engagement features improve learning outcomes versus only engagement

### Takeaway
Evidence that engagement features raise engagement or retention is considerably stronger than evidence that they raise learning. Gamification meta-analyses show small-to-medium effects (g = .25 to .49) that lose significance for motivation and behavior in methodologically rigorous subsets; planning and nudge effects from small studies shrink roughly an order of magnitude or go null at scale; expected tangible rewards undermine free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.34) in a large but contested meta-analysis.

### Cited Findings

#### Streaks
- `[Experimental (seven studies; designs not retrieved) · Moderate]` Seven studies: "intact streaks highlighted via behavioral logs increase consumers' subsequent engagement" relative to highlighted broken streaks; the effect is "independent of actual past behavior" and depends on how behavior is represented in the log; it is amplified when consumers attribute a break to themselves and attenuated when a broken streak can be "repaired". A streak is defined as behaviors performed consecutively three or more times. Populations and field data not retrieved. — [Silverman & Barasch 2023, J Consumer Research](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb/full)
- `[OBS · Moderate-Weak]` Habit formation, 96 volunteers recruited (82 completed) over 84 days, self-reported automaticity of a chosen daily health behavior: mean 66 days to maximum automaticity, ranging from 18 days to a predicted 254 days (upper figure extrapolated); "a single missed day had little impact on later automaticity gains" but too many missed repeats lowered the maximum automaticity reached; about half of participants had not reached a high enough automaticity score after 84 days; exercise behaviors took about 1.5 times as long to plateau as eating or drinking behaviors. Behaviors were health habits, not learning. — [BPS Research Digest on Lally et al. 2010](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit); UCL release: "missing one opportunity did not significantly impact the habit formation process" and "people who were very inconsistent in performing the behaviour did not succeed in making habits" — [UCL News 2009](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit)
- `[CO · opinion]` Duolingo's stated rationale for decoupling streak from daily goal: "lowering the barriers to building a consistent daily habit is more important" than how much learners do each day, at least early on. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)
- `[SEC · Very weak]` Podcast page summary (transcript paywalled): "600+ experiments" on streaks; changing copy from "continue" to "commit to my goal" and an opt-out letting users choose streak duration each "significantly increased retention" per the page summary; no figures. — [Lenny's Newsletter page](https://lennysnewsletter.com/p/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks)
- No retrieved study measures the effect of streak features on learning outcomes (see Gaps).

#### Goals, planning and commitment devices
- `[META · Strong (general goal attainment, mostly non-academic)]` Implementation intentions: 94 independent tests, medium-to-large effect d = .65 on goal attainment; effective for initiating goal striving, shielding ongoing goal pursuit from unwanted influences and disengaging from failing courses of action; mediated by enhanced accessibility of specified opportunities and automated goal-directed responses. — [Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f)
- `[RCT (small) · Weak-Moderate]` 66 second-year high-school students (mean age 16.07): a 30-minute written mental contrasting with implementation intentions task versus a placebo essay; over the summer, intervention students "completed more than 60% more practice questions" in a PSAT workbook (about 140 versus about 84), beta = 0.27, p = 0.04; no outcome effect size reported; no goal-commitment measure collected. — [Duckworth et al. 2011](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/duckworth_self-regulation_11.pdf)
- `[RCT (natural field experiment) · Moderate]` Three HarvardX courses: planning prompts "increased course completion by 29%, and payment for certificates by 40%" (relative or percentage-point change not specified; N not in abstract); "students whose plans focused on specific times were unlikely to complete the course". — [Yeomans & Reich 2017](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/137973)
- `[RCT · Moderate]` Four large-scale RCTs of planning prompts in MOOCs: "an overall null effect on the completion rate", effects beyond [-7%, 3%] ruled out; in one course the prompt raised completion by 19%; random causal forests suggest differential effects by subgroup. — [Andor, Fels, Renz & Rzepka 2018](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:zbw:rwirep:790)
- `[RCT at scale · Strong]` About one quarter million students across 247 online courses (Harvard, MIT, Stanford, 2.5 years): "Self-regulation interventions raised student engagement in the first few weeks but not final completion rates"; value-relevance interventions raised completion in developing countries only in courses with a global achievement gap; the authors expected "medium-to-large effects as in prior studies" and did not find them; machine learning could not predict where gaps occur. Four arms: plan-making, reflecting on benefits and barriers, social accountability (pick someone to track progress), value relevance. Kizilcec said effects at scale were "an order of magnitude smaller" than in earlier small studies (Cornell paraphrase; the Cornell page says 250 MOOCs and about 250,000 students versus the abstract's 247 courses). — [Kizilcec et al. 2020 PNAS abstract (MIT DSpace)](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/135236); [Cornell news](https://news.cornell.edu/node/319503)
- `[RCT · Moderate]` 657 students in a Stanford-hosted MOOC (working paper, April 2015; the published JEBO 2018 numbers may differ): commitment device (pre-commit to time limits on distracting Internet activities) led to "24% more time working on the course", grades 0.29 SD higher and 40% greater likelihood of completion; the reminder tool and the focusing tool had no effect (not statistically distinguishable from control). — [Patterson, CHERI WP165](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/entities/publication/0ccc8fbd-c323-4536-b4a3-dd587b4f643c)
- `[QUASI · Very weak]` College engineering technology courses: 152 students (77 implementation-intention, 23 related-goal control, 52 unrelated control; 37 in engineering technology); groups were "blindly split" and some students were moved after the fact (not described as randomized); the results "do not support the benefit of implementation intentions" for low-point assessments; the implementation group did best only in a subset who said assessments were worthwhile, "not statistically significant". Outcome was assessment grades, not completion rates; the paper's tables were garbled in extraction. — [ASEE conference paper](https://peer.asee.org/an-introductory-study-of-the-impact-of-implementation-intentions-on-assignment-completion-rates-with-an-emphasis-on-engineering-technology-students.pdf)

#### Progress monitoring and visualization
- `[META · Strong (general goals)]` 138 randomized studies, N = 19,951: interventions increased monitoring frequency (d+ = 1.98, 95% CI [1.71, 2.24]) and promoted goal attainment (d+ = 0.40, 95% CI [0.32, 0.48]); frequency of monitoring mediated the effect; larger effects when outcomes were reported or made public and when progress information was physically recorded. — [Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/)
- `[META (systematic review) · Moderate]` 38 studies of learning analytics dashboards: "there is no evidence to support the conclusion that LADs have lived up to the promise of improving academic achievement"; "Most studies reported negligible or small effects"; motivation and attitude effects "modest"; participation showed a "relatively substantial" impact with "Several studies" at medium to large effect sizes; "limited evidence from well-powered controlled experiments", small samples and self-selection. — [Kaliisa et al., LAK 2024 (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15042)

#### Reminders and notifications
- `[RCT (A/B) · Moderate]` Duolingo sleeping-recovering bandit for recurring notifications versus uniform-random template choice among eligible templates (control as extracted from Section 3.2 of the paper; the abstract says only "a strong baseline" and the two descriptions were not reconciled): abstract "0.5 % increase in total daily active users" and "2 % increase in new user retention over a strong baseline"; Table 8 after two weeks: DAUs +0.5%, lessons completed +0.4%, existing-user D1 retention +0.5% (all p < 0.05), existing-user D7 +0.2% (not significant), new-user D1 +2.2%, new-user D7 +2.0% (p < 0.05); five months after launch the 95% on the bandit had 2.5% higher reward than a 5% random holdout; the authors warn that repeated templates "will get repetitive, desensitize users, and fail to improve engagement in the long run". Sample sizes, opt-out and unsubscribe rates not reported. — [Yancey & Settles, KDD 2020](https://research.Duolingo.com/papers/yancey.kdd20.pdf)
- `[RCT · Moderate]` Nearly 25,000 college students across three campuses: online and text-message interventions including coaching produced "some improvement" in mental health and study time, but "none of the interventions we evaluate significantly influences academic outcomes"; students study about five to eight hours a week less than they planned and the interventions did not change this. — [Oreopoulos & Petronijevic, NBER w26059](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26059)
- Patterson's reminder tool had no effect (see Goals above).

#### Social accountability and competition
- `[RCT at scale · Strong]` The social-accountability arm in Kizilcec et al. 2020 (choose a person to track your progress and plan what to tell them) was grouped with the planning interventions that "boosted engagement for the first few weeks" with an impact that "faded as courses progressed"; per-arm effect sizes were not retrieved. — [Cornell news](https://news.cornell.edu/node/319503)
- `[META · Moderate]` Competition combined with collaboration: behavioral outcomes g = .70 [0.41, 0.99] versus no social interaction g = -.06; motivational outcomes g = .61 [0.36, 0.85] versus competition alone g = -.09; no significant social-interaction differences for cognitive outcomes. — [Sailer & Homner 2020](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/109056/109056.pdf)
- `[QUASI · Weak]` Listed in a critical review table: Christy & Fox (2014), 80 women, leaderboards "create stereotype threat". — [Dichev & Dicheva 2017 table](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-017-0042-5/tables/13)
- `[CO · Very weak]` Duolingo leagues post (2023-05-03): leaderboards first tested in 2018, "a little competition worked for a lot of learners"; opt-out exists ("aren't for everyone"); Duolingo monitors for illegitimate XP gains and removes those learners; no numeric results. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-leagues-leaderboards/)

#### Gamification: meta-analyses and field studies
- `[META · Moderate]` Random-effects Hedges g: cognitive g = .49 (SE .10, 95% CI [0.30, 0.69]), k = 19, N = 1686, I2 = 72.21%; motivational g = .36 [0.18, 0.54], k = 16, N = 2246, I2 = 75.13%; behavioral g = .25 [0.04, 0.46], k = 9 (10 experiments), N = 951, I2 = 63.80%. In the high-rigor subsplit (experimental or quasi-experimental with pretests): cognitive g = .42 [0.14, 0.68] (k = 9, N = 686) still significant; motivational g = .22 [-0.11, 0.56] (k = 7, N = 1063) and behavioral g = .27 [-0.16, 0.70] (k = 5, N = 667) not significant. Moderators: cognitive g in school settings 1.12 [0.68, 1.57] versus higher education .41 [0.25, 0.57]; motivational outcomes in quasi-experimental studies g = .51 [0.35, 0.68] versus experimental g = .13 [-0.23, 0.50]; game fiction raised behavioral g to .41 [0.31, 0.51] versus .10 [-0.14, 0.34] without (behavioral outcomes mostly measured during interventions, so possibly effort on tasks); duration made no difference for cognitive or behavioral outcomes, while for motivational outcomes interventions of half a year or less (g = .59 [0.39, 0.79]) beat those of one day or less (g = .19 [-0.07, 0.45]). Only one study had a delayed posttest; the authors say the results can weaken the fear that effects do not persist but "does not allow for any conclusions about how enduring the obtained effects will be". Publication-bias tests were non-significant. Learner skill levels were mostly not reported. — [Sailer & Homner 2020, Educ Psychol Rev](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/109056/109056.pdf)
- `[META · Moderate]` 22 experimental studies (2008-2023): Hedges g = 0.782 (random effects) on academic performance, described as "a moderately positive effect"; N, effect-size count, moderator values and publication-bias results not retrievable from the abstract page (this g is larger than Sailer & Homner's, with fewer studies and a different inclusion window). — [Zeng et al. 2024, BJET](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/exploring-the-impact-of-gamification-on-students-academic-performance-a-comprehensive-metaanalysis-of-studies-from-the-year-2008-to-2023/)
- `[RCT · Moderate]` N = 273 (mean age 32.80), single-session tagging task: points, leaderboard and levels increased tag quantity (points vs control t(141) = 2.613, d = .44; leaderboard vs points d = .39; levels vs points d = .35) but not tag quality (F(3, 265) = .727, p = .537); no effect on intrinsic motivation (p = .702), competence or autonomy; authors: "points, levels and leaderboards functioned as extrinsic incentives, effective only for promoting performance quantity" and "game elements did not decrease intrinsic motivation" in this study. — [Mekler et al. 2017, Computers in Human Behavior](https://glassmanlab.seas.harvard.edu/annotated_works/mekler17.pdf)
- `[QUASI · Weak]` 80 students, one-semester communication course, gamified version (badges, virtual coins, leaderboard, pseudonyms; earning badges mandatory) versus otherwise identical non-gamified version: gamified students reported "significantly lower levels of class satisfaction", lower intrinsic motivation and lower final-exam grades; the authors suggest intrinsic motivation mediated the grade difference and propose social comparison or reduced autonomy as explanations. Statistics (betas, p values) were not retrievable. — [Hanus & Fox 2015, Computers & Education](https://www.smhp.psych.ucla.edu/pdfdocs/gamil.pdf); sample and course from [Dichev & Dicheva 2017 table](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-017-0042-5/tables/13)
- `[QUASI/RCT · Weak]` Same review table: Long & Aleven (2014), 267 students, "Does not improve learning". — [Dichev & Dicheva 2017 table](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-017-0042-5/tables/13)
- `[CO · Weak]` Duolingo distinguishes engagement from learning: learning-efficacy studies are "too slow" for daily A/B tests, so it uses "Time Spent Learning Well" (TSLW = minutes on Path + 0.5 x minutes in other lessons) as a proxy; features are said to "positively impact TSLW", not shown to improve learning; it cites an independent Northern Arizona University and East Carolina University study finding that "the number of completed lessons was the strongest predictor of learning gains"; leaderboard XP rebalancing toward path lessons yielded about +1.1M minutes per day (partial) and about +1.8M minutes per day (full); no sample sizes. Published 2024-06-13 (Rachel Salvador). — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/time-spent-learning-well)

#### Extrinsic rewards and the overjustification effect
- `[META · Strong, contested]` 128 studies (94 articles and 19 dissertations; free-choice k = 101, self-report k = 84). Free-choice intrinsic motivation was significantly undermined by engagement-contingent (d = -0.40), completion-contingent (d = -0.36) and performance-contingent (d = -0.28) rewards, and by all rewards (d = -0.24, 95% CI [-0.29, -0.19], k = 101), all tangible rewards (d = -0.34, [-0.39, -0.28], k = 92) and all expected rewards. Self-reported interest was undermined by engagement-contingent (d = -0.15), completion-contingent (d = -0.17), tangible (d = -0.07, [-0.13, -0.01], k = 70) and expected rewards, while all rewards on self-report was not significant (d = 0.04, [-0.02, 0.09], k = 84). Unexpected tangible rewards: free-choice d = 0.01 [-0.20, 0.22], k = 9 (no effect). Positive feedback enhanced free-choice (d = 0.33) and self-reported interest (d = 0.31). Tangible rewards were more detrimental for children than college students; verbal-reward enhancement on free-choice was d = 0.43 [0.27, 0.58] (k = 14) for college students versus d = 0.11 [-0.11, 0.34] (k = 7, not significant) for children. Boring-task conditions were excluded from primary analyses (13 studies). — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 (full text)](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf); abstract-level d values confirmed at [Ewha repository](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/a-meta-analytic-review-of-experiments-examining-the-effects-of-ex/); the performance-contingent self-report value is not given in the abstract
- `[META dispute · n/a]` Deci et al. reanalyze Cameron & Pierce (1994) and Eisenberger, Cameron & Pierce (1996), whose conclusion was that undermining was "largely a myth"; they state the earlier all-rewards free-choice effect was d = -0.06 (non-significant) versus their d = -0.24, attributing the gap to four methodological differences (dull-task conditions pooled with interesting-task conditions, inappropriate control groups, misclassified studies, omitted studies and dissertations). A later Cameron, Banko & Pierce (2001) reply exists but could not be retrieved. — [Deci et al. 1999 full text](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)

### Inferences
- For streaks, the retrieved evidence is company A/B retention data plus consumer-psychology experiments on subsequent engagement; no retrieved study links a streak feature to learning gains. Broken streaks, when highlighted, lowered later engagement in lab experiments (Silverman & Barasch), and the effect shrank when the streak could be repaired.
- Goal and planning interventions show a strong pooled effect on goal attainment (d = .65) and one small high-school result (60% more practice questions), but large field tests in education show early-engagement bumps that fade and null or near-null completion effects (Kizilcec 2020, Andor 2018, Oreopoulos 2019); positive MOOC results (Yeomans & Reich, +29%) coexist with null results from the same type of intervention, so context heterogeneity is large.
- The commitment device in Patterson (restricting distraction) had the largest completion effect among the MOOC tools retrieved (+40%, N = 657) while reminders and blocking had none; this is one study and a working-paper version.
- Gamification effects on cognitive outcomes survive the high-rigor subsplit (g = .42) but motivational and behavioral effects do not; school effects (g = 1.12) exceed higher-education effects (g = .41); durability is essentially untested (one delayed posttest). Experiments show points and leaderboards raise quantity but not quality of work (Mekler), and one semester-long college comparison showed lower motivation and exam scores under badges plus leaderboard (Hanus & Fox, N = 80).
- Duolingo's own reporting separates engagement metrics from learning outcomes and states the learning-efficacy research is slower than its A/B cycle (inference: its streak and retention gains are not learning evidence).
- Tangible expected rewards (d = -0.34 free choice) differ from verbal feedback (d = 0.33) and unexpected rewards (d = 0.01); effects in the meta-analysis were measured on free-choice time and self-reported interest, so application to app points or badges is by analogy (inference), and the effect is disputed (-0.06 vs -0.24 for all rewards).
- Transferability caveat: the notification and A/B evidence relies on server-side experimentation at Duolingo scale; none of it was tested in an offline, no-account context (inference).

### Gaps
- No RCT or quasi-experiment isolating streak features' effect on learning outcomes, and none in a high-school or college self-directed practice app.
- Per-arm results (including social accountability) for Kizilcec et al. 2020 were not retrieved; the exact N was not on any retrieved page (the abstract says "one-quarter million students").
- Sailer & Homner does not break results into high school versus college beyond the school/higher-education context moderator.
- Cameron, Banko & Pierce (2001) was not retrieved; the Hanus & Fox statistics were not retrieved.
- Endowed-progress, goal-gradient and fresh-start effects (Nunes & Drèze 2006; Kivetz et al. 2006; Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014) were not retrieved this session; no empirical study of streak anxiety was found (only opinion pieces surfaced in search).
- Duolingo leaderboard and Streak Freeze numbers could only be reached via secondary sources.

## 3. Workload and burnout: review backlog, daily time caps and catch-up

### Takeaway
No controlled study linking review-backlog size to quitting was found. What exists is documentation of how Anki and SuperMemo handle backlogs and small self-report surveys of medical students reporting overwhelm; habit research from health behaviors suggests a single missed day has little effect while cumulative misses do.

### Cited Findings
- `[DOC]` Anki manual, Maximum Reviews/Day: "When this limit is reached, Anki will not show any more review cards for the day, even if there are more waiting"; interday learning cards count toward the limit. New Cards/Day: if you study fewer than the limit "or miss a day, the next day the counts will be back to their original setting". — [Anki manual, deck options](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html?highlight=easy+days)
- `[DOC]` Same page, backlog handling: "it is recommended that you stop introducing new cards until you catch up with that backlog"; review order "Relative overdueness" ("Shows cards that you're more likely to have forgotten first") is recommended for a "large backlog that may take some time to get through", while "Due date, then random" (the default) is recommended "when you are up to date, or when you only have a small backlog"; with FSRS enabled, relative overdueness is replaced by ascending retrievability. — [Anki manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html?highlight=easy+days)
- `[DOC]` Same page, load: learning new cards raises review load and a user who studied "hundreds of new cards" in early days "has become overwhelmed by the reviews required"; higher FSRS desired retention "leads to shorter intervals and more reviews per day"; Easy Days adjusts a computed interval "by a small amount to change the due date" with no immediate change in due counts; users are told to spend "a few weeks with the defaults" before adjusting. Default limit values, Load Balancer, Postpone and Advance were not on the retrieved text. — [Anki manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html?highlight=easy+days)
- `[DOC]` SuperMemo Mercy: "a humorous name for the overload tool that makes it possible to spread outstanding repetitions into the future"; also usable to study early before a vacation or exam; proposed by Andrzej Horodenski in 1992 and independently by users of other spaced repetition apps such as Michael Nielsen. — [SuperMemo Guru, Mercy](https://supermemo.guru/wiki/Mercy)
- `[DOC]` SuperMemo help: Postpone "increase[s] intervals of all outstanding repetitions by a selected delay factor" (delay factor 1.1 on a 100-day interval delays it by 10 days); Auto-postpone "always leaves a number of top-priority elements in the queue"; "Mercy postpones repetitions at the cost of retention", "You cannot undo Mercy", and repeating it before a vacation is discouraged because "two repetitions of the same item executed in short succession count as little, or sometimes less, than a single repetition!"; the stated options for overload are "add less material, do more learning, or accept very long intervals on some items"; after an exam, loads can be cut "to just 10-30 item repetitions and 3-5 topic repetitions". — [SuperMemo overload help](https://www-v1.supermemo.com/archives1990-2015/help/faq/overload)
- `[DOC]` SuperMemo Mercy/Spread described for "Unload the backlog of material in a low priority branch, or in a low priority collection"; a warning on Advance: "Massed review of related items may produce false positives (i.e. good grades in items than are not remembered well)". — [SuperMemo Postpone/Advance/Mercy help](https://www-v1.supermemo.com/archives1990-2015/help/pam)
- `[SURVEY · Weak]` Medical students: 75% (63) "never or rarely" kept up with daily reviews from previous modules; 82.1% found Anki overwhelming; 68.3% felt frustrated when reviews went unfinished; 34.5% lost sleep; no discontinuation or time-per-day data. — [Nour & Harris 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12662189). In a separate survey, Anki use was associated with better sleep (P = .01) and no difference in other wellness measures — [Wothe et al. 2023](https://doaj.org/article/7aaf357db5d54ad39fea3f672ad5aae4). In a third, mean agreement was 4.06 on "Using Anki increases my stress level" and 3.64 on "decreases my stress level" on a 7-point scale (4 = neutral), neither significant — [Harris & Chiang 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9045547)
- `[OBS · Moderate-Weak]` Habit study: "a single missed day had little impact on later automaticity gains" but too many missed repeats, even spread out, cumulatively lowered the maximum automaticity reached. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit)
- `[OBS · Moderate]` MOOC survey: reasons for disengaging most prominently "personal commitments, work conflict, and course workload". — [Kizilcec et al. 2013](https://web.stanford.edu/~cpiech/bio/papers/deconstructingDisengagement.pdf)
- `[CO · Weak]` Duolingo: shorter sessions did not increase learners' motivation to do more lessons and hurt TSLW; the company's target is a higher share of learners reaching at least 15 minutes per day; the post mentions burnout and churn but gives no drop-off figures ("they can't learn if they churn"). Streak/goal decoupling raised streak holding while reducing the share reaching daily goals. — [Duolingo TSLW post](https://blog.duolingo.com/time-spent-learning-well); [Duolingo, improving the streak](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)
- `[OBS · Weak-Moderate]` Khan Academy's recommended dosage is 30+ minutes per week; "Approximately 9% (~33K)" of 350K sampled students reached it; mean use in the later PNAS-linked sample was about 10-15 minutes per week. — [Khan Academy Nov 2024](https://blog.khanacademy.org/?p=18194); [Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/national-study-in-top-journal-finds-khan-academy-learning-gains-after-accounting-for-key-unmeasured-factors/)
- `[RCT · Moderate]` College students "study about five to eight hours a week less than they planned", unchanged by text-message or coaching interventions. — [Oreopoulos & Petronijevic](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26059)
- `[RCT (A/B) · Moderate]` Scheduling choice changed practice volume: HLR versus Leitner scheduling lowered the practice-activity retention measure by 7.3% (p < 0.001) while any-activity and new-lesson retention changed by +0.3% each. — [Settles & Meeder 2016](https://aclanthology.org/P16-1174.pdf)

### Inferences
- Every catch-up mechanism retrieved (Anki order and limits, SuperMemo Postpone/Mercy/Spread) is documentation; none was evaluated for adherence. SuperMemo itself states Mercy trades retention for relief and that stacked postponement lengthens intervals.
- Links between backlog and quitting rest on self-reported overwhelm (82.1%) and not keeping up (75%) in medical students; none is longitudinal, and neither survey measured discontinuation.
- Habit data from health behaviors (not learning) suggests isolated lapses are tolerable while cumulative misses matter; no learning-specific evidence was found.
- There is no retrieved evidence on daily time caps' effect on persistence; the only quantitative workload anchors are recommended dosages (Khan 30 minutes per week, Duolingo's 15 minutes per day target) and the observed shortfall against them.

### Gaps
- No experimental or longitudinal evidence of backlog size, missed days or catch-up policy on quitting for any spaced-repetition app.
- No evidence on daily time or review caps and persistence.
- Anki default limits, Load Balancer, Postpone/Advance and FSRS long-gap handling were not confirmed from the retrieved docs; Anki-specific surveys did not report time per day or reasons for stopping.
- No Anki-specific burnout instrument beyond a "burnout risk" item in one survey (no difference reported).

## 4. Difficulty and persistence: does hard, unassisted practice reduce persistence, and what mitigates it

### Takeaway
No retrieved study directly tests hard, unassisted practice versus easier or assisted practice on multi-week persistence. Adjacent evidence: learners' "appropriate difficulty" ratings peaked near 65% real success in one adaptive practice app while answer volume did not vary significantly by target; unproductive persistence is common in mastery practice (9.1% to 46.9% of student-skill pairs depending on definition); growth-mindset messaging shows small, heterogeneous and contested average effects; performance predicts later self-efficacy more strongly than self-efficacy predicts performance.

### Cited Findings

#### Difficulty and willingness to keep practicing
- `[RCT (A/B) · Moderate-Weak]` Geography fact-practice app slepemapy.cz (about 6 million answers overall; Experiment 2 more than 1,700,000 answers; learners with fewer than 10 answers excluded): target success rates ranged over [50%, 95%], default 75%. No statistically significant trend in total answers across target probabilities (real success 65% to 90%); perceived-difficulty ratings followed a U-shape with "Appropriate" peaking at 65% real success; out-of-school users peaked near 65% while in-school students preferred easier questions (about 75%); maximum satisfaction at a 60-65% target. Dynamic difficulty adjustment (Experiment 3, 176,000 answers): median answers 28 versus 21 (p = 0.02) and "Appropriate" votes 68% versus 63%, with average success about 75% in both. Authors: about 65% is a good target by self-reported difficulty and answer count may be an insufficient proxy for motivation. Outcome was answers per learner, not return rate or dropout. — [Papoušek & Pelánek, AIED 2015](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/aied15.pdf)
- `[theory/simulation · n/a for human persistence]` The optimal error rate for training is around 15.87% (optimal accuracy about 85%), derived for stochastic-gradient-descent learners on binary classification and shown for artificial neural networks and biologically plausible networks; the abstract does not describe tests with human learners. — [Wilson et al. 2019, Nat Commun](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/natcom/v10y2019i1d10.1038_s41467-019-12552-4.html)
- `[OBS · Moderate]` ASSISTments Skill Builders: 9.1% of 75,481 student-problem set pairs wheel-spun (authors' definition) versus 46.9% (Beck & Gong's definition); predictors included not requesting hints in at least one problem; quitting before the 10th problem was excluded from the wheel-spinning model (42.7% of non-persistent pairs quit without mastery); all findings correlational. — [Kai et al., JEDM](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/ShiminJEDM.pdf)
- `[CO · Weak]` Duolingo: "Harder lessons take longer, so fewer completed sessions doesn't necessarily mean less learning"; adding content to the path "always had a positive impact on TSLW"; shorter lessons did not raise motivation to do more lessons. — [Duolingo TSLW post](https://blog.duolingo.com/time-spent-learning-well)
- `[OBS · Moderate]` MOOC survey: course workload among the most prominent reasons for disengaging (see Section 1). — [Kizilcec et al. 2013](https://web.stanford.edu/~cpiech/bio/papers/deconstructingDisengagement.pdf)

#### Self-efficacy and mastery experience
- `[META · Moderate]` Cross-lagged meta-analysis (k = 11, N = 2688, two-wave longitudinal studies): self-efficacy to later performance beta = 0.071 (p < 0.001); performance to later self-efficacy beta = 0.205 (p < 0.001), a significantly larger effect; reciprocity held for adults but not children, where performance predicted later self-efficacy but self-efficacy did not predict later performance; stronger effects in studies following self-efficacy theorists' recommendations. — [Talsma et al. 2018](https://figshare.utas.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/I_believe_therefore_I_achieve_and_vice_versa_a_meta-analytic_cross-lagged_panel_analysis_of_self-efficacy_and_academic_performance/22962173)

#### Growth-mindset messaging: effect sizes and replication status
- `[RCT (preregistered, national) · Moderate-Strong]` Short (less than one hour) online growth-mindset module in a nationally representative US secondary sample: grades improved among lower-achieving students and enrollment in advanced math rose; effects depended on school context ("changed grades where peer norms matched its messages"); the authors cite independent data collection, preregistered analyses and a blinded Bayesian reanalysis. — [Yeager et al. 2019, Nature (abstract)](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/national-experiment-reveals-where-growth-mindset-improves-achievement)
- `[SEC · Weak (numbers)]` EdWeek on the same study: "nearly 12,500 9th graders" from 76 public high schools; ".10 of a point improvement on a four-point GPA scale" for low-performing students; critic Macnamara: a 2.0 to 2.1 change "not really changing a whole lot"; another expert called it "pretty meaningful" for a scalable low-cost intervention; Yeager said lengthening the intervention "won't add much value". — [Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/national-study-bolsters-case-for-teaching-growth-mindset/2019/08)
- `[META · Moderate]` Two meta-analyses: mindset-achievement correlation (abstract k = 273, N = 365,915; results text lists 129 studies and 273 effect sizes) r = .10, 95% CI [.08, .13]; low-SES r = .17 [.10, .23]; high-risk r = .08 [-.04, .21]. Interventions (abstract k = 43, N = 57,155; results text lists 29 studies and 43 effect sizes): d = 0.08 [0.02, 0.14]; low-SES d = 0.34 [0.07, 0.62] (moderator significant, Q(1) = 4.76, p = .029); high-risk d = 0.17 [-0.11, 0.45] (moderator not significant); using at-risk subsamples d = 0.19 [0.02, 0.36] (8 effect sizes). "Overall effects were weak for both meta-analyses." — [Sisk et al. 2018, Psychological Science](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/sisk-et-al-2018-to-what-extent-and-under-which-circumstances-are-growth-mind-sets-important-to-academic-achievement-two.pdf)
- `[META · Moderate]` 53 independent samples: academic achievement d = 0.14, 95% CI [.06, .22] in meta-regression with targeted subsamples and high fidelity; mental health d = 0.32 [.10, .54]; social functioning d = 0.36 [.03, .68]; 95% prediction intervals for focal effects ranged from -0.08 to 0.35 for academic achievement. — [Burnette et al. 2023, Psychol Bull (abstract)](https://ifp.nyu.edu/?p=1678668)
- `[META · Moderate]` Macnamara & Burgoyne meta-analysis as restated in their reply (the original abstract was not retrievable): 63 studies; overall d = 0.05, 95% CI [0.02, 0.09], p = .004; studies showing a mindset change d = 0.04 [-0.01, 0.10]; best-available-evidence model (mindset change shown and at least 60% of best-practice criteria) d = 0.02 [-0.06, 0.10], p = .666; "higher quality studies were less likely to demonstrate a benefit"; "94%" of samples included confounds; 3% of samples preregistered; "authors with a known financial incentive were two and a half times as likely to report positive effects"; evidence of publication bias; the authors dispute a reanalysis by Tipton et al. that found a significant at-risk effect. — [Macnamara & Burgoyne reply, Psychol Bull 149(3-4)](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/2023-90931-004.pdf); contradicted in magnitude by Burnette et al. above
- `[RCT · Moderate-Strong]` England "Changing Mindsets" trial: 101 schools, 5,018 pupils (Year 6), eight weeks of sessions, trial with a control group of non-participating schools (design label not stated on the retrieved page); "no evidence that the pupils made additional progress in literacy or numeracy" on national Key Stage 2 tests. — [Education Endowment Foundation](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/eef-publishes-findings-from-growth-mindsets-approach)

#### Visible progress (see also Section 2)
- `[META · Strong (general goals)]` Progress-monitoring interventions d+ = 0.40 on goal attainment, larger when recorded physically or made public. — [Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/)
- `[META · Moderate]` Learning analytics dashboards: negligible or small effects on achievement, "relatively substantial" on participation (38 studies). — [Kaliisa et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15042)

### Inferences
- Direct evidence for the question is absent: no study contrasts deliberately hard, unassisted practice with easier or assisted practice on return rate or multi-week persistence in high-school or college self-directed use.
- In the one adaptive-practice experiment retrieved, answers per learner were flat across target success rates of about 65% to 90% real success, while perceived appropriateness peaked near 65%; whether this generalizes to hard unassisted problem solving is unknown (inference).
- Wheel-spinning prevalence ranges from 9.1% to 46.9% depending on definition, and not requesting hints is associated with wheel-spinning; this is correlational and cannot show that removing hints causes unproductive persistence, but it shows unproductive persistence occurs in mastery practice (inference).
- The 85% rule's 15.87% error rate comes from machine-learning theory and has not been shown as a human persistence optimum in the retrieved sources (inference).
- Growth-mindset average effects range from d = 0.02 (best-evidence subset) to 0.14 (targeted high-fidelity subsamples) across meta-analytic estimates, with an English school trial null and a US preregistered trial positive for lower achievers where peer norms supported effort; the replication status is mixed and moderated, and effects reported by authors with financial interests were more often positive (Macnamara).
- Cross-lagged data suggest success experiences shape self-efficacy more than self-efficacy shapes later performance (adults; children show only performance-to-self-efficacy), which bears on how repeated failure may affect later confidence (inference).

### Gaps
- No retrieved study of "unassisted" or hint-free practice and persistence; no study of deliberately hard practice in apps.
- Deslauriers et al. 2019 (PNAS, feeling of learning versus actual learning; PubMed page blocked), Kirk-Johnson et al. 2019 (misinterpreted-effort hypothesis), Multon, Brown & Lent 1991 (self-efficacy and persistence meta-analysis) and Locke & Latham goal-setting work were not retrieved.
- The original Macnamara & Burgoyne abstract and total N, the Tipton et al. reanalysis and Yeager et al. 2019's full statistics (N, confidence interval) were not retrievable; GPA figures come from EdWeek.
- Whether growth-mindset messaging inside a practice app (as opposed to a one-off module) affects persistence was not found.

### Inferences
- Direct high-school evidence in this set comes from two studies (Duckworth N = 66; the US mindset trial) and college evidence from small or null studies (Hanus & Fox N = 80; ASEE N = 152; Oreopoulos about 25,000 null); most large-N adherence evidence is MOOC or consumer-app based and involves adults of mixed educational background.
- Effect sizes in the strongest syntheses are small to medium (gamification g = .25 to .49, mindset d = 0.02 to 0.14, progress monitoring d+ = 0.40, implementation intentions d = .65), and in several cases the headline estimate drops when restricted to higher-rigor subsets (gamification motivational and behavioral; mindset best-evidence).
- Company-reported effects are mostly relative percentage changes in retention or DAU (+0.5% to +14%) with undisclosed samples, which are not directly comparable with standardized effects.

### Gaps
- No high-school or college randomized test of adherence features (streaks, reminders, goals, caps) inside a self-directed spaced-repetition or problem-practice app was found.
- Per-arm sample sizes and standardized effects for Duolingo's streak, Streak Wager and Weekend Amulet experiments are not published in the retrieved sources.
- Population breakdowns (high school versus college) were not available in Sailer & Homner beyond the context moderator, nor in Zeng et al. beyond the abstract.
