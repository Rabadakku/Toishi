## Q1. Does retrieval practice of facts or definitions improve later application or higher-order problem solving, or only fact recall?

### Takeaway
In the studies that tested this directly, retrieval practice of facts or definitions strongly improved recall of those same facts (d of about 1.2) but produced little or no gain on higher-order, application or reversed-direction questions. None of those studies used math. The meta-analytic transfer effect (d = 0.40) is real but is mostly near transfer (inference questions on the same text, changes of test format). It depends on moderators and is weakest for worked-example problem solving. No study was found that tests whether recalling math formulas or definitions improves later math problem solving.

### Cited Findings
- **Agarwal (2019), J. Educational Psychology, Exp. 1. [RCT, lab, within-subjects · FULL]**
  - Sample and materials: 48 college students; eight passages of about 1,000 words on controversial topics.
  - Conditions: study once, study twice, fact quiz, or higher-order quiz (apply/analyze/evaluate/create). One multiple-choice (MC) quiz per passage, with immediate feedback. Final MC test after 48 h, with rephrased stems.
  - Final fact test: fact quiz .78 vs study once .54 (d = 1.23); higher-order quiz .53.
  - Final higher-order test: higher-order quiz .72 vs study once .44 (d = 1.39). Fact quiz .46, not reliably above study once (.44) or study twice (.49). Interaction ηp² = .37.
  - [Agarwal 2019 PDF](http://pdf.poojaagarwal.com/Agarwal_2019_JEdPsych.pdf)
- **Agarwal (2019), Exp. 2. [RCT, lab, within-subjects · FULL]**
  - 48 college students, same materials. Conditions: higher-order quiz 1X, higher-order quizzes 2X, fact quizzes 2X, mixed 2X.
  - Higher-order test: higher-order 2X .85, higher-order 1X .77, mixed .71, fact 2X .48 (fact 2X vs higher-order 2X d = 1.24).
  - Fact test: fact 2X .90, mixed .78, higher-order 2X .53, higher-order 1X .54.
  - Author's conclusion: fact-based retrieval practice did not improve higher-order learning, "contrary to popular intuition" based on Bloom's taxonomy. Matching quiz type to test type gave the largest gains (transfer-appropriate processing).
  - [Agarwal 2019 PDF](http://pdf.poojaagarwal.com/Agarwal_2019_JEdPsych.pdf)
- **Agarwal (2019), Exp. 3. [classroom experiment, within-subjects across chapters, class sections not randomly assigned · FULL]**
  - 6th-grade social studies; 142 enrolled, 88 analyzed.
  - Three clicker quizzes per chapter, with feedback. Final test 48 h after the review quiz, using items identical to the quiz items.
  - Higher-order quizzes did not raise fact-test scores (.64 vs .64 for nonquizzed material) but did raise higher-order scores (.75 vs .56, d = 0.99).
  - [Agarwal 2019 PDF](http://pdf.poojaagarwal.com/Agarwal_2019_JEdPsych.pdf)
- **Agarwal (2019), stated limitations. [FULL]**
  - MC formats only; the author notes transfer may depend on question format.
  - Low reliability on some final tests (Cronbach's α = .254 for the Exp. 2 higher-order test).
  - Exp. 3: nonrandom class sections, no restudy or fact-only condition, identical final items, and an incentive (pizza party) that may have affected motivation.
  - No explicit transfer instructions; the material was not math.
  - [Agarwal 2019 PDF](http://pdf.poojaagarwal.com/Agarwal_2019_JEdPsych.pdf)
- **McDaniel, Thomas, Agarwal, McDermott & Roediger (2013), Applied Cognitive Psychology 27(3), 360-372. [classroom experiment · ABS]**
  - Middle-school science; three spaced MC quizzes per unit.
  - Exp. 1: quizzing produced large benefits on classroom exams, with similar effect sizes for identical and transfer items.
  - Exp. 2: quizzes targeted either definitions or applying a principle. Application quizzes improved exam performance on definition-type questions and on different application questions. Definition quizzes did not improve application questions (as summarized from the abstract).
  - N is not shown on the record page.
  - [WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/quizzing-in-middle-school-science-successful-transfer-performance/)
- **Jensen, McDaniel, Woodard & Kummer (2014), Educational Psychology Review. [QUASI, two course sections · PART]**
  - Introductory biology; both sections were taught in the same inquiry-based style.
  - One section took low-level (memory) quizzes and exams all semester; the other took high-level ones (application, analysis, evaluation). Both took a common final with 20 low-level and 21 high-level items.
  - The high-level section outperformed on both the low-level and the high-level items. Reported ηp² values were .072, .044 and .068; the extracted text does not map them clearly to item types.
  - F(1, 167) implies roughly 169 students (inferred, not stated). Randomization and group equivalence were not established in the extracted text.
  - Authors: high-level exams encouraged deeper processing and better retention of core information.
  - [Springer DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-013-9248-9)
- **Pan & Rickard (2017), JEP: Applied, term-definition facts. [RCT, lab · FULL]**
  - UCSD undergraduates; analyzed N = 54, 59, 49/52 and 113 in Exps. 1, 2, 3a/3b and 4.
  - Materials: 36 neuroscience/biopsychology term-definition facts, about 17 words per definition. MC practice with correct-answer feedback; final test after 48 h.
  - Against a restudy control, practicing the term produced testing effects (Exp. 2 d = 0.68; Exp. 4 d = 0.97). Practicing the definition helped only after three practice trials per fact (d = 0.37).
  - Transfer to the reversed direction (practice the term, then be tested on the definition, or the reverse) was absent or minimal, e.g. d = 0.21, p = .25.
  - Authors' reading: earlier term-definition "transfer" likely reflected no-training controls or elaborated practice. No application questions were tested.
  - [Pan & Rickard 2017 PDF](https://rickardlab.ucsd.edu/pdf/PR_JEPA_2017.pdf)
- **Pan & Rickard (2018), Psychological Bulletin. [META · PART: abstract and methods read; per-category and moderator d values sit in tables that could not be extracted]**
  - 192 transfer effect sizes from 122 experiments in 67 articles (N = 10,382). Overall transfer d = 0.40, 95% CI [0.31, 0.50].
  - Transfer was greatest across test formats, to application and inference questions, and to medical-diagnosis problems.
  - Transfer was weakest to rearranged stimulus-response items, to untested material seen during study, and to problems involving worked examples.
  - Response congruency, elaborated retrieval practice and initial test performance strongly moderated transfer. After publication-bias adjustment, the intercepts were substantially reduced, often indicating no positive transfer when none of those moderators was present.
  - Nearly all samples were young adults: only 3 studies used elementary children and 2 used high-school students.
  - [Pan & Rickard 2018 PDF](https://rickardlab.ucsd.edu/pdf/PR_2018.pdf)
- **Pan & Rickard (2018), secondary analyses. [META · SUM, via Pan's 2018 dissertation]** Weighted mean effect sizes in secondary meta-analyses limited to specific transfer types "ranged from d = 0.16 to 0.61." — [Pan dissertation, eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt45x0w7t8/qt45x0w7t8.pdf)
- **Pan & Rickard (2018), response congruency. [SECONDHAND, earlier notes]** d = 0.58 for congruent vs 0.28 for incongruent responses. — [Matuschak notes, as cited in learning_science.md](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zC1oBp6yE72b7YHzaJZmjXf)
- **Butler (2010), JEP: Learning, Memory, and Cognition 36(5), 1118-1133. [RCT, lab · ABS]**
  - Four experiments with prose passages covering facts and concepts; repeated testing vs repeated studying.
  - Final test one week later: the same questions (Exp. 1a), new inferential questions from the same domain (Exps. 1b and 2), or new inferential questions from a different knowledge domain (Exp. 3).
  - "Repeated testing produced superior retention and transfer on the final test relative to repeated studying." Effect sizes are not on the record page.
  - [WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/repeated-testing-produces-superior-transfer-of-learning-relative-/)
- **Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu & Shanks (2021), Psychological Bulletin, classroom meta-analysis. [META · SECONDHAND, earlier notes]**
  - By final-exam content: fact g = 0.524, concept g = 0.644, problem solving g = 0.453 (k = 59). The moderator was not significant (p = .087).
  - Untested material from the same lessons g = 0.321; engineering classrooms g = 0.584 (k = 11).
  - [Yang et al. 2021 PDF, as cited in learning_science.md](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- **Murray, Horner & Goebel (2025), Educational Psychology Review, math-specific meta-analysis. [META · SECONDHAND, earlier notes]**
  - Testing vs restudy in mathematics: 7 studies, 32 effects, g = 0.18 with a CI crossing zero ("inconclusive").
  - Spaced vs massed practice in mathematics: g = 0.28 (27 studies).
  - [White Rose record, as cited in science_of_practice.md](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/229807)
- **Yeo & Fazio (Journal of Educational Psychology; the authors' 2018 post cites it as 2018). [RCT, lab · SUM, authors' own blog post]**
  - College students learning Poisson-probability problems. Design: learning goal (remember a worked passage vs learn the procedure) crossed with strategy (retrieval/repeated problem solving vs restudying worked examples). Tests were immediate or after one week; the procedure group solved 8 novel problems.
  - Immediately: studying worked examples beat solving problems.
  - After one week: problem solving equaled worked-example study for non-identical problems and beat it for identical problems.
  - The authors suggest retrieval mainly strengthens memory for steps and facts, while varied worked examples build the underlying principles. N and numbers appear only in figures.
  - [Learning Scientists guest post](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2018/4/11-1)
- **Math retrieval-as-problem-solving, precalculus. [classroom experiment · FULL]** Spacing the quiz problems for precalculus skills raised retention one month after the final (.59 vs .54). Details are under Q5. — [Hopkins et al. 2018, ASEE](https://peer.asee.org/29900.pdf)

### Inferences
- Four direct tests (Agarwal Exps. 1-2, McDaniel 2013 Exp. 2, Pan & Rickard 2017) agree: practicing facts or definitions helped the practiced response but not application, higher-order or reversed-direction questions. All four used prose or verbal materials, not math.
- The reverse direction conflicts across studies. Application or higher-order practice improved fact or definition items in McDaniel 2013 and Jensen 2014, but not in any of Agarwal's three experiments.
  - Possible reasons: Agarwal used MC-only, 48 h tests built from "Taking Sides" passages, while the others used course exams over a unit.
  - Jensen's comparison is between nonrandom sections. No study resolves the conflict.
- Pan & Rickard list "application and inference questions" among the strongest transfer categories, yet Agarwal found zero transfer from fact quizzes to higher-order items. The conflict is apparent: the meta-analytic category pools practice with many question types and feedback styles, and their moderators (response congruency, elaborated practice) are a plausible explanation.
- The meta-analytic transfer results (Pan & Rickard d = 0.40; Butler 2010; Yang problem-solving g = 0.453) mostly concern questions about the same content as the practice. They do not test a recalled fact used as one step inside a multi-step problem, which is how formula recall enters related-rates or optimization problems. That kind of transfer is untested in the literature found.
- For math, the testing-vs-restudy evidence is thin and statistically inconclusive (g = 0.18). Pan & Rickard flag worked-example problems as a weak-transfer category, and Yeo & Fazio found retrieval helps procedures mainly after a delay and mainly for identical problems. Psychology-course effect sizes therefore may not carry over to math problem solving.

### Gaps
- Carpenter (2012, Current Directions, "Testing enhances the transfer of learning") was not retrieved.
- No experiment was found that tests whether retrieval practice of geometry or precalculus formulas and definitions improves calculus problem solving (related rates, optimization), either alone or added to problem practice.
- Not retrieved: N and effect sizes for McDaniel et al. 2013; McDaniel et al. 2011 (J. Educ. Psych.); Avvisati & Borgonovi (2020) PISA test-practice field experiment; van Gog et al. (2015) and Leahy, Hanham & Sweller (2015) on testing with complex problems.
- Pan & Rickard (2018) per-category d values (application/inference, problem solving) and the moderator table could not be extracted from the PDF text.
- Butler (2010) population, N and effect sizes are not on the record page.
- The last ~19k characters of the Agarwal 2019 PDF (end of the discussion, references) were not read.

## Q2. Successive relearning of key-term definitions: criterion levels, relearning sessions, long-delay retention, and course-exam effects

### Takeaway
Successive relearning means retrieving each definition to a criterion (typically 3 correct recalls) and then relearning it to 1 correct recall in each of several spaced sessions. For durable recall of definitions in college courses it is one of the most consistently potent techniques.
- Classroom results: about a letter grade on unit exams, and a larger advantage roughly a month later.
- About three relearning sessions may be enough, with diminishing returns after that. The effect of the initial criterion largely disappears once relearning sessions are added.
- Nearly all evidence is for verbal definitions in psychology-type courses. Effects on new or transfer items are much smaller, and Janes et al. 2018 could not be found.

### Cited Findings
- **Rawson & Dunlosky (2022), Current Directions 31(4), 362-368, as summarized by May & Scullin, APS Observer, Aug. 23, 2022. [REVIEW · SUM]**
  - Procedure: recall each definition correctly three times when cued with the term, then recall it correctly once in each of at least three spaced relearning sessions.
  - Three sessions "may suffice" for maximal benefit, depending on when the test occurs (citing Rawson & Dunlosky 2011). Recall time decreases across relearning sessions.
  - The evidence base is "more than a dozen studies": foreign-language vocabulary, psychology and statistics terms, and probability concepts, mostly with undergraduates.
  - Stated limits: the number of sessions needed is not established, may depend on the retention interval, and other age groups need more work.
  - [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/teaching-current-directions-studying-psychopathology)
- **Rawson, Dunlosky & Sciartelli (2013), Educational Psychology Review, introductory psychology course. [classroom experiment · SUM, two summaries]**
  - Material learned by successive relearning scored 10% higher on the unit test ("a full letter grade") than self-studied material, and "40% higher" nearly a month later (APS summary).
  - Agarwal's summary: two experiments with three conditions, successive relearning (retrieve each item correctly three times; sessions e.g. 1, 3, 8, 10 and 15 days after introduction), spaced restudy, and business as usual. Successive relearning raised exam performance by about a letter grade (C to B) over both comparisons. The gain held for higher- and lower-performing students and was larger with three spaced sessions than with one.
  - [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/teaching-current-directions-studying-psychopathology); [RetrievalPractice.org, Agarwal, Jan. 18, 2018](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/archive/2018/successive-relearning)
- **Rawson & Dunlosky (2011), JEP: General. [RCT, lab · SECONDHAND, earlier notes (abstract)]**
  - Three experiments, 533 students, conceptual material.
  - Initial criterion of 1-4 correct recalls, then relearning to 1 correct recall in 1-5 later sessions; retention tested after 1-4 months.
  - Criterion and relearning were "subadditive": the initial criterion mattered strongly before relearning and diminished as relearning increased.
  - Prescription: an initial criterion of 3, then 3 widely spaced relearning sessions.
  - [DOI 10.1037/a0023956, as cited in science_of_practice.md](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023956)
- **Rawson & Dunlosky (2013), "Relearning attenuates the benefits and costs of spacing," JEP: General. [RCT, lab · SECONDHAND, earlier notes (abstract)]**
  - Three experiments, 567 students, key-term definitions.
  - The benefit of spacing initial practice was substantial before relearning and significantly attenuated after it. Spacing's time costs were also attenuated, because relearning was faster.
  - [DOI 10.1037/a0030498, as cited in curves_time_and_forgetting.md](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030498)
- **Vaughn, Dunlosky & Rawson (2016), Memory & Cognition. [RCT, lab · SECONDHAND, earlier notes (abstract)]** With Swahili-English pairs and an initial criterion of 1-7 recalls, the benefit of a higher criterion did not persist across weekly relearning sessions, including at a one-month test. — [DOI 10.3758/s13421-016-0606-y, as cited in curves_time_and_forgetting.md](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-016-0606-y)
- **Rawson, Vaughn, Walsh & Dunlosky (2018), JEP: Applied. [RCT, lab · SECONDHAND]**
  - One correct recall in each of three spaced sessions produced more than twice the recall of three correct recalls in a single session (APS summary).
  - The abstract reports relearning effects of ds = 1.52 to 4.19 (earlier notes).
  - [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/teaching-current-directions-studying-psychopathology); [DOI 10.1037/xap0000146, as cited in curves_time_and_forgetting.md](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000146)
- **Higham, Zengel, Bartlett & Hadwin (2022), J. Educational Psychology. [classroom within-subjects experiment · SECONDHAND, earlier notes (preprint PDF)]**
  - 212 UK introductory-psychology students. Weekly sets of 20 items, practiced in 3 sessions two days apart, either relearned to a criterion of 1 or restudied.
  - Repeated items at days 42 and 68: relearn .55 vs restudy .41 (d = 0.705). Transfer and new items showed only marginal differences.
  - [Higham et al. preprint, as cited in science_of_practice.md](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/449677/1/Higham_et_al._in_press_JEdPsych.pdf)
- **How "correct" was decided in the Rawson/Dunlosky method. [project description · ABS]**
  - In the IES-funded Retrieval-Monitoring-Feedback (RMF) method, students recall a concept and compare their answer with the correct answer broken into idea units. They judge which units they included and restudy the full answer.
  - Those self-judgments decide whether and when an item is practiced again.
  - Samples: about 400 undergraduates and about 100 middle-school students; outcomes were cued recall after 1 and 3 months and savings in relearning.
  - [IES award R305A080316](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/developing-retrieval-monitoring-feedback-rmf-method-improving-durability-and-efficiency-student)
- **Scope claimed by the authors. [REVIEW · ABS]** Rawson & Dunlosky's 2014 chapter "Bang for the Buck" frames successive relearning as a strategy for foundational STEM material "such as facts, terminology, formulas, and concepts." — [WUSTL Open Scholarship record](https://openscholarship.wustl.edu/circle_book/8)

### Inferences
- The letter-grade course-exam gains come from psychology courses whose exam items closely matched the practiced definitions. Higham et al. found transfer and new-item benefits only marginal, so the large figures describe retention of the practiced content, not broader application.
- In the RMF line of work, the criterion of "1 correct recall" was applied by the students' own idea-unit judgments. The effective criterion therefore depends on how accurately students self-score (Q3).
- The authors extend the scope to formulas, but no study found here tested successive relearning on formulas, theorem statements, or math problem-solving outcomes.
- The APS figure of "40% higher" is ambiguous: it could be a relative difference or percentage points.

### Gaps
- Janes, Dunlosky, Rawson & Jasnow (2018), the biopsychology high-stakes exam study, did not appear in any source searched or fetched.

  - The Rawson & Dunlosky 2022 full text on SAGE failed with a client error.

- Rawson, Dunlosky & Janes (2020, "All good things must come to an end") was not retrieved.
- No successive-relearning study on math formulas, or with application or problem-solving outcomes in math, was found.

## Q3. Self-scoring accuracy for definition recall: do students over-credit their own answers, and what makes self-scoring accurate?

### Takeaway
Students who judge their own recalled definitions tend to be overconfident, and low performers are the worst calibrated.
- Comparing against a standard helps, and an idea-unit checklist helps more than the full definition: overconfidence bias fell from 0.14 to 0.06 in one study of 127 students. No support removed overconfidence entirely.
- Students' own decisions to stop practicing an item after a single correct recall lowered later recall.
- The primary over-crediting papers could not be read in this pass: Dunlosky & Rawson 2012, Lipko et al. 2009, and the delayed-judgment meta-analysis were blocked, and Rawson & Dunlosky 2007 was not retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- **Nederhand, Tabbers, Abrahimi & Rikers (2018), J. Cognitive Psychology. [RCT, lab, between-subjects on standard type · FULL]**
  - 127 first-year psychology students recalled keyword definitions from six texts. They estimated their score before and after seeing a standard: idea units (N = 63) or the full definition (N = 64).
  - With standards present, absolute calibration error was 0.20 with idea units vs 0.24 with full definitions (β = -0.23, p = .004). Bias (overconfidence) was 0.06 vs 0.14 (p < .001).
  - High performers were better calibrated (0.16) than average (0.23) and low performers (0.26).
  - On later texts without standards, calibration improved slightly (β = -0.08, p = .039), and more for the idea-unit group (interaction β = -0.19, p = .032).
  - This replicates Dunlosky et al. (2011): idea-unit standards beat full-definition standards.
  - [EUR repository PDF](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/109964/REPUB_109964-OA.pdf)
- **Earlier studies as cited by Nederhand et al. [SECONDHAND · no numbers given]**
  - Rawson & Dunlosky (2007): students who compared their recall with a standard were better calibrated than students who judged without one.
  - Dunlosky et al. (2011): idea-unit standards gave better calibration than full-definition standards.
  - Lipko et al. (2009): cited for students without standards showing no calibration improvement.
  - Dunlosky & Rawson (2012): cited for miscalibration being most prominent among low performers and affecting study decisions; overconfident learners may under-study.
  - [EUR repository PDF](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/109964/REPUB_109964-OA.pdf)
- **The RMF self-scoring procedure. [project description · ABS]** Students check their recall against the correct answer broken into idea units and mark which units they included; these judgments drive rescheduling. The project's listed outputs include Dunlosky & Rawson (2012, Learning and Instruction 22(4)) and Lipko et al. (2009, JEP: Applied 15(4)). — [IES award page](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/developing-retrieval-monitoring-feedback-rmf-method-improving-durability-and-efficiency-student)
- **Kornell & Bjork (2008), Memory, self-regulated dropping of flashcards. [RCT, lab · FULL]**
  - Four experiments (N = 60 Columbia students; 112, 25 with 22 analyzed, and 57 UCLA undergraduates) with Swahili-English pairs.
  - Letting students drop cards produced "a small but consistent disadvantage." In Exp. 4, students dropped 68% of items after one correct response and 8% before any correct response.
  - Exp. 3: final accuracy for items dropped after 0, 1 or 2 correct responses was .19, .62 and .88.
  - Exp. 4: automatic dropping after one correct recall gave .21 vs .44 without dropping; after two, .50 vs .56.
  - Judgments of learning for dropped items averaged 51% vs 56% actual recall (n.s.). The authors therefore attribute the cost to drop decisions, a belief that one recall means an item is learned, rather than to faulty monitoring.
  - [Kornell & Bjork 2008 PDF](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Kornell_Bjork_2008_Memory.pdf)
- **Rawson, Dunlosky & Sciartelli (2013), self-directed condition. [SECONDHAND · SUM]** In Agarwal's summary, when students chose when to drop cards from the deck, retrieval showed no benefit on exam performance. — [RetrievalPractice.org](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/archive/2018/successive-relearning)
- **Kent State metacomprehension program, news report. [SECONDHAND · news]**
  - "Students are extremely over confident about what they're learning," and "without checking, people often believe they've remembered something correctly when in fact they haven't."
  - The advice given was to self-test after a short delay and check against the source; this was advice, not a tested condition.
  - [ScienceDaily, Nov. 2007](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/11/071126162526.htm)

### Inferences
- The improvements from standards and idea units were measured on prose definitions, where partial credit is common. A formula or theorem statement has fewer, more discrete parts, so a full-answer comparison may already work like an idea-unit check. This is untested.
- Two separate risks appear in this evidence: crediting incomplete answers (miscalibration that standards reduce but do not remove), and stopping after one success (Kornell & Bjork; the self-directed condition of Rawson et al. 2013).
- Several secondary sources cite Dunlosky & Rawson (2012) for the chain from overconfidence to underachievement, but its numbers (for example, how often incorrect answers were judged correct, or retention effects) were not verified here.

## Q4. Free recall vs cued recall vs recognition (multiple choice) for definitions and formulas: which formats produce better retention and transfer?

### Takeaway
When the correct answer is shown after each attempt, short-answer (recall) and MC practice produced similar retention and similar benefits on inference questions: between-format ds were 0.09 or less in Smith & Karpicke 2014.
- Short-answer pulled ahead mainly when feedback was given (Kang et al. 2007). Without feedback, MC matched or beat recall, because recall attempts failed more often.
- Lab meta-analytic data favor production over recognition (cued recall g = 0.61 vs recognition g = 0.29, secondhand). Matching practice format to test format adds a modest benefit.
- MC with plausible alternatives can also strengthen related, untested facts.
- No format comparison for math formulas was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Smith & Karpicke (2014), Memory 22(7), 784-802. [RCT, lab · FULL]**
  - Four experiments with undergraduates (N = 80, 100, 48, 144) and prose science and history texts. Conditions: short-answer, MC, hybrid MC plus short-answer (massed or spaced), and no retrieval. Feedback was given in Exps. 1, 2 and 4; Exp. 3 used restudy instead. Final tests after one week.
  - Effect sizes vs no retrieval, overall: MC d = 1.24, short-answer 1.31, hybrid-massed 1.22, hybrid-spaced 1.27. Between-format ds ranged from 0.02 to 0.09.
  - Exps. 1-2 combined (N = 144): verbatim/inference scores were MC .50/.47, short-answer .49/.44, no retrieval .23/.23. All formats improved inference questions about equally.
  - Exp. 4 final short-answer test: hybrid .63, short-answer .59, MC .50, none .41. When initial success rates were closer (MC .83 vs short-answer .72), the hybrid beat MC.
  - Exp. 3 (no feedback): MC lures intruded on 22% of final answers vs 8% for short-answer. The authors conclude that feedback with the correct answer is needed to avoid this negative suggestion effect.
  - Short-answer took longer per correct response (14.3 vs 11.3 s; 14.9 vs 12.3 s; 13.5 vs 8.8 s).
  - [Smith & Karpicke 2014 PDF](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2014/2014_Smith_Karpicke_Memory.pdf)
- **Kang, McDermott & Roediger (2007), European J. Cognitive Psychology. [RCT, lab, within-subjects · FULL]**
  - 48 undergraduates per experiment, with articles from Current Directions in Psychological Science. Initial test: MC (4 options), short-answer, reading the facts as statements, or a filler task. Final MC and short-answer tests after 3 days.
  - Initial accuracy: MC .86 vs short-answer .54 (Exp. 1); .85 vs .56 (Exp. 2).
  - Without feedback (Exp. 1): initial MC beat initial short-answer on the final MC test (d = .36) and the final short-answer test (d = .45). The authors attribute this mainly to the gap in initial success.
  - With feedback (Exp. 2): short-answer beat MC on the final MC test (d = .41), but did not differ significantly from MC on the final short-answer test. Feedback improved outcomes after short-answer practice (d = .40) but not reliably after MC.
  - A wrong MC choice reappeared on the final MC test at .65 without feedback, falling to .30 with feedback.
  - [Kang et al. 2007 PDF](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/2007-kang.pdf)
- **Little, Bjork, Bjork & Angello (2012), Psychological Science. [RCT, lab · FULL]**
  - Exp. 1: 32 UCLA undergraduates, no feedback. Exp. 2: 96 WUSTL undergraduates, with or without feedback. Materials: two passages of about 1,100 words. MC with competitive (plausible) alternatives vs cued recall. Final cued-recall test after a 5-minute distractor in Exp. 1.
  - Tested items, Exp. 1: MC d = 1.30 and cued recall d = 0.67 vs controls; initial accuracy was 70% vs 43%.
  - With feedback, MC and cued recall did not differ on tested items.
  - Related, untested items: MC beat controls in Exp. 2 (d = 0.43) and beat cued recall regardless of feedback (d = 0.59). Cued recall lowered related-item recall in Exp. 1 (d = 0.55 below controls).
  - These benefits depend on competitive alternatives.
  - [Little et al. 2012 PDF](https://itlab.us/TBL/Harvesting/TBL/Multile%20Choice%20Tests%20Exonerated.pdf)
- **Rowland (2014), Psychological Bulletin, lab meta-analysis. [META · SECONDHAND, earlier notes]**
  - 61 studies and 159 effect sizes, g = 0.50. Cued recall g = 0.61 vs recognition g = 0.29.
  - Feedback g = 0.73 vs 0.39 without.
  - Without feedback, initial test success of 50% or less gave g = 0.03, and above 75% gave g = 0.56.
  - [Rowland 2014 PDF, as cited in learning_science.md](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf)
- **Yang et al. (2021), classroom meta-analysis. [META · SECONDHAND, earlier notes]** Practice format matching the exam format g = 0.531 vs mismatched 0.399. Rephrased items g = 0.558 vs identical items 0.512. — [Yang et al. 2021 PDF, as cited in learning_science.md](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- **Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017), Review of Educational Research, 217 studies. [META · SUM, Learning Scientists post by Weinstein, Feb. 2017]**
  - Hybrid formats (e.g., MC plus cued recall) were most effective, and matching formats had a modest advantage.
  - Benefits held on transfer tests and in classrooms; the largest effects were in secondary school.
  - Feedback was only slightly more effective than no feedback.
  - The post gives no g values.
  - [Learning Scientists](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/2/9-1)
- **Pan & Rickard (2018). [META · PART]** Transfer was greatest "across test formats." — [Pan & Rickard 2018 PDF](https://rickardlab.ucsd.edu/pdf/PR_2018.pdf)
- **Pan & Rickard (2017). [RCT, lab · FULL]** MC practice with feedback on term-definition facts produced a testing effect on a later short-answer test for the practiced term (Exp. 3a). Practicing the definition response needed three trials per fact to beat restudy (Exp. 4, d = 0.37). — [Pan & Rickard 2017 PDF](https://rickardlab.ucsd.edu/pdf/PR_JEPA_2017.pdf)
- **Prior format studies as summarized by Smith & Karpicke (2014). [SECONDHAND]**
  - Gay (1980): short-answer practice was better only when the final test was also short-answer.
  - McDaniel, Roediger & McDermott (2007): explain short-answer advantages by greater retrieval difficulty.
  - Butler & Roediger (2007), McDaniel et al. (2007), Clariana (2003) and Duchastel (1981) reported short-answer advantages. Clariana & Lee (2001), Duchastel & Nungester (1982), Frase (1968), Haynie (1994) and Williams (1963) did not.
  - [Smith & Karpicke 2014 PDF](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2014/2014_Smith_Karpicke_Memory.pdf)

### Inferences
- Format differences are small next to the effect of retrieving at all (d of about 1.2-1.3 vs no retrieval in Smith & Karpicke) and next to the effect of feedback. Most of the meta-analytic recall-over-recognition gap may arise in no-feedback conditions; Rowland's format split was not reported separately by feedback here, so this is unverified.
- Recall practice costs about 3-5 extra seconds per correct item in Smith & Karpicke.
- Without feedback, recall's lower initial success removes much of its benefit (Rowland's initial-success moderator; Kang Exp. 1).
- Without feedback, MC lures persist (.65 in Kang). For formulas, lures such as wrong coefficients or exponents would be the analogue, but no study tested formula lures.
- All format evidence here is on prose facts. Formulas and theorem statements were not studied, and the free-recall vs cued-recall distinction for definitions was not tested in the studies retrieved.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved: Carpenter & DeLosh (2006), free vs cued vs recognition as the initial test; Glover (1989); McDermott, Agarwal, D'Antonio, Roediger & McDaniel (2014), MC vs short-answer quizzes in middle and high school; McDaniel, Anderson, Derbish & Morrisette (2007), classroom short-answer vs MC; Little & Bjork (2015).
- Adesope et al. (2017) moderator g values were not retrieved.
- Rowland (2014) and Yang (2021) numbers come only from earlier notes.
- Little et al. (2012) used a short delay (5 minutes in Exp. 1), so long-delay format effects are not established by that study.
- No study compared formats for math formulas or geometric theorems.

## Q5. Forgetting of precollege geometry and algebra and its effect on calculus: long-term retention, prerequisite knowledge as a predictor, and whether brief refreshers help

### Takeaway
School algebra and geometry are largely forgotten unless used in later courses. In Bahrick & Hall's cross-sectional study of about 1,700 people, those who took math beyond calculus kept their algebra for about 50 years, while people who studied it for only one year performed near chance (secondhand).
- Prerequisite mastery predicts calculus outcomes moderately: concept-readiness r = .28 to .51; a basic-skills test R² = .34; precalculus mastery had "twice the impact" of taking high-school calculus.
- Spacing retrieval of precalculus skills raised retention into the next course by about 5 points.
- Refresher and remediation studies are small, nonrandomized and confounded.
- No source isolated geometry-formula recall as the bottleneck in calculus applications. In geometric related-rates problems, the weakest steps were translating prose into geometric and symbolic form.

### Cited Findings
- **Bahrick & Hall (1991), JEP: General, "Lifetime maintenance of high school mathematics content." [OBS, cross-sectional · SECONDHAND, three summaries]**
  - EdWeek interview: 1,726 people tested with MC questions on algebra and plane geometry, ages "ranging … from students to senior citizens"; "The rate of forgetting over time does not appear to be a factor of ability." — [EdWeek, Jan. 1991](https://www.edweek.org/education/psychologist-examines-retention-of-mathematical-knowledge/1991/01)
  - Willingham: "more than 1,000" people, 0 to 74 years after the algebra course. Those who took courses beyond calculus showed no algebra loss about 50 years later; those who took Algebra II remembered more but eventually lost most of it. — [Willingham, American Educator, Fall 2015](https://aft.org/node/10659)
  - Karsenty: about 1,700 people. When learning spanned several years, with content relearned and used in later math courses, performance was retained for more than 50 years. Those whose learning was limited to one year performed near chance. Grades and standardized test scores had "minor influences" on the rate of loss; the amount and distribution of practice affected it most. — [Karsenty 2002, Educational Studies in Mathematics 51](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/ScienceTeaching/Karsenty/sites/ScienceTeaching.Karsenty/files/uploads/Karsenty%202002%20in%20ESM.pdf)
  - The three summaries disagree on N (1,726, about 1,700, more than 1,000). None gives geometry-specific results.
- **Other school-knowledge retention cited by Willingham. [REVIEW/OBS · SECONDHAND]**
  - A mid-1990s review (author not named in the fetched text): about 28% average loss across 22 experiments with recall questions, and about 16% across 52 with recognition questions.
  - Pawl et al. (2012): MIT seniors retook a freshman mechanics exam about three years later. Biology majors lost about 55% of problem-solving ability; physics majors lost none of it but about 25% of conceptual understanding.
  - [Willingham, AFT](https://aft.org/node/10659)
- **Karsenty (2002), own study. [OBS, qualitative interviews · FULL]** 24 adults aged 30-45 with post-secondary degrees; most could not draw a correct graph of a simple linear function such as y = x. Of the 7 who succeeded, 5 were high-track graduates. — [Karsenty 2002 PDF](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/ScienceTeaching/Karsenty/sites/ScienceTeaching.Karsenty/files/uploads/Karsenty%202002%20in%20ESM.pdf)
- **Sadler & Sonnert (2018), J. for Research in Mathematics Education. [OBS · SUM, Harvard Gazette, July 2018]** More than 6,000 college freshmen at 133 institutions. "A firm grip on precalculus subjects" (algebra, geometry, trigonometry) "had twice the impact of a high school course" in calculus on college calculus performance. Among students who took high-school calculus, the weakest benefited most. — [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2018/07/masters-of-calculus-come-prepared-harvard-study-shows/)
- **Carlson, Madison & West, Calculus Concept Readiness (CCR) instrument. [OBS, validation study · FULL (arXiv preprint, 2010)]**
  - 25 MC items on function, covariation, rate of change, and exponential and trigonometric functions. 631 beginning calculus students at three universities.
  - Correlation of CCR score with Calculus 1 final grade: 0.51 (fall) and 0.28 (spring). Correlation with ACT math: 0.54.
  - Fall 2009: 30% of students scoring 0-7 earned A/B/C, vs 100% of the 15 scoring 17-25. Mean score 11.83 for A/B/C students vs 8.49 for D/W/F. Students scoring 11 or more passed at 66%, 86% and 91% across universities.
  - A footnote says procedure-focused precalculus courses may not shift scores on this conceptual readiness test.
  - [arXiv 1010.2719](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1010.2719)
- **Lo & Piccolo, UBC Basic Skills Test (CWSEI). [OBS · FULL]**
  - 30 items: 50% algebra, 25% functions, 25% geometry.
  - Differential calculus (Math 180, 2008, N = 416): R² = 0.34 with final grade. Science One (N = 61): R² = 0.62-0.72.
  - Weak spots: isolating a variable (29-69% correct by course), inequalities (34-50%), and word problems (37% in Math 180/184).
  - No intervention was tested.
  - [CWSEI PDF](https://cwsei.ubc.ca/files/outcomes/EOY/Lo_Piccolo_BasicSkillsMath.pdf)
- **Chariker, Ralston, Hieb & Wilkins (2013, ASEE), University of Louisville. [OBS · PART]**
  - 2011 cohort, N = 340 took an Algebra Readiness Exam.
  - Scores were positively related to exam and quiz performance in Engineering Analysis I and predicted staying in the course.
  - Students who completed a summer calculus-preview (algebra review) program were more likely to stay, despite lower readiness scores.
  - Self-selection was acknowledged, and no effect sizes were given.
  - [ASEE PEER](https://peer.asee.org/an-analysis-of-two-interventions-designed-to-improve-student-performance-in-engineering-calculus)
- **Hopkins, Lyle, Hieb & Ralston (2016), Educational Psychology Review 28, 853-873. [classroom experiment: within-subject spaced vs massed, plus comparison with a control cohort · SUM, guest post by an author (Lyle, Oct. 2016)]**
  - Engineering precalculus, fall 2014. Three quiz questions per operation, either massed on one quiz or spaced across about 4 weeks.
  - Spaced operations scored about 3% higher within the experimental group, and about 8% higher than the same operations massed in the control cohort.
  - Next-semester calculus final: the experimental group scored about 10% higher on average; 68% earned at least a C vs 48% of controls, not statistically significant. 54 students were in the calculus analysis.
  - [Learning Scientists guest post](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2016/10/11-1)
- **Hopkins, Lyle, Ralston, Bego & Hieb (2018, ASEE). [classroom experiment, objectives counterbalanced across conditions · FULL]**
  - N = 51 engineering precalculus students. Design: 2 (amount: 3 vs 6 questions) × 2 (timing: massed vs spaced across weeks 0, 2 and 4), covering 32 objectives.
  - Retention was measured with an ungraded readiness test in the next course, one month after the precalculus final.
  - Spaced .59 vs massed .54, F(1, 50) = 7.87, p = .007, η² = .136 ("half a letter grade"). Amount had no effect (F < 1).
  - The analyzed sample was self-selected (students who continued to the next course). The timing effect was significant in 71% of 100 balanced subsets.
  - [ASEE PDF](https://peer.asee.org/29900.pdf)
- **Lyle, Bego, Hopkins, Hieb & Ralston (2020), Educational Psychology Review. [classroom experiment · SUM, Learning Scientists post by Nebel, Sept. 2022]**
  - Precalculus for pre-engineering students. Conditions: 3 vs 6 questions, on one weekly quiz vs spread across four.
  - On the precalculus final, spacing and extra quizzing were about equally beneficial, and combining them helped further.
  - On a pretest about four weeks into the next semester's calculus course, the spacing benefit persisted and the extra-quizzing benefit disappeared. Spacing lowered initial quiz scores.
  - The analysis was limited to the roughly half of the class who took all quizzes.
  - [Learning Scientists](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2022/9/7-1)
- **"Spaced Retrieval Practice Imposes Desirable Difficulty in Calculus Learning," Educational Psychology Review 34(3), 1799-1812 (NSF-funded). [classroom experiment · ABS]** In a calculus course, 3 problems per objective were massed on one quiz or spaced across three quizzes. Spacing increased retention on an end-of-semester test but lowered practice-quiz performance on the first two problems. — [NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10369433)
- **Bego et al. (2024), International J. of STEM Education. [META across courses · SECONDHAND, earlier notes]** In single-paper meta-analyses of spaced retrieval in nine introductory STEM courses, Calculus I (N = 180) was one of the courses with a significant spacing benefit. The pooled nine-course improvement was 2.06 percentage points (95% CI 0.16-3.97). — [DOI 10.1186/s40594-024-00468-5, as cited in curves_time_and_forgetting.md](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40594-024-00468-5)
- **Martin (2000), Mathematics Education Research Journal 12(2), 74-91, geometric related rates. [OBS · ABS]**
  - University introductory calculus students solved geometric related-rates problems and took a separate test of the individual solution steps, each classed as procedural or conceptual.
  - Overall performance was poor. The weakest steps were conceptual, especially translating prose into geometric and symbolic representations.
  - Overall performance related most strongly to the procedural steps.
  - [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF03217077)
- **Vandenbussche (ASEE; Southern Polytechnic State University; Fall 2012 data), early incentivized remediation of prerequisites in Calculus I. [QUASI · FULL]**
  - Intervention: algebra and trigonometry worksheets on the first two class days, then a 35-point prerequisite test on day 5. Remediation was required for an optional retake (practice to 80% correct; office hours for low scorers).
  - Groups: test section N = 34; 208 students in other Fall 2012 sections; 65 in the author's Spring 2012 sections.
  - Retake gain +5.5 of 35 points. GPA 2.44 vs 1.84 (p = .02) and vs 1.40 (p = .001). F or W 15% vs 31% (p = .02) and vs 48%.
  - The author states the results "cannot be decisively attributed to the remediation program"; the homework policy also changed between semesters.
  - [ASEE PDF](https://sftp.asee.org/a-study-of-the-effects-of-early-remediation-in-prerequisite-material-in-a-calculus-i-course.pdf)
- **Elias (2012), MA thesis, University of Houston. [experiment; randomization not stated · ABS]** 63 college students received procedural or conceptual algebra refreshers; both groups improved from pretest to posttest, with no overall difference between them. A conceptual advantage (d = +0.53) appeared only after excluding students at ceiling or floor. — [UH repository](https://uh-ir.tdl.org/items/f4df5126-ea53-4d85-bb25-761e665a5409/full)

### Inferences
- Bahrick-type data (secondhand) indicate forgetting is the default without continued use. Continued use in later courses was the strongest maintenance factor, stronger than grades or ability.
- Prerequisite measures explain a moderate share of calculus outcomes (r of about .3 to .5; R² of about .34). Most of the variance is left unexplained, and the measures mix procedural algebra, functions and some geometry.
- Retrieval-practice interventions in precalculus with measured carryover into calculus come from one institution (Louisville). The effects are modest: about 5 points on a readiness test, and a nonsignificant pass-rate difference in the 2016 study. The carryover benefit came from spacing rather than from extra questions.
- None of the refresher or remediation studies isolates a brief review of facts such as formulas and definitions from broader remediation, and all are confounded (nonrandom sections, policy changes, self-selection).
- For geometry specifically, Martin's abstract points to representation (translating prose into a diagram and equations) as the weakest step rather than formula recall. The abstract does not separate formula recall from the other procedural steps.

### Gaps
- The Bahrick & Hall (1991) primary was not read. An earlier pass found PsycNet blocked; it was not retried here. Geometry-vs-algebra curves, the timing of the decline, and exact percentages are missing.
- Hailikari and colleagues: the one candidate PMC page (PMC3217262) returned a reCAPTCHA browser-verification page and was not retried. Searches did not surface the Hailikari, Nevgi & Komulainen 2008 mathematics study.
- The Sadler & Sonnert (2018) primary (effect sizes, how geometry was measured) was not read.
- No randomized trial of brief prerequisite-fact refreshers in calculus was found.
- Martin (2000): N, per-step success rates, and whether formula recall was a separate step are not in the abstract.
- No data were found on retention of specific geometry formulas or theorems (e.g., cone volume, the inscribed angle theorem) among calculus students.

## Q6. Mixing fact retrieval with problems in the same session versus separate sessions

### Takeaway
Only Agarwal (2019) directly compared mixed fact-plus-higher-order practice with pure practice. Mixed quizzes produced gains on both test types.
- College lab (Exp. 2): each pure quiz type beat the mix on its own matched test (higher-order .85 vs .71; fact .90 vs .78).
- 6th-grade classroom (Exp. 3): mixed quizzes beat higher-order-only quizzes on fact items (.91 vs .64) and marginally on higher-order items (.82 vs .75, d = 0.34).
- No study was found that compares mixing within one session against separate sessions, or that mixes math formula recall with math problems.

### Cited Findings
- **Agarwal (2019), Exp. 2, college lab. [RCT, within-subjects · FULL]**
  - Mixed 2X (one fact and one higher-order quiz, so the total number of quizzes was matched to the other 2X conditions) vs fact 2X, higher-order 2X and higher-order 1X.
  - Delayed averages: mixed .75, fact 2X .69, higher-order 2X .69, higher-order 1X .65.
  - Higher-order test: mixed .71 vs higher-order 2X .85 (d = 0.84, mixed significantly lower). Mixed was also below higher-order 1X (.77); the significance of that comparison is not in the extraction.
  - Fact test: mixed .78 vs fact 2X .90 (d = 0.77).
  - [Agarwal 2019 PDF](http://pdf.poojaagarwal.com/Agarwal_2019_JEdPsych.pdf)
- **Agarwal (2019), Exp. 3, 6th-grade classroom. [classroom within-subjects experiment, nonrandom sections · FULL]**
  - Mixed quizzes had 4 fact and 4 higher-order items in the same quiz, compared with 8-item higher-order quizzes and nonquizzed material; three quizzes per chapter.
  - Fact test: mixed .91 vs higher-order .64 (d = 1.44) and nonquizzed .64 (d = 1.55).
  - Higher-order test: mixed .82 vs nonquizzed .56 (d = 1.37) and higher-order .75 (d = 0.34, p = .078 after Bonferroni correction, marginal).
  - Main effect ηp² = .60; interaction ηp² = .25.
  - Confounds: final items identical to quiz items; pizza-party incentive; week-long blocking.
  - [Agarwal 2019 PDF](http://pdf.poojaagarwal.com/Agarwal_2019_JEdPsych.pdf)
- **Agarwal (2019), conclusions. [FULL]** Mixed quizzes were "robust" for higher-order learning in Exp. 3 and gave the strongest overall results. Building fact knowledge first through retrieval practice "may be less potent" than higher-order retrieval practice from the outset. — [Agarwal 2019 PDF](http://pdf.poojaagarwal.com/Agarwal_2019_JEdPsych.pdf)
- **McDaniel et al. (2013), Exp. 2. [classroom experiment · ABS]** Application quizzes alone improved both definition-type and new application exam questions, while definition quizzes did not help application questions. Application practice covered both item types without a separate definition quiz. — [WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/quizzing-in-middle-school-science-successful-transfer-performance/)
- **Jensen et al. (2014). [QUASI · PART]** A semester of high-level quizzes and exams improved both low-level and high-level final-exam items compared with low-level quizzing. — [Springer DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-013-9248-9)
- **Mixed formats, not mixed content. [RCT, lab · FULL]** In Smith & Karpicke (2014), hybrid MC plus short-answer practice, massed or spaced within the practice phase, performed about the same as single formats (between-format ds ≤ 0.09). In Exp. 4, the hybrid beat MC on the final short-answer test (.63 vs .50). — [Smith & Karpicke 2014 PDF](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2014/2014_Smith_Karpicke_Memory.pdf)
- **Adesope et al. (2017). [META · SUM]** Hybrid test formats were the most effective (format mixing, not content-level mixing). — [Learning Scientists](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/2/9-1)
- **Yeo & Fazio. [RCT, lab · SUM]** Which strategy wins depends on the learning goal (remember facts or a passage vs learn a procedure) and on the delay. — [Learning Scientists guest post](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2018/4/11-1)

### Inferences
- In Agarwal Exp. 2 the total number of retrieval opportunities was fixed at two, so the mixed condition got half the matched practice for each test type. Its pattern (best average, but below pure matched practice on each test) fits the idea that matched-practice dose matters, rather than any special benefit of mixing.
- In Agarwal Exp. 2, adding a fact quiz to one higher-order quiz did not raise higher-order scores (.71 vs .77 for higher-order 1X), consistent with no transfer from facts to higher-order questions in that study.
- The mixed advantage on higher-order items in Exp. 3 is marginal and confounded (identical final items, incentives). Whether mixing adds anything beyond covering both item types is unresolved.
- McDaniel 2013 and Jensen 2014 suggest application or high-level practice can cover definitional items, but Agarwal's three experiments do not replicate this. Both lines of evidence come from non-math courses.

### Gaps
- No study was found that compares fact retrieval and problems in the same session against separate sessions or days.
- No math-specific study was found that mixes formula or definition recall with problems.
- The order of the fact and higher-order quizzes within Agarwal's Exp. 2 mixed condition was not extracted.
- No study was found that tests "interleaving facts with application items" as a distinct manipulation from simply including both item types.
