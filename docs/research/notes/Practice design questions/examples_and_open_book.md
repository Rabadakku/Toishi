# Worked example first vs problem first, and open-book vs closed-book practice, for a newly taught math skill

Labels used on every finding: evidence type (meta-analysis, systematic review, review, RCT = randomized experiment, quasi-experiment, observational, or secondhand = described by someone other than the study's authors) and what was actually read (full text, abstract, citation only, or a secondary summary). Populations and N are given wherever the page read stated them; "N not stated" means the page read did not give it.

## 1. For learners who have already been taught the skill, does starting practice with a worked example beat starting with a problem?

### Takeaway
In controlled comparisons of pair sequences, example-first (EP) usually beats problem-first (PE) and problem-only (PP) on immediate tests. A random-effects pooling of 10 EP-vs-PE comparisons gave d = 0.35 for EP, with 8 of 10 favoring EP. But these studies mostly used novices with no prior instruction on the skill, and they mostly measured immediate outcomes. For learners who have already been taught, the evidence is only indirect. Worked examples still help in math when used as practice, not just for first acquisition (meta-analysis, g = 0.48 overall). However, immediate advantages for examples often shrink or vanish on one-week delayed tests, and they reverse once learners know more.

### Cited Findings

#### Example-problem vs problem-example vs problem-only sequences
- **[RCT; full text read]** van Harsel, Hoogerheide, Verkoeijen & van Gog (2019, *Contemporary Educational Psychology* 58, 260–275), Experiment 1: 124 first-year electrical/mechanical engineering students at a Dutch university of applied sciences (117 male). They learned to approximate a definite integral with the trapezoidal rule from video modeling examples. Each did four training tasks in one of four sequences: EEEE (n=34), EPEP (n=25), PEPE (n=30), PPPP (n=35). Students were novices because the topic was not yet in their curriculum. There was no prior instruction phase. The design was pretest, training, then immediate posttest — [Source](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/115428/Repub_115428.pdf)
- **[RCT; full text read]** Same paper, Experiment 1 results:
  - Isomorphic posttest: EEEE > PPPP (r = .551), EEEE > EPEP (r = .366), and PEPE > PPPP (r = .356). EEEE vs PEPE was not significant, and neither was EPEP vs PEPE (p = .131).
  - No condition differences on procedural transfer (Simpson's rule, p = .276) or conceptual transfer (p = .104).
  - Training mental effort was lower for EEEE and EPEP than for PPPP (r = .730 and .535).
  - Training time was shortest for EEEE and longest for EPEP.
  - Self-efficacy after training was higher in every example condition than in PPPP, and EEEE > EPEP (r = .375).
  - A planned one-week delayed test was excluded from analysis because it was not isomorphic to the immediate test.
  — [Source](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/115428/Repub_115428.pdf)
- **[RCT; full text read]** Same paper, Experiment 2: 81 first-year primary-teacher-training students with the same materials: EEEE (n=24), EPEP (n=19), PEPE (n=22), PPPP (n=16).
  - EEEE (r = .414) and EPEP (r = .576) beat PPPP on the isomorphic posttest. PEPE did not differ from any condition.
  - No transfer differences.
  - Reliability was low (α = .33 for the isomorphic test, .39 for conceptual transfer), and pretest self-efficacy differed between conditions.
  - The paper's abstract and its results section disagree on whether EEEE was more efficient than EPEP in this experiment.
  — [Source](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/115428/Repub_115428.pdf)
- **[Small meta-analysis inside an RCT paper; full text read]** van Harsel et al. pooled 10 EP-vs-PE comparisons with a random-effects model: d = 0.35 favoring EP, with substantial heterogeneity (8 comparisons favored EP, 2 favored PE). No single candidate moderator (example format, number of tasks) explained the variation. The authors had proposed that starting with a failed problem demotivates learners, but this explanation was not supported in either experiment — [Source](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/115428/Repub_115428.pdf)
- **[RCT; abstract of the 2010 conference version read; population taken from the van Harsel et al. introduction, so secondhand]** van Gog, Kester & Paas (2011, *Contemporary Educational Psychology*) compared four conditions: worked examples only, EP pairs, PE pairs, and problem solving only. Examples only and EP pairs were "more effective and efficient than problem solving only and problem-example pairs" — [Source](https://research.ou.nl/en/publications/effects-of-worked-examples-example-problem-pairs-and-problem-exam/). Per van Harsel et al., the participants were secondary-education students learning to troubleshoot electrical circuits, and PEPE did not outperform PPPP — [Source](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/115428/Repub_115428.pdf). This conflicts with van Harsel Experiment 1, where PEPE > PPPP.
- **[RCT; abstract read]** Leppink, Paas, van Gog, van der Vleuten & van Merriënboer (2014, *Learning and Instruction* 30, 32–42), Study II: 74 university freshmen learning applications of Bayes' theorem (18 EE, 18 EP, 18 PE, 20 PP). The EE and EP groups outperformed the PE and PP groups on the posttest. The abstract does not say whether there was a prior instruction phase, and it gives no effect sizes or test delay. Study I (174 students in a statistics lecture) found that statistics exam scores correlated negatively with the intrinsic and extraneous load measured during the lecture — [Source](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/effects-of-pairs-of-problems-and-examples-on-task-performance-and/)
- **[RCT; citation, plus the first author's dissertation abstract]** Kant, Scheiter & Oschatz (2017, *Learning and Instruction* 52, 46–58): school students acquiring scientific-reasoning (experimentation) skills. The dissertation abstract says that showing video modeling examples before the inquiry task, or in place of it, worked better than showing them after it. Grade, N, measures and effect sizes were not on the pages read — [Source](https://bibliographie.uni-tuebingen.de/xmlui/handle/10900/87311); [Source](https://publikationen.uni-tuebingen.de/xmlui/handle/10900/74159)
- **[Classroom experiment; dataset description read]** Hsu, Kalyuga & Sweller (data released 2014, linked to an article in *Archives of Scientific Psychology*): high-school physics students. The study compared example-problem and problem-example sequences that used identical examples and problems, so only the order varied. It also tested adding principle-based explanations. The authors conclude learning benefits from "early rather than later use of explicit guidance" that prioritizes worked examples. N and any prior-knowledge interaction were not reported on the page — [Source](https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/web/ICPSR/studies/35626/staff)

#### Worked examples in mathematics: the meta-analysis
- **[Meta-analysis; abstract read in a repost that matches the title]** Barbieri, Miller-Cotto, Clerjuste & Chawla (2023, *Educational Psychology Review* 35, 11):
  - Scope: 8,033 abstracts screened; 43 articles, 55 studies, 181 effect sizes, from elementary school through postsecondary; robust variance estimation.
  - Overall: g = 0.48 (p = .01; CI not shown) favoring worked examples over problem-solving controls.
  - Correct-only examples produced larger effects than incorrect-only or mixed correct and incorrect examples.
  - Adding self-explanation prompts was a significant moderator: it reduced the effect compared with examples without prompts.
  - The effect was medium both when examples were used for practice and when they were used for first acquisition of the skill.
  — [Source](https://ifp.nyu.edu/?p=1659284)
- **[Secondhand: blog summary of the published meta-analysis]** Barbieri et al. did not find that prior knowledge consistently moderated the benefit. Some studies showed larger benefits at higher prior knowledge, some at lower, and some no interaction. Comparisons were against problem-solving-only controls — [Source](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2024/1/25-1)
- **[Meta-analysis, preliminary 2022 conference poster; full poster read]** The earlier version reported 40 articles, 49 studies (46 experimental, 3 quasi-experimental) and 185 effect sizes: g = 0.43, 95% CI [0.31, 0.55], I² ≈ 94%. None of the moderators tested (example type, administration format, grade/school level, content area) was significant. Only 4 articles used faded examples. These figures differ from the published version (55 studies, g = 0.48) — [Source](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Worked-Examples-Meta-Poster_022722_SC.pdf)

#### Delayed tests
- **[Four RCTs; abstract read]** van Gog, Kester, Dirkx et al. (2015, *Educational Psychology Review* 27, 265–289):
  - Experiment 1 (N = 120 Dutch university students): examples only vs example-problem pairs vs example-recall, tested after 5 minutes or after one week. Solving a problem or recalling the example after example study gave no benefit over continued example study.
  - Experiment 2 (N = 124 Belgian university students), Experiment 3 (N = 129 Belgian university students) and Experiment 4 (57 vocational students analyzed; probability calculation): no testing (problem-solving) benefit over restudy on the one-week delayed test in any experiment.
  — [Source](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-015-9297-3)
- **[RCT; summary written by the study's own authors]** Yeo & Fazio (2019, *Journal of Educational Psychology*, online 2018): college students learning to solve Poisson-distribution probability problems.
  - Immediate test: studying worked examples beat solving problems, whether the practice problems were identical or non-identical.
  - One-week delay: problem solving equaled example study with non-identical problems and beat it with identical problems.
  - N not stated.
  — [Source](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2018/4/11-1)
- **[RCT; abstract read]** Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller (2016, *Learning and Instruction* 45, 20–30): high-school trigonometry, with a 2 (guidance) × 2 (element interactivity) × 2 (expertise) design.
  - Immediate test: a worked-example effect for novices, and a generation effect (problem solving better) for more knowledgeable learners.
  - Delayed test: novices showed a worked-example effect on high-element-interactivity material and a generation effect on low-element-interactivity material. More knowledgeable learners showed a generation effect on both.
  - N not stated.
  — [Source](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/Relations_between_the_worked_example_and_generation_effects_on_immediate_and_delayed_tests/12052686)

#### Fading (Renkl and colleagues)
- **[Two RCTs; abstract read]** Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill (2003, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 95, 774–783).
  - The abstract reports that prior research found gradually fading worked-out steps reliably improved near transfer compared with example-problem pairs, but not far transfer.
  - Here, combining fading with prompts to name the principle behind each worked step produced "medium to large" effects on both near and far transfer, without adding time on task.
  - Population and N not on the page read.
  — [Source](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/transitioning-from-studying-examples-to-solving-problems-effects-/)

### Inferences
- The most direct EP-vs-PE evidence (van Harsel 2019, van Gog 2011, Leppink 2014, Hsu, Kant 2017) comes from learners meeting the skill for the first time and from immediate posttests. van Harsel explicitly had no prior instruction phase. These studies did not test the "already read the section or attended the lecture" case.
- The sample closest to college Calculus I is van Harsel Experiment 1 (first-year engineering students, a definite-integral approximation task). There, EP and PE did not differ significantly. The significant contrasts were examples-only vs the rest, and any-example condition vs problems-only.
- The pooled d = 0.35 for EP over PE is small to moderate and heterogeneous, from 10 comparisons.
- Barbieri's finding that the effect is similar for examples used as practice and for first acquisition is the closest evidence for already-instructed learners. It compares examples with problem solving, not the order within pairs.
- On delayed tests, the evidence is mixed and appears to depend on prior knowledge and element interactivity:
  - Example advantages at immediate test did not persist at one week for non-identical problems (Yeo & Fazio).
  - Practice problems after examples did not beat restudy at one week (van Gog 2015).
  - More knowledgeable learners did better with problem solving at delay (Chen 2016).
- Conflict on self-explanation prompts: in math, prompts reduced the worked-example benefit (Barbieri 2023), while fading combined with prompts to identify principles produced medium-to-large gains (Atkinson 2003). The comparison conditions differ, so the two results are not directly comparable.

### Gaps
- No study was found that compares EP and PE for learners who have already received textbook or lecture instruction on the same skill and that includes a delayed test.
- van Gog & Kester (2012, *Cognitive Science* 36, 1532–1541), "A test of the testing effect", was verified only as a citation; its abstract and results were not on the page read — [Source](https://research.ou.nl/en/publications/a-test-of-the-testing-effect-acquiring-problem-solving-skills-fro/)
- Other Hoogerheide work not retrieved: van Harsel et al. (2020, *Applied Cognitive Psychology*) on shorter vs longer sequences. Also not retrieved: Renkl et al. (2002) and Kissane et al. (2008) on fading and delayed performance.
- Barbieri et al. (2023): the published moderator statistics for age/grade and prior knowledge, and the CI, were not seen.
- Missing details: Kant 2017 (grade, N, effect sizes); Hsu et al. (N, effect sizes, journal volume/pages); Leppink 2014 (effect sizes, timing of the test).

## 2. Expertise reversal: at what level of prior knowledge do worked examples stop helping or start hurting?

### Takeaway
A 2025 meta-analysis (60 studies, 176 effect sizes, about 6,000 participants; seen only through a secondary summary) reports a medium-sized reversal in both directions. Learners with low prior knowledge gained more from high-support instruction (d = 0.505). Learners with high prior knowledge gained more from low-support instruction (d = −0.428). The reversal was strongest in higher education and grew with age. No source gives a numeric prior-knowledge cut-off. Cognitive-load-theory authors describe the crossover in terms of how complex the material is (element interactivity) relative to what the learner already knows, and they detect it with quick, task-specific knowledge tests.

### Cited Findings
- **[Meta-analysis; secondhand blog summary plus citation]** Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod (2025, *Learning and Instruction* 98, 102142), "A cornerstone of adaptivity: A meta-analysis of the expertise reversal effect": 60 studies, 176 effect sizes, about 6,000 participants.
  - Low prior knowledge: high-support > low-support instruction, d = 0.505.
  - High prior knowledge: low-support > high-support instruction, d = −0.428.
  - The effect grows with student age, is strongest in higher education (few primary-school studies exist), and is weaker in language and humanities.
  - The summary does not say whether worked examples were analyzed as their own support type.
  - The blog cautions that prior knowledge and instructional structure are continuous, not categories.
  — [Source](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/one-size-doesnt-fit-all-teaching-experts-and-novices/); citation — [Source](https://hubbs.schule/mediathek/tetzlaff-et-al-2025-instruktion-versus-selbstorganisation)
- **[Review; abstract read]** Kalyuga (2007, *Educational Psychology Review* 19, 509–539) reviews research since the mid-1990s showing that learners' prior knowledge changes which instructional techniques work best. The review explains this with cognitive load theory, notes its resemblance to aptitude-treatment-interaction research, and covers adaptive systems that apply it — [Source](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-007-9054-3)
- **[RCT; abstract read]** Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller (2016), high-school trigonometry. The reversal depends on both expertise and the complexity of the material:
  - Immediate test: worked examples were better for novices, and problem solving for more knowledgeable learners.
  - Delayed test: novices benefited from worked examples only on high-element-interactivity material. More knowledgeable learners benefited from problem solving on both low- and high-interactivity material.
  — [Source](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/Relations_between_the_worked_example_and_generation_effects_on_immediate_and_delayed_tests/12052686)
- **[Experiment with yoked controls; abstract read]** Kalyuga & Sweller (2005, *Educational Technology Research and Development* 53, 83–93): an elementary-algebra tutor adapted instruction to each learner's changing expertise, using rapid knowledge tests combined with cognitive-load ratings. Each control learner received the same sequence as a yoked partner. The adapted group showed greater knowledge gains and cognitive efficiency. N, and the exact formats switched between (examples, faded examples, problems), were not on the page read — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf02504800)
- **[Citation only]** "When problem solving is superior to studying worked examples" (2001, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 93, 579–588). The two repository records list the authors in different orders (UOW: Chandler, Kalyuga, Sweller & Tuovinen; UNSW: Kalyuga, Chandler, Sweller, Tuovinen). Neither page had the abstract — [Source](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/139/); [Source](https://unsworks.unsw.edu.au/entities/publication/2b55084b-53c3-4baf-8795-703413f0b115/full)
- **[Secondhand: blog summary]** Within math worked-example studies, prior knowledge did not consistently moderate the benefit (Barbieri et al. 2023) — [Source](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2024/1/25-1)
- **[Observational; full text read; on help rather than examples, related]** In a geometry tutor, whether seeking help was associated with better next-step performance depended on the student's estimated skill on that step (see Question 4) — [Source](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/Roll%20HT%20JLS%20clean.pdf)

### Inferences
- The meta-analytic contrast is between low and high prior knowledge. Learners who have just read the section or attended the lecture are plausibly in between, and the summary read does not say where intermediate learners fall.
- The finding that the reversal is strongest in higher education matters for college Calculus I samples.
- The sources treat expertise as specific to a task and a type of material (element interactivity), measured with quick diagnostic tests, not as a global trait of the learner (Chen 2016; Kalyuga & Sweller 2005).
- The math worked-example meta-analysis did not find consistent prior-knowledge moderation (Barbieri), while the general expertise-reversal meta-analysis did (Tetzlaff). The two used different study pools and different definitions of "support".

### Gaps
- The Tetzlaff et al. (2025) full text was not read. All of its numbers are secondhand. Unknown: how prior knowledge was split into groups, whether worked examples were a separate subgroup, and the CIs.
- The details of the 2001 *Journal of Educational Psychology* paper and of Kalyuga et al. (2003, *Educational Psychologist*) were not retrieved, including populations, how experience changed across sessions, and effect sizes.
- No source found gives a quantitative prior-knowledge threshold at which examples stop helping or start hurting.
- The rapid-assessment method (first-step tests) and N for Kalyuga & Sweller (2005) were not described on the page read.

## 3. Productive failure and problem solving before instruction: effects on procedural vs conceptual knowledge and transfer, and does any of it apply when instruction comes first?

### Takeaway
Problem solving before instruction (PS-I) beats instruction first (I-PS) on conceptual knowledge and transfer (reported g = 0.36), but not on procedural knowledge. This comes from the main meta-analysis, Sinha & Kapur 2021 (53 studies), whose numbers were read only in secondary summaries. Effects were larger when Productive Failure design principles were followed, and there was no advantage for 2nd and 5th graders. A review concludes PS-I helps only when instruction uses contrasting cases or builds on students' own solutions. RCTs with complex material in Year 5 students favor instruction first. All of this concerns problem solving before any instruction. The app's case (instruction already given, then practice) is the I-PS comparison condition. No retrieved study tests unguided problem-first practice after a lecture.

### Cited Findings
- **[Meta-analysis; secondhand news summary]** Sinha & Kapur (2021, *Review of Educational Research*): 53 studies of STEM students, from grade 2 to postgraduate, mostly grades 6–10, in North America, Europe and Asia.
  - For procedural skills, the first author is quoted: "instruction first will work as well as a problem-solving first design."
  - The PS-I advantage did not hold for second and fifth graders.
  - The article frames the overall effect as about twice "that of an outstanding teacher over a year", and about three times that with Productive Failure design principles.
  — [Source](https://techlearning.com/news/study-productive-failure-a-success-in-education)
- **[Meta-analysis; secondhand university teaching-center summary]** Sinha & Kapur (2021): about 36.7% of comparisons involved undergraduates, and about half were quasi-experiments in real classrooms. PS-I was more effective for conceptual knowledge and transfer (Hedges' g = .36), not significantly better for procedural learning, and weaker when Productive Failure principles were not maintained — [Source](https://education.utexas.edu/about/deans-office-units/office-instructional-innovation/instruction-then-problem-solving-or-vice-versa)
- **[Meta-analysis, working paper; abstract read]** "From problem-solving to sensemaking: A comparative meta-analysis of preparatory approaches for future learning" (EdArXiv working paper, May 2021, ETH record; authors not shown on the page read): 118 comparisons from 33 articles.
  - Plain PS-I vs scaffolded PS-I: g = −0.08 [−0.20, 0.04]. A Bayesian analysis gave about 99% probability that the difference is below 0.2.
  - PS-I vs an alternative sensemaking activity: g = 0.22 [−0.06, 0.51].
  - After adjusting for publication bias: g = 0.55 and 0.64 favoring PS-I.
  - The page did not compare PS-I with I-PS.
  — [Source](https://research-collection.ethz.ch/handle/20.500.11850/492056)
- **[Review; abstract read]** Loibl, Roll & Rummel (2017, *Educational Psychology Review* 29, 693–715): PS-I improves learning only when the design includes contrasting cases or builds instruction on students' own solutions. The review proposes cognitive mechanisms that explain both positive and negative PS-I effects. The abstract does not break results down by knowledge type — [Source](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/towards-a-theory-of-when-and-how-problem-solving-followed-by-inst/)
- **[Meta-analysis; abstract read]** Darabi, Arrington & Sayilir (2018, *Educational Technology Research and Development* 66, 1101–1118): of 187 publications, 62 were relevant and only 12 were experiments with usable statistics, covering productive failure and failure-driven memory. They report a "moderately positive result for the effect of learning from failure". Grade level, subject domain and study duration were not significant moderators. The numeric effect size was not on the page — [Source](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-018-9579-9)
- **[Two RCTs; abstract read of the 2018 CogSci proceedings paper by the same authors, an earlier version of the 2019 article]** Newman & DeCaro:
  - Experiment 1: learners explored statistical variance before instruction using invention, a completion problem, or a worked example. The worked example produced the most learning and the lowest cognitive load.
  - Experiment 2: undergraduate statistics students did invention or worked-example problems either before or after instruction. Learning was greater when problem solving came before instruction, and the worked example did not improve learning over invention.
  — [Source](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/17r1k961)
- **[Two RCTs; abstract read in a repost]** Ashman, Kalyuga & Sweller (2020, *Educational Psychology Review*, DOI 10.1007/s10648-019-09500-5 per the page URL): Year 5 students learning about light energy efficiency.
  - Experiment 1 (N = 64): explicit instruction first was better on problems similar to those taught, with no difference on transfer.
  - Experiment 2 (N = 71, higher element interactivity): explicit instruction first was better on both similar and transfer problems.
  - The authors conclude that explicit instruction should come before problem solving when element interactivity is high.
  — [Source](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2019/journal-article-abstracts/s10648-019-09500-5/)

### Inferences
- In this literature, "instruction" means the canonical lesson. PS-I vs I-PS is a question about the first encounter with a concept. When the textbook section or lecture has already happened, problem-first practice in the app is an instance of I-PS, the comparison condition, so the PS-I benefits do not carry over directly.
- The reported PS-I benefits are for conceptual knowledge and transfer, not procedural knowledge. Practice on Calculus I procedures (for example, applying differentiation rules) falls under procedural knowledge, where no PS-I advantage was found.
- The PS-I advantage depends on specific design features: contrasting cases, instruction that builds on students' solutions, and fidelity to Productive Failure principles. An unscaffolded problem with the solution shown only on give-up is not described as one of those designs in any source read.
- Element interactivity is the boundary condition offered by the cognitive-load side (Ashman 2020; Chen 2016). The Productive Failure side points to design fidelity and outcome type.

### Gaps
- The Sinha & Kapur (2021) primary text was not accessed. The only full-text link that surfaced was a third-party university VPN proxy, which was deliberately not used. Its numbers here (g = .36 for conceptual knowledge and transfer, the fidelity effect, the share of undergraduates) are secondhand. CIs, the number of comparisons per outcome, and the high-fidelity effect size were not verified.
- Kapur's primary experiments (for example, *Cognitive Science* 2014) were not retrieved.
- The full Newman & DeCaro (2019, *Learning and Instruction*) paper could not be retrieved because its PDF link redirected to a department home page. Only the 2018 proceedings abstract was read, so any working-memory interaction is unverified.
- Darabi et al.'s numeric effect size was not available.
- A 2025 *Educational Psychology Review* article that surfaced in a PS-I search (DOI 10.1007/s10648-025-09993-3) failed to load, so its content is unknown. A Quebec research-summary PDF on the meta-analysis returned a 404.
- No study was found that tests problem-first practice after prior lecture instruction for conceptual or transfer outcomes.
- The sibling note `success_rate_and_struggle.md` in this folder may overlap on productive failure; not cross-checked.

## 4. Worked examples available on demand during practice: how often learners consult them, whether they copy, help abuse, and effects on later performance

### Takeaway
When examples are available during problem solving, students consult them often, and weaker learners use them to find something to copy:
- In the original physics protocols, poor solvers made 6.6 example references per problem vs 2.7 for good solvers, and read about 13 lines per reread vs about 1.6–1.8.
- A reanalysis found that most students, good and poor alike, transferred whole lines from the example.
- Better learners use analogy sparingly, for checking or getting past impasses.

Having an analogous solution available helps on equivalent problems but not on structurally different ones. In tutoring systems, help abuse (such as clicking through to the bottom-out hint) is associated with poorer learning at every skill level. Training better help seeking changed behavior but not domain learning. No controlled study was found that isolates the effect of an on-demand worked example on delayed closed-book performance.

### Cited Findings

#### Analogy and copying from examples
- **[Observational protocol study; full text of the technical-report version read]** Chi, Bassok, Lewis, Reimann & Glaser, the self-explanation study (later published in *Cognitive Science*, 1989). Eight students with no college physics studied Newtonian-mechanics worked examples. They were split post hoc into 4 Good and 4 Poor students by problem-solving success.
  - Both groups consulted examples on a similar number of problems (Good 9, Poor 10 of 12 isomorphic problems).
  - Example references per problem: Good 2.7 vs Poor 6.6 (marginal, p < .10).
  - Rereading episodes per problem: Good 0.6 vs Poor 4.1.
  - Lines read per rereading episode: Poor 13.0 vs Good 1.6. The text says 1.6 and the table says 1.8.
  - Copying ("map") episodes, where an equation, label or diagram was copied: Good 1.1 vs Poor 2.1. Checking episodes: Good 1.0 vs Poor 0.2.
  - The authors: Poor students "use the examples as a way to find a solution that can be copied". Good students entered an example with a specific goal.
  - Overall problem-solving success: Good 82% vs Poor 46% (Poor: 62% on isomorphic problems, 30% on chapter problems).
  - The fetch covered about the first 100k of 112k characters.
  — [Source](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA187035.pdf)
- **[Observational reanalysis; secondhand, through a 2007 cognitive-modeling paper by Klenk & Forbus]** VanLehn (1998, *Cognitive Science*) reanalyzed Chi et al.'s protocols (9 subjects, according to the citing paper). Per problem there were about 1.2 initialization events and 4.9 transfer events. Whole-line transfers were about 54% of transfers, checking about 15%, and failed transfers about 8%. VanLehn (p. 364), as quoted there: "most students, both Good and Poor, transferred whole lines from the example to the problem" — [Source](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA470406.pdf)
- **[Observational protocol analysis; abstract read]** VanLehn & Jones (1993, *ICML*), college physics homework. Effective learners generally avoid analogy for the main goals of a problem and use it occasionally for meta-level goals such as checking a solution or getting past an impasse. Ineffective learners use analogy in place of ordinary problem solving, which keeps them from discovering gaps in what they know. N not stated — [Source](https://mlanthology.org/icml/1993/vanlehn1993icml-better)
- **[Four experiments; abstract read]** Reed, Dempster & Ettinger (1985, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition* 11, 106–125): college students in an algebra class were given a worked solution to a distance, mixture or work problem.
  - Experiment 1: studying the solution did not increase correct solutions on either equivalent or similar problems.
  - Experiments 2–3: being allowed to consult the solution while working, or getting a more detailed solution, improved transfer to equivalent problems but not to similar problems that required modifying the solution.
  - Experiment 4: some transfer occurred to similar problems that differed in complexity, but students leaned too heavily on a syntactic strategy of filling in the "slots" of an equation.
  - N not given in the abstract.
  — [Source](https://causeweb.org/cause/node/7737)
- **[Protocol and modeling studies; abstract read]** Pirolli & Recker (1994, *Cognition and Instruction* 12, 235–275), using the CMU Lisp Tutor. Learning gains were associated with:
  - explanations that connect example material to the abstract terms in the text, and that focus on new concepts;
  - planning time, with diminishing returns;
  - monitoring one's own knowledge;
  - reflecting on solutions.
  This is the Pirolli line of work; Pirolli & Anderson (1985) itself was not retrieved — [Source](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/itls_facpub/3)

#### Help seeking and help abuse in tutoring systems
- **[Review; abstract read]** Aleven, Stahl, Schworm, Fischer & Wallace (2003, *Review of Educational Research* 73, 277–320). Learners often do not use on-demand help in interactive learning environments effectively. Effective help seeking is associated with better learning outcomes. Metacognitive skills interact with prior knowledge in deciding when help seeking is appropriate — [Source](https://www.uni-muenster.de/PsyIFP/AEBromme/veroeffentlichung/2003/abstract/RER.htm)
- **[Observational log analysis; full text of the author manuscript read]** Roll, Baker, Aleven & Koedinger, "On the benefits of seeking (and avoiding) help in online problem-solving environments". The file name indicates the *Journal of the Learning Sciences*; the year is not stated in the text read. 38 students at a rural vocational high school used the Geometry Cognitive Tutor for two months. Each action was classified with a help-seeking model, according to the student's estimated skill on that step.
  - Seeking help when the model judged it appropriate was associated with better performance at the next chance to use the same skill: overall odds ratio 1.45, high-skill steps 2.29, medium-skill steps 2.72. On low-skill steps it was not a significant predictor (odds ratio 0.69).
  - Help abuse, which includes clicking through hints to the bottom-out answer, was associated with worse learning at every skill level (odds ratios: high 0.17, medium 0.08, low 0.24).
  - On low-skill steps, attempting without seeking help (labeled "inappropriate attempts") was associated with better later performance (odds ratio 2.20).
  - Frequencies: help abuse 5% of actions (10% on low-skill steps); unaided attempts the model judged inappropriate 12% (28% on low-skill steps); appropriate help seeking 83%.
  — [Source](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/Roll%20HT%20JLS%20clean.pdf)
- **[Project documentation; secondhand account of Roll et al. 2006]** Help Tutor studies with the Geometry Cognitive Tutor:
  - In Study 1, 17% of student actions were classified as help-seeking errors.
  - Background data from Aleven et al. (2006): 30% of actions were rapid consecutive hint requests ("clicking through hints").
  - Help-seeking errors correlated r = −0.42 with learning.
  - In the month after Study 2, students in the help condition read hints longer (12 s vs 8 s).
  - Neither study found domain-learning differences.
  — [Source](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/TheHelpTutor_Roll)
- **[Classroom experiments; abstract read]** Roll, Aleven, McLaren & Koedinger (2011, *Learning and Instruction* 21, 267–280). In Study 1 (58 students), feedback from the Help Tutor improved help-seeking behavior. Study 2 (67 students) added help-seeking instruction and self-assessment support and replicated the effect. The improved help seeking carried over to learning new content in the month after the intervention, without support — [Source](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/improving-students-help-seeking-skills-using-metacognitive-feedba/)
- **[Review; abstract read]** Aleven, Roll, McLaren & Koedinger (2016, *International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education* 26, 205–223). Help Tutor feedback made students use on-demand help more deliberately, even after the feedback stopped, but it did not produce better learning outcomes. The authors no longer consider on-demand, principle-based help during tutored problem solving as important as they once did, though they still see it as useful in some circumstances — [Source](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/help-helps-but-only-so-much-research-on-help-seeking-with-intelli/)

### Inferences
- The copying evidence comes from very small protocol samples (Chi et al., N = 8) and correlational log analyses. It shows that weaker learners consult and copy more. It cannot show that consulting causes poorer learning.
- In Reed et al. Experiments 2–3, having the example available improved performance on near-identical problems during the session. That is performance with the example present, not retention or transfer once it is removed.
- Roll et al. (2014) suggests that the value of consulting help depends on the learner's current skill on the step. On low-skill steps, attempting first was associated with better later performance than seeking help. Help abuse was associated with worse learning at every level.
- Estimates of misuse vary with the definition: help abuse was 5% of actions in Roll 2014, help-seeking errors 17% in Roll 2006, and rapid consecutive hint requests 30% in Aleven 2006.
- None of the retrieved studies tested a policy of showing the full worked solution only after the learner gives up.

### Gaps
- No RCT was found that compares on-demand worked examples with none during practice and measures delayed closed-book performance.
- LeFevre & Dixon (1986, *Cognition and Instruction*) could not be retrieved; searches returned unrelated technical-writing pages. The commonly cited claim that learners follow an example when it conflicts with written instructions remains unverified here.
- Pirolli & Anderson (1985) was not retrieved, so the claim that novices relied heavily on examples by analogy when writing their first recursive functions is unverified here.
- VanLehn (1998) was read only secondhand. The citing paper reports 9 subjects while the Chi report describes 8 students; the discrepancy is unresolved.
- No data were found on how often college math students consult optional worked examples or textbook material in a practice app.

## 5. Open-book vs closed-book practice tests and exams: delayed closed-book retention and study behavior

### Takeaway
Open-book practice tests produce higher scores during practice. In lab studies with prose passages and feedback, they also produced delayed closed-book retention similar to closed-book practice tests. Two systematic reviews, however, find the overall effect of format on learning mixed: closed-book assessments produce less forgetting, and preparation favors closed-book (37 studies). Classroom comparisons tend to favor closed-book for later retention: an introductory-biology final (about 74 vs 63) and a Dutch master's-course cohort study. Direct math evidence is sparse: one introductory-statistics course, reported only through the review.

### Cited Findings

#### Lab experiments
- **[Two experiments; abstract read]** Agarwal, Karpicke, Kang, Roediger & McDermott (2008, *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 22, 861–876). Subjects studied prose passages, then restudied them or took an open-book or closed-book test.
  - Tests with feedback improved long-term retention more than restudying or testing without feedback.
  - Open-book testing produced better initial performance, but the advantage did not last: open-book and closed-book testing gave equivalent retention on the delayed test.
  - Subjects predicted that restudying would help more than testing, the opposite of the results.
  - N and the delay were not on the page.
  — [Source](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/examining-the-testing-effect-with-open-and-closed-book-tests/)
- **[Two experiments; abstract read]** Agarwal & Roediger (2011, *Memory* 19, 836–852), GRE reading passages.
  - Open-book initial tests produced better performance than closed-book.
  - Experiment 1: retention on a closed-book test two days later was similar for both formats.
  - Experiment 2: participants who expected an open-book test studied for less time and scored lower on the delayed closed-book test, on both comprehension and transfer questions.
  — [Source](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/expectancy-of-an-open-book-test-decreases-performance-on-a-delaye/)
- **[Systematic review describing the same study; full text read]** Durning et al., reporting on Agarwal & Roediger: expecting a closed-book test improved later performance by about 10%. Delayed transfer scores were similar across formats (for example, 0.40 closed-book vs 0.42 open-book with feedback) — [Source](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/blogs.cornell.edu/dist/b/8179/files/2020/07/Durning-2016-Comparing-Open-Book-and-Closed-Book-Examinations.pdf)

#### Systematic reviews
- **[Systematic review; full text read]** Durning et al. (2016, *Academic Medicine* 91, 583–599).
  - Search: MEDLINE, Embase, ERIC and PsycINFO through June 2013; 4,192 records screened, 37 studies included.
  - Samples: 65% undergraduates, 22% medical students, 2 high-school samples. Fields included psychology, biology, statistics (Block; Gharib et al.), engineering and economics. 92% were single-institution studies, most exams were medium-stakes, and 70% of studies reported no prior open-book experience or did not mention it.
  - Preparation: overall favored closed-book. Some studies found students reported preparing more for closed-book exams (for example, Boniface; Heijne-Penninga 2010), and no study found more preparation for open-book. In Moore & Jensen, class attendance dropped before open-book exams for some students. In Broyles et al., study tactics often did not change.
  - Performance: most studies found no difference or favored closed-book.
    - Moore & Jensen (introductory biology): closed-book final about 74 vs 63.
    - Heijne-Penninga 2008 (570 medical students): closed-book scores higher on 9 of 14 exams.
    - Exception: Broyles et al. found a small open-book advantage (88.2 vs 84).
    - Block (2012, statistics) and Heijne-Penninga (2011) found worse later closed-book performance after students had prepared for open-book exams.
    - Open-book exams took about 10–60% longer to complete.
  - Retention: both formats produced testing effects. Moore & Jensen found the closed-book testing effect larger. Heijne-Penninga 2013 found higher core-knowledge retention in an open-book-assessed problem-based curriculum on 4 of 8 progress tests.
  - Anxiety: mixed.
    - Baillie & Toohey: 45% of students were as stressed by open-book exams as by closed-book.
    - Dickson & Miller: 79% expected a cheat sheet to lower anxiety, but only 41% said it did.
    - Broyles et al.: 80% reported less anxiety with open-book.
  - Conclusion: "There does not appear to be sufficient evidence for relying solely on OBE or CBE formats."
  - Limitations: mostly single-institution studies, mostly before widespread internet use, few theoretical frameworks. The abstract counts 24 testing-effect studies while the results section counts 13.
  — [Source](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/blogs.cornell.edu/dist/b/8179/files/2020/07/Durning-2016-Comparing-Open-Book-and-Closed-Book-Examinations.pdf)
- **[Systematic review; abstract read]** Permzadian & Cho (2025, *Teaching in Higher Education* 30, 880–897). The overall effect of assessment format on learning is mixed. Two findings are robust: closed-book assessments produce a lower rate of forgetting (the change between an initial and a final test) than open-book, and students prefer open-book. Overlap between test items and study material is suggested as a possible moderator. The number of studies was not on the page — [Source](https://scholarsmine.mst.edu/psysci_facwork/356)

#### Classroom studies
- **[Quasi-experiment across four cohorts; full text read]** Spiegel & Nivette (2024, *European Journal of Psychology of Education* 39, 3943–3962), social science at Utrecht University.
  - Courses: a bachelor course (588 enrolled; 39–50 survey respondents per cohort) and a master course (215 enrolled; 22–34 respondents).
  - Formats: in-class, mostly closed-book exams vs take-home, open-book, time-limited exams.
  - Retention was measured 4–6 months after the course with a 10-item multiple-choice quiz.
  - Master course: the take-home cohorts scored lower on retention than the first in-class cohort, with the largest gaps in cohorts 3 and 4. One take-home cohort also had lower exam grades (b = −0.65).
  - Bachelor course: no retention differences.
  - Exam scores predicted retention in both courses (b = 0.62 bachelor, 0.48 master).
  - Caveats: respondents had higher grades than the cohort averages, suggesting selection bias, and study behavior was not measured.
  — [Source](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10212-024-00821-w)
- **[Classroom study; secondhand, a radio interview transcript with the researchers]** Gharib, Phillips & Mathew (2012).
  - Design: introductory psychology took open-book, cheat-sheet and closed-book exams across a semester; statistics sections used only the cheat-sheet and open-book formats. A pop quiz followed each exam by about two weeks.
  - Retention-quiz scores were "pretty much exactly the same" across formats.
  - The exam-grade gain was small ("might be a half-a-letter grade"), and each student's scores were highly correlated across formats.
  - How detailed a student's cheat sheet was had "essentially no relationship" with exam scores or retention.
  - Students studied slightly more for cheat-sheet exams.
  - N was not reported.
  — [Source](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/167180405). Durning et al. report that in this study self-reported study time did not differ, although students believed they would study more for a closed-book exam — [Source](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/blogs.cornell.edu/dist/b/8179/files/2020/07/Durning-2016-Comparing-Open-Book-and-Closed-Book-Examinations.pdf). The two accounts may be consistent (a slight, non-significant difference) but are worded differently.

### Inferences
- The lab finding that open-book and closed-book practice tests give equivalent delayed retention (Agarwal 2008; Agarwal & Roediger 2011, Experiment 1) comes from prose reading comprehension with feedback, not math problem solving.
- Much of the observed open-book disadvantage appears to work through preparation: less study time when an open-book test is expected, lower attendance, and less preparation reported. It does not appear to come from the act of consulting during the test.
- The two reviews partly conflict with the lab results. Closed-book assessment showed less forgetting (Permzadian & Cho; Moore & Jensen's larger closed-book testing effect), while the lab work found delayed retention equivalent (Agarwal 2008).
- No retrieved source studies open-book practice where the open resource is a textbook containing worked examples of the same procedure. The Reed et al. (1985) analogy results (Question 4) are the closest evidence.

### Gaps
- No open-book vs closed-book study in calculus or precalculus was found. Block (2012, *PRIMUS*, introductory statistics) was seen only through Durning et al.; its design, N and effect sizes were not read.
- The Gharib et al. (2012) article was not read; its DOI surfaced only through a third-party library proxy link, which was not used. Its details are secondhand.
- A PubMed Central article that surfaced in an open-book search (PMC6428738) was blocked by a reCAPTCHA check. It was not accessed and its identity is unconfirmed.
- Moore & Jensen (2007), Boniface (1985), Heijne-Penninga et al. and other primary studies were seen only through Durning et al.
- N and the retention delay for Agarwal et al. (2008) were not on the abstract page.
- The Durning search ends in June 2013. The study count in Permzadian & Cho (2025) is unknown.

## 6. Test expectancy: does expecting a closed-book or timed test change how students practice and what they retain?

### Takeaway
Yes, for the expected format. Over repeated cycles, learners shift to strategies suited to the format they expect and do best on that format (Finley & Benjamin; a crossover pattern). Expecting an open-book test reduced study time and lowered delayed closed-book comprehension and transfer (Agarwal & Roediger 2011; about 10% according to the Durning review). A semester of higher-order quizzes raised both low- and high-level final-exam performance in introductory biology (Jensen et al. 2014, quasi-experiment). No study was found on how expecting a timed test affects study behavior or retention.

### Cited Findings
- **[Three experiments; thesis abstract read]** Finley (2010, master's thesis, University of Illinois), published as Finley & Benjamin (2012, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*) under the same title.
  - Undergraduates studied word-pair lists over four study–test cycles while expecting either cued recall or free recall.
  - On a final cycle with the expected or the unexpected format, participants did better on whichever format they had expected (a crossover). The authors interpret this as qualitatively different encoding strategies, not just more or less effort.
  - Experiment 2: participants adjusted their metacognitive monitoring across cycles.
  - Experiment 3: participants adjusted how they allocated study time across cycles.
  — [Source](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/16275)
- **[Secondhand: blog summary]**
  - Finley & Benjamin (2012): students expecting cued recall used cue–target association strategies; those expecting free recall focused on the target words.
  - Szpunar, McDermott & Roediger (2007): expecting a final cumulative test improved recall of word lists.
  - McDaniel, Blischak & Challis (1994): expecting either a multiple-choice or an essay test improved performance on prose over expecting no test.
  - Colak (2015): students studying psychology textbook chapters did better when they got the test format they expected, both immediately and two weeks later.
  - Storm, Hickman & Bjork (2016): experiencing the test, not just being told about it, led to the most adoption of effective strategies.
  — [Source](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2018/5/15-1)
- **[Experiment; abstract read]** Agarwal & Roediger (2011), Experiment 2: participants expecting an open-book test studied for less time and scored lower on a delayed closed-book test, on comprehension and transfer questions — [Source](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/expectancy-of-an-open-book-test-decreases-performance-on-a-delaye/). Durning et al. summarize this as students studying less when they expect an open-book test, and expecting a closed-book test improving later performance by about 10% — [Source](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/blogs.cornell.edu/dist/b/8179/files/2020/07/Durning-2016-Comparing-Open-Book-and-Closed-Book-Examinations.pdf)
- **[Quasi-experiment, two course sections; abstract read]** Jensen, McDaniel, Woodard & Kummer (2014, *Educational Psychology Review* 26, 307–329), introductory biology.
  - Both sections were taught with inquiry-based instruction. One section got low-level (memory) quiz and exam questions all semester; the other got high-level questions (application, analysis, evaluation).
  - Both took the same final exam, with 20 low-level and 21 high-level items.
  - The high-level section outperformed the other on both low-level and high-level items. The statistics reported were F(1, 167) with partial η² between .044 and .072; the page did not show which item type each value belongs to.
  — [Source](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-013-9248-9)

### Inferences
- Most test-expectancy experiments use verbal materials (word pairs, prose, textbook chapters). Jensen et al. is a STEM course, but its questions were conceptual biology items, not computational problem solving. None of the retrieved studies is about math problem-solving practice.
- The mechanisms the studies document are changes in encoding strategy, study time and metacognitive monitoring (Finley & Benjamin), and reduced study when an open-book test is expected (Agarwal & Roediger). They suggest expectancy acts through how students prepare, consistent with the preparation findings in Question 5.
- Finley & Benjamin and Storm et al. suggest that experiencing the test format, more than being told about it, drives strategy change.

### Gaps
- No study was found on how expecting a timed test changes practice or retention. The sibling note `timed_practice.md` in this folder may cover time pressure; not cross-checked.
- The Lundeberg & Fox (1991) meta-analysis on classroom test expectancy was seen only as a citation in a blog post; no design, k or effect sizes were retrieved.
- The published Finley & Benjamin (2012) article itself was not read; its volume and pages were not verified.
- No study was found on whether knowing that a later closed-book certification sitting is coming changes how students use open-book practice.
