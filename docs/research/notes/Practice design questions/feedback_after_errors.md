# What students get after a wrong answer in math practice: feedback type, errors, timing, self-explanation, incubation, AI help

*Compiled 2026-11-02. Facts and evidence strength only; no design recommendations.*

**Evidence labels used on every finding**
- **[STRONG]** peer-reviewed meta-analysis, or large randomized trial; numbers read from the source named.
- **[MODERATE]** single randomized experiment of moderate N, quasi-experiment with credible identification, a recognized narrative review, or a strong source read only at abstract level.
- **[WEAK]** observational/correlational, very small N, non-math or very different population, unreviewed preprint/working paper, or secondhand (another paper's or a blog's description).
- **[UNVERIFIED]** primary source not retrieved; claim is secondhand.

**Reading caveat.** All pages were read through a fetch tool that returns model-written extractions of the page text, so numbers are "as extracted"; where I noticed an inconsistency I flag it. Several publisher pages (PMC, SAGE, Annual Reviews, ScienceDirect/Elsevier linkinghub) were blocked or empty, so some sources are abstract-level only. Populations are given for each study because most are not college math.

## 1. Feedback types compared: verification only ("wrong"), answer-until-correct / multiple-try, knowledge of correct response, worked solution, elaborated feedback

### Takeaway
Syntheses rank correctness-only feedback (KR) lowest (d = 0.05 in computer-based learning), correct-response feedback (KCR) at 0.32, and explanation/elaborated feedback at 0.49; in the largest feedback meta-analysis, "high-information" feedback (d = 0.99) far outranks corrective feedback (0.46). Evidence on "try again" feedback is thin and mixed: gains appear mostly where tries were capped (e.g., three) and/or hints were attached, and the one large-scale observational look at very many tries is confounded. Prior knowledge can reverse the sign of simple right/wrong feedback in at least one math study (grades 2–3).

### Cited Findings

**1a. Meta-analyses and reviews of feedback overall**
- **[STRONG; meta-analysis, computer-based learning, mixed subjects/levels]** Van der Kleij, Feskens & Eggen (2015, *Review of Educational Research* 85(4)): 40 studies, 70 effect sizes "ranged from −0.78 to 2.29". Effect sizes by feedback type: elaborated feedback (EF, e.g., an explanation) 0.49; knowledge of correct response (KCR) 0.32; knowledge of results (KR, correctness only) 0.05. EF was more effective than KR and KCR for higher-order learning outcomes. Mathematics showed larger effect sizes than social sciences, science and languages. Per the extraction, primary-school and high-school level were associated with smaller effect sizes (reference category not retrieved). Pooled overall effect and participant total were not on the page read. — [ACU repository abstract page](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/86y84/effects-of-feedback-in-a-computer-based-learning-environment-on-students-learning-outcomes-a-meta-analysis)
- **[STRONG; meta-analysis, all educational feedback, mixed populations]** Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020, *Frontiers in Psychology* 10:3087): 435 studies, k = 994 effect sizes, N > 61,000 ("about 61,000 subjects"). All effects, random-effects: d = 0.55, 95% CI 0.48–0.62; 17% of effects negative; I² = 86.47%. After removing 35 outliers (3.5%): d = 0.48 (CI 0.44–0.51), I² = 83.40%. Fixed-effect model: d = 0.41 (CI 0.40–0.43). — [Full text PDF (Augsburg repository)](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/71009/fpsyg-10-03087.pdf); canonical page: [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full)
  - By outcome type (Table 3): cognitive k = 597, d = 0.51 [0.46, 0.55]; motor skills k = 19, d = 0.63 [0.34, 0.92]; motivational k = 109, d = 0.33 [0.23, 0.42]; behavioral k = 30, d = 0.48 [−0.09, 1.06].
  - By feedback type (Table 3): reinforcement or punishment k = 39, d = 0.24 [0.06, 0.43]; corrective feedback k = 238, d = 0.46 [0.39, 0.55]; high-information feedback k = 42, d = 0.99 [0.82, 1.15]. "High-information" is coded as task, process, and sometimes self-regulation information.
  - Design dependence: controlled designs k = 713, d = 0.42 [0.37, 0.46]; pre-post designs k = 244, d = 0.63 [0.56, 0.69]; heterogeneity is high in every subgroup (I² 50.0% to 89.5%). Egger's test: z = 9.52, p < 0.001 (asymmetry attributed to journal articles; dissertations z = 1.03, p = 0.30). 21% of motivational-outcome effects were negative, and 86% of the interventions producing them were uninformative (rewards/punishment).
  - Not coded/reported in this paper: timing (immediate vs delayed), task difficulty, subject (math), student level, prior knowledge, computer vs non-computer (channel moderator non-significant, QB = 5.12).
- **[STRONG; meta-analysis, mostly non-math, lab and field]** Kluger & DeNisi (1996, *Psychological Bulletin*): 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations; feedback interventions (FIs) improved performance on average (d = .41); "over 1/3 of the FIs decreased performance"; "FI effectiveness decreases as attention moves up the hierarchy closer to the self and away from the task." Moderated by task characteristics "still poorly understood". Note: Wisniewski et al. cite K&DN's average as 0.38 rather than .41 (discrepancy in secondary quoting). — [Hebrew Univ. abstract page](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-feedback-interventions-on-performance-a-historical/)
- **[MODERATE; narrative review]** Shute (2008, *Review of Educational Research*), as extracted: lists feedback forms (verification; correct response; try again = "one or more attempts"; error flagging = "highlights errors in a solution, without giving correct answer"; elaborated; attribute isolation; response-contingent; hints/cues/prompts). Statements: verification "does not have much utility for learning" (p. 175); Bangert-Drowns et al. found correct-response feedback had higher effect sizes than verification (p. 171); "Overall, Bangert-Drowns et al.'s (1991) meta-analysis found generally weak effects of feedback on achievement" and "feedback can promote learning if it is received mindfully" (p. 171); response-specific feedback enhances achievement "more than other types of feedback, such as simple verification or 'answer until correct'" (p. 159); Clariana (1990): "low-ability students benefit from receipt of correct response feedback more than from try again feedback" (p. 166); Hanna (1976): "verification feedback condition produced the highest scores for high-ability students" while elaborated scored highest for low-ability students (p. 166); Kulhavy et al. (1985): "complexity of feedback was inversely related to both ability to correct errors and learning efficiency" (p. 159); hints "can also be abused" (Table 4, p. 179); "directive feedback may be more helpful for novices" (p. 163). Checklist items: "Provide feedback after learners have attempted a solution"; "Do not let learners see answers before trying to solve a problem on their own" (Table 2, p. 178), tied to Bangert-Drowns' finding that "uncontrolled presearch availability of answers" lowered effect sizes (p. 171). — [Shute 2008 PDF (author site)](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)
- **[WEAK–MODERATE; secondhand summary by a test-prep company's literature review]** Bangert-Drowns et al. (1991, test-like events): feedback "raises scores by about one fourth of a standard deviation (ES = 0.26, SE = 0.06)" (p. 224); KCR superior to KR; elaboration about as effective as KCR. Jaehnig & Miller (2007) as summarized: "KR alone shows almost no effect on achievement"; elaboration superior only when combined with KR or KCR; answer-until-correct (AUC) "no better than other types of feedback." — [ATI literature review PDF](https://www.ati-online.com:443/pdfs/researchK12/ABriefReviewLiteratureonTestFeedback.pdf)

**1b. Verification-only ("right/wrong") feedback**
- **[MODERATE; single within-subject experiment, college undergraduates, general-knowledge multiple-choice, not math]** Marsh, Lozito, Umanath, Bjork & Bjork (2012, *Memory*): 48 Duke undergraduates (29 in the error-correction analysis). Proportion of initially-wrong items later answered correctly (averaged over immediate and 48-hour tests): no feedback .08; verification feedback .28 (.36 with two alternatives, .31 with three, .17 with four); answer feedback .53. With two alternatives (where the right answer is inferable from "wrong") verification still corrected fewer errors than answer feedback (.36 vs .52): "even when verification feedback allowed successful inference of the correct answer, it was less effective than receiving the answer." Final short-answer test, previously tested items: no feedback .35 (immediate)/.37 (delayed); verification .58/.44 (M = .51); answer .70/.53 (M = .61). Maintaining already-correct answers: no feedback M = .61; verification and answer feedback each M ≥ .75, "about equally". Whether participants re-answered after verification (second try) had no effect on later short-answer performance (F(1, 46) = 1.06). — [Marsh et al. 2012 PDF](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Marsh_Lozito_Umanath_Bjork_Bjork2012.pdf)
- **[MODERATE; randomized, 1-on-1 tutoring, 2nd–3rd graders, math equivalence]** Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson, "Feedback both helps and hinders learning: the causal role of prior knowledge" (*J. Educational Psychology*; PDF header read as 2015, 107(2); my recollection of the print citation is 2016, 108(1), not verified). Exp. 1: N = 108 (M age 8.4); 2 (induced strategy knowledge: yes/no) × 2 (right-wrong verification feedback only: present/absent). Procedural knowledge, feedback × knowledge interaction F(1, 93) = 16.80, p < .001, η²p = .15. Children with no strategy knowledge: feedback 50% (SE 6%) vs no feedback 18% (SE 7%), η²p = .12. Children with induced strategy knowledge: feedback 62% (SE 6%) vs no feedback 81% (SE 6%), p = .03, η²p = .05. Conceptual knowledge: interaction F(1, 93) = 0.60, p = .44 (no reliable effect). Exp. 2 (N = 101; all children given strategy instruction): vs no feedback, immediate correct-answer feedback and summative correct-answer feedback each lowered the odds of 100% on procedural items (immediate OR = 0.31, p = .03; summative OR = 0.13, p = .001) and lowered conceptual knowledge (53% no feedback vs 41% immediate and 40% summative; η²p = .05 and .06). Authors: "feedback can have negative, not just neutral, effects for learners with some prior domain knowledge"; hypothesized mechanisms: strengthening incorrect strategies, redundant information overloading working memory, ego threat (the self-assessment and difficulty data did not support ego-threat or load). Limitations stated: 1-on-1 setting, immediate posttest only (2-week retention test completed by only 58% in Exp. 1 and not reported), one domain. — [Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson PDF](https://news.vanderbilt.edu/files/FyfeRittleJohnson2015_JEP.pdf)
- **[MODERATE; narrative review]** Zhang & Fiorella (2022, *Educational Psychologist*, "An integrated model of learning from errors"): memory research "has found that feedback including correct answers helps more than no feedback or feedback that only says whether a response was right" (extraction paraphrase of the claim "external feedback that included correct answers enhanced learning from errors"). — [NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10429504)

**1c. Answer-until-correct / multiple-try feedback (MTF)**
- **[MODERATE; review, abstract-level]** Clariana & Koul (2005), "Multiple-try feedback and higher-order learning outcomes": MTF ("try again" on error) "may be slightly superior" to other common feedback forms for higher-order posttest outcomes; for lower-order, verbatim outcomes the other forms "will be equivalent to or superior to MTF." No numbers in the abstract. (A 2023 paper reports, secondhand, an effect size of 0.11 for MTF on higher-order outcomes from this review: [WEAK/UNVERIFIED].) — [learntechlib abstract](https://learntechlib.org/p/63335)
- **[UNVERIFIED; primary abstract not retrieved]** Attali (2015, *Computers & Education* 86:260–267), "Effects of multiple-try feedback and question type during mathematics problem solving on performance in similar problems". Publisher/ETS pages gave metadata only (keywords: multiple-try feedback, mathematics problem solving, transfer, item types). Two secondhand descriptions: (i) Schwerter et al. (Introduction): participants solved math problems in multiple-choice and open-ended formats; conditions were MTF with and without hints, KCR, and no feedback; 15 items; MTF produced higher learning gains than KCR; hints+MTF outperformed MTF without hints; open-ended outperformed multiple-choice; attributed to MTF eliciting "more attentive and effortful problem solving". (ii) The same paper's Discussion says Attali found "no effect" during the learning phase but "significant differences in the exam immediately following" (internally inconsistent with (i) as extracted). (iii) Cubillos et al. say Attali's MTC and MTH variants produced larger improvements than their own MTF and describe Attali's hints as "partial pieces of information … without providing the result explicitly". Sample size, population and effect sizes not obtained. — [ETS metadata page](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/article/2015/jwdp.html); [Schwerter et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-022-10105-z); [Cubillos et al.](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/13/11/1119)
- **[MODERATE; randomized within-student, college statistics (87 sociology undergraduates, Germany), 12 weekly e-learning sessions]** Schwerter et al. (2022, *Educational Technology Research and Development*; first author per repository listing): multiple-try feedback in which students got knowledge of the correct response after three failed attempts (MCR) vs the same plus a hint after the first wrong answer (MCR+H); conditions alternated by session, randomized per individual; no no-feedback group "for ethical reasons". MCR+H vs MCR: learning-phase proportion correct "3.7%" higher (4.7% for effective proportion correct); students "14.3% less likely to make errors"; immediate test: no significant increase; delayed test one week later: MCR+H students "learned more than the MCR group" (no coefficient given); final exam grades: "small and insignificant". Mean proportion correct in sessions 58%. — [Schwerter et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-022-10105-z)
- **[MODERATE–WEAK; randomized, N = 73, 3rd–4th graders in Chile, 55-minute game session, 9-point math test]** Cubillos, Roncagliolo & Cabrera-Paniagua (2023, *Education Sciences* 13(11):1119): MTF (three attempts, knowledge of result between tries, correct answer never shown) n = 36 vs KCR (single try, then correct answer shown) n = 37. - **[WEAK; observational, calculus-based physics, large enrollment (>500 per course), online free-response homework]** arXiv paper "An Empirical Study of the Effect of Granting Multiple Tries for Online Homework" (author not captured in retrieval): four semesters with different maximum tries (10, 12, 20, 99); instructors/textbooks also varied; feedback was immediate correctness only, no penalty for tries; exam performance used only to split high/low achievers, not compared across conditions; no significance tests reported. - **[MODERATE; randomized, N = 2,445, online practice-test items in mathematics; population/grade not stated on page; abstract only, no effect sizes]** Attali & van der Kleij (2017, *Computers & Education* 110:154–169): participants answered pairs of isomorphic items in consecutive web-based practice tests; conditions: KCR alone vs KCR + elaborated feedback (EF, "extra explanations of the correct answer"); multiple-choice vs constructed response; immediate vs delayed. - **[WEAK; secondhand blog summary of Barbieri et al. 2023, *Educational Psychology Review*]** Meta-analysis of the worked-examples effect on mathematics performance: 43 studies (mostly middle- and high-school algebra/geometry); g = 0.48; effect holds "whether used for practice or initial skill acquisition"; the summary says contrasting correct with incorrect examples did not benefit students and that pairing examples with self-explanation prompts "may not be a fruitful design modification" (self-explanation "reduced learning on average"). **1e. Elaborated feedback**
- See 1a (EF d = 0.49; more effective than KR/KCR for higher-order outcomes) and 1d (Attali & van der Kleij: EF > KCR only after incorrect first responses). Shute: Kulhavy et al. (1985) found feedback complexity inversely related to ability to correct errors and learning efficiency; ATI summary: Jaehnig & Miller and Pridemore & Klein found insufficient evidence to recommend KCR over elaboration. — [Shute 2008](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf); [ATI review](https://www.ati-online.com:443/pdfs/researchK12/ABriefReviewLiteratureonTestFeedback.pdf)

### Inferences
- In the syntheses retrieved, "KR" (correctness only) carries essentially no average benefit (d = 0.05; "almost no effect"), but those KR conditions are almost always a single response without retry, so the numbers do not isolate "wrong + unlimited retries with no information."
- The only math-specific "try again" evidence retrieved is short-term, small, and mostly includes a cap on tries and/or hints (Attali via secondhand; Schwerter: three tries then answer; Cubillos: three tries, grades 3–4). No retrieved study tests unlimited retries with zero hints and a worked solution only on giving up.
- Marsh et al. is the cleanest isolation of "verification only": it beats nothing (.28 vs .08 errors corrected) and trails the answer (.53), but it is multiple-choice general knowledge in undergraduates.
- The sign of simple feedback depends on learner prior knowledge in at least one math study (Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson), but children were grades 2–3 on one task; Shute's review (Hanna 1976; Clariana 1990) points to similar ability-dependence in older samples, secondhand.
- All meta-analytic averages hide very large heterogeneity (I² 83–86%; 17% to over one-third of effects negative), and Wisniewski's strongest category ("high-information", d = 0.99) is a coded category of task/process information, not a measured comparison against "worked solution" specifically.

### Gaps
- Attali (2015) primary abstract, N, population and effect sizes could not be retrieved (ScienceDirect/linkinghub returned empty; ETS page metadata only); secondhand accounts conflict in part.
- Bangert-Drowns et al. (1991), Kulik & Kulik (1988), Jaehnig & Miller (2007) not read at the primary level; only secondary summaries.
- Van der Kleij et al. (2015): full text (pooled effect, math-specific effect size, education-level contrasts) not retrieved; only abstract-level.
- No college-level math study comparing verification-only + unlimited retries vs. answer/worked solution on delayed retention was found.
- Wisniewski et al. report no math, level, prior-knowledge or timing moderator.

## 2. Learning from errors: Metcalfe (2017), hypercorrection, error generation followed by correction, persistence of uncorrected errors

### Takeaway
Reviews agree that producing an error helps only when followed by corrective information; errors made with high confidence are corrected more readily than low-confidence errors. Without feedback, error correction on a later test is rare (.08 in one experiment). No retrieved study tests "wrong" alone followed by retries as a route to correcting math errors.

### Cited Findings
- **[MODERATE; review, abstract-level via ERIC]** Metcalfe (2017), "Learning from Errors", *Annual Review of Psychology* 68:465–489 (ERIC ED574569). Short exact quotes: "errorful learning followed by corrective feedback is beneficial to learning"; "Errors committed with high confidence are corrected more readily than low-confidence errors." Paraphrase of abstract: error avoidance common in American classrooms but lab studies suggest it may be counterproductive for neurologically typical students; corrective feedback that analyzes the reasoning behind the mistake is essential; errors give teachers information on misconceptions; letting students make and correct errors in low-stakes settings may help high-stakes performance. The full text was not retrieved. — [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED574569)
- **[MODERATE; five lab experiments, N = 24–40 each, adults; question domain not retrieved]** Metcalfe & Eich (2019, *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications* 4:4): "In all five experiments, participants hypercorrected when they had made a mistake" (corrective feedback better remembered when the error was high-confidence): γ = .29 (Exp. 1, N = 29), .27 (Exp. 2, N = 29), .26 (Exp. 3, N = 24), .32 (Exp. 4, N = 40), .39 (Exp. 5, 24 analyzed), all p < .001 except Exp. 3 p = .008. No hypercorrection when false feedback followed an initially correct answer; but people hypercorrected to false feedback that followed their own errors in Exp. 3–5 (γ = .40, .12, .19): "people are vulnerable to false information when they have made a high-confidence error." Delayed (Phase 3) accuracy was .42 after errors followed by true feedback vs .87 for correct answers followed by false feedback (Exp. 4); in Exp. 5 the corresponding values were .63 (errors followed by true feedback) vs .99 (correct answers followed by false feedback). The paper has no no-feedback condition and no condition isolating error generation, so it cannot say whether errors persist without correction or whether generating errors helps. — [NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10309371)
- **[MODERATE; narrative review]** Zhang & Fiorella (2022, *Educational Psychologist*): "external feedback is necessary to initiate learning from errors"; "internal feedback is the proximate cause of learning from errors"; students more likely to correct high-confidence than low-confidence errors (citing Butterfield & Metcalfe 2006); correct-answer feedback on a pretest may hinder learning if students then do not attend to later materials (citing Sana et al., 2021); immediate feedback may beat delayed for short-term error correction but the advantage may fade on delayed tests; students did better on pretested material even though pretest answers were almost always wrong; self-testing after reading reference information "even without any feedback" is cited (Metcalfe & Miele 2014) as a way to guard against errors returning. No effect sizes in the text read. — [NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10429504)
- **[MODERATE]** Persistence without correction: Marsh et al. (2012) found that, for items initially answered wrongly, later correct responding was .08 with no feedback vs .28 verification-only vs .53 answer feedback (details in 1b). — [Marsh et al.](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Marsh_Lozito_Umanath_Bjork_Bjork2012.pdf)
- **[MODERATE–WEAK; pre-registered experiments, high-school-level math problems, population/N not on page read]** Kumar, Rothschild, Goldstein & Hofman, "Math Education with Large Language Models: Peril or Promise?": Experiment 1: LLM-based explanations improved learning compared with seeing only correct answers; "benefits were largest for those who attempted problems on their own first." Experiment 2: incorrect LLM explanations (arithmetic errors and wrong final answers) produced learning gains between those from seeing only correct answers and from correct explanations. No effect sizes on the page. — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4641653)
- **[WEAK; very small N]** Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher (1994): "roughly one fourth of self-explanations were incorrect"; excluding them did not change the pattern; authors propose (untested) that incorrect self-explanations may conflict with later sentences and allow self-repair. (Details in section 4.) — [Chi et al. 1994 PDF](https://pcl.sitehost.iu.edu/rgoldsto/courses/cogscilearning/chiselfexplanation.pdf)
- **[MODERATE; mechanism hypothesis]** Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson list "strengthening of incorrect strategies" as one hypothesized reason feedback hurt children with some strategy knowledge, and report that for children with no strategy knowledge verification feedback reduced perseveration (repeating an unsuccessful approach) and raised correct strategy use (30% vs 9%, F = 7.87, p = .01; authors also report more correct-strategy generation). — [Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson](https://news.vanderbilt.edu/files/FyfeRittleJohnson2015_JEP.pdf)

### Inferences
- Both reviews retrieved (Metcalfe 2017; Zhang & Fiorella 2022) frame corrective information as a precondition for error-based learning; the retrieved empirical datum on uncorrected errors (.08) is consistent, but it is from a non-math lab task.
- Hypercorrection means correction depends on the learner's confidence in the wrong answer, and also exposes learners to accepting wrong "corrections" after confident errors (Metcalfe & Eich), which links the accuracy of whatever correction is shown to the benefit.
- Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson's finding that verification feedback reduced perseveration in novices, versus hurting those with some knowledge, shows that the same "wrong" signal can act differently on someone with no strategy versus someone with a partially correct strategy.

### Gaps
- Metcalfe (2017) full text not retrieved (Annual Reviews page failed; Wesleyan PDF disallowed by robots.txt). Butterfield & Metcalfe (2001), Butler, Fazio & Marsh (2011), Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009), Butler & Roediger (2008) not retrieved.
- No math-specific hypercorrection study retrieved; no study on whether math errors persist without correction in college practice.
- Productive-failure meta-analysis (Sinha & Kapur 2021, *Review of Educational Research*) could not be read (SAGE fetch failed), so nothing is reported on generation-before-instruction in math.

## 3. Delayed vs immediate feedback for problem solving and retention

### Takeaway
No consistent main effect of timing. Reviews say immediate feedback helps short-run/procedural performance and difficult tasks and low achievers, while delayed feedback may help transfer; in an engineering-course field experiment, feedback delayed one week gave better transfer to new problems even though students preferred immediate feedback. In grade-2 math, answers shown after each problem scored descriptively higher than answers shown after all 12 (each vs no feedback).

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG; meta-analysis, computer-based feedback]** Van der Kleij et al. (2015): per the abstract extraction, delayed feedback timing "negatively affected" effect sizes; "immediate feedback appeared more effective for lower-order learning and delayed feedback for higher-order learning, but no significant interaction was found." — [ACU abstract](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/86y84/effects-of-feedback-in-a-computer-based-learning-environment-on-students-learning-outcomes-a-meta-analysis)
- **[MODERATE; narrative review]** Shute (2008): "no consistent main effect of timing" (p. 163); immediate feedback may be more efficient "particularly in the short run and for procedural skills," delayed "may be superior for promoting transfer of learning" (p. 165); "if the task is difficult, then immediate feedback is beneficial, but if the task is easy, then delayed feedback may be preferable" (p. 165); "low-achieving students may benefit from immediate feedback, whereas high-achieving students may prefer or benefit from delayed feedback" (p. 166); Table 4: "For low-achieving learners, use immediate feedback"; "For retention of procedural or conceptual knowledge, use immediate feedback"; "Do not interrupt learner with feedback if the learner is actively engaged"; Kulik & Kulik (1988) cited only as "many field studies demonstrate the value of immediate feedback" (pp. 165–166). — [Shute 2008](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)
- **[MODERATE; two field experiments, upper-level college engineering course; N not on page read; abstract only]** Mullet, Butler, Verdin, von Borries & Marsh (2014, *J. Applied Research in Memory and Cognition*): weekly homework on complex engineering concepts; feedback given immediately after the assignment deadline vs one week later; later exams with new problems on the same concepts: "delayed feedback produced superior transfer of knowledge"; students said they benefited most from immediate feedback, "a metacognitive disconnect between actual and perceived effectiveness." — [WUSTL profile abstract](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/delaying-feedback-promotes-transfer-of-knowledge-despite-student-/)
- **[MODERATE; randomized, N = 2,445, math practice items, abstract only]** Attali & van der Kleij (2017): after an incorrect first response, immediate feedback with a delayed review outperformed delayed feedback; after a correct first response it underperformed delayed feedback; immediate feedback without delayed review performed worse than delayed feedback in both cases. — [ACU abstract](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8610z/effects-of-feedback-elaboration-and-feedback-timing-during-computer-based-practice-in-mathematics-problem-solving)
- **[MODERATE; Grade 2, RCT with WWC reservations]** Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson (2016, JECP): on the one-day-later test the immediate-feedback group (answer after each problem) scored descriptively higher than the summative group (answers after all 12 problems): overall 86.00 vs 78.00, each compared separately with the same 65.00 no-feedback group; transfer 83.00 (immediate, significant vs control) vs 70.00 (summative, n.s. vs control) vs 58.00 control. The WWC page does not report a direct immediate-vs-summative test. — [WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/89954)
- **[MODERATE; Exp. 2 of Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson JEP, grades 2–3]** For children who had been taught a strategy, both immediate correct-answer feedback and summative correct-answer feedback lowered procedural (OR = 0.31 and 0.13) and conceptual scores relative to no feedback; "Summative feedback was also harmful." — [Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson](https://news.vanderbilt.edu/files/FyfeRittleJohnson2015_JEP.pdf)
- **[MODERATE; narrative review claim]** Zhang & Fiorella (2022): immediate feedback may beat delayed for short-term error correction, "but that advantage may fade on delayed tests." — [NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10429504)
- **[MODERATE; Schwerter et al.]** Delayed-test (one week) advantage for hints+MTF reported in a college statistics course (see 1c).

### Inferences
- The manipulations differ: "delay of the correctness signal," "delay of the explanation/answer," and "delay of the next attempt" are different variables. Retrieved studies manipulate the first two (Attali & van der Kleij; Mullet; Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson). None retrieved manipulates delay of the next attempt after a failed problem, so the evidence does not directly address deferring a re-attempt to the next day.
- Direction of the timing effect in the retrieved studies depends on outcome (immediate/procedural vs. transfer), learner level (low vs. high achievers), and whether the first response was right or wrong (Attali & van der Kleij).

### Gaps
- Kulik & Kulik (1988), Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2007), Metcalfe, Kornell & Finn (2009), Corbett & Anderson (2001) not retrieved. Mullet et al. N and effect sizes not on the page read. Attali & van der Kleij population and effect sizes not retrieved.
- No study retrieved of immediate vs delayed *retry opportunity* in math.

## 4. Self-explanation and self-correction prompts without new information; comparing one's own work with a worked solution

### Takeaway
Self-explanation prompts have a medium average effect in meta-analysis (g = .55 across 64 reports; math g = .441, k = 16), measured mostly on immediate tests, and classroom/long-term evidence is weaker. The meta-analysis does not support the idea that self-explanation works only by adding information. One abstract-level study found that comparing one's own flawed model with an expert model beat self-explaining the expert model alone. No retrieved study tests generic "check your work / re-try" prompts in math without new information.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG; meta-analysis; mostly immediate tests; excludes studies that combined self-explanation with feedback]** Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne (2018, *Educational Psychology Review*), "Inducing self-explanation": 64 research reports, 69 effect sizes, 5,917 participants; overall g = .55 (SE .050; 95% CI .45–.65); Q = 196.63 (df = 68), I² = 65.42. By domain: mathematics N = 1,808, k = 16, g = .441 (CI .251–.632); science k = 25, g = .565; computer science k = 9, g = .761; social sciences k = 6, g = .555 (subject moderator not significant, p = .571). Education level: elementary k = 10, g = .483; high school k = 13, g = .426; undergraduate k = 42, g = .611; professional k = 4, g = .680 (p = .384). Outcome: recall k = 6, g = .498; transfer k = 17, g = .534; problem solving k = 14, g = .445. Prompt wording: interrogative k = 36, g = .559; imperative k = 12, g = .395; predirected k = 8, g = .700; fill-in-the-blank k = 2, g = .895; multiple choice k = 2, g = .242 (CI −.247 to .731). Self-explanation vs instructional explanation: g = .35; vs no additional explanation: g = .67 (k = 41). "Contrary to the coverage hypothesis"; "a substantial portion of the benefit reported for self-explanation appears to be alternately available from instructor-provided explanations." Prior knowledge, delayed tests and generated-vs-menu prompts were not coded. — [Bisra et al. 2018 PDF (Gwern mirror)](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf)
- **[MODERATE; meta-analysis, abstract-level only]** Rittle-Johnson, Loehr & Durkin (2017, *ZDM Mathematics Education* 49:599–611), meta-analysis of self-explanation prompts in mathematics: prompted self-explanation produced a "small to moderate improvement in procedural knowledge," plus gains in conceptual knowledge and procedural transfer, when measured immediately; evidence that it reliably improves classroom learning or long-term retention is "much weaker"; scaffolding high-quality explanations strengthened the effect on immediate outcomes; controlling time on task did not change the effect. Stated design principles: (a) scaffold high-quality explanations, (b) design prompts so they do not divert attention from other important content, (c) prompt learners to explain correct information, (d) prompt learners to explain why common misconceptions are wrong. No study counts or g values on the page read. — [Springer abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11858-017-0834-z)
- **[WEAK; N = 24 eighth graders, biology text, 1994]** Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher (1994, *Cognitive Science* 18:439–477): prompted group (n = 14) vs unprompted control (n = 10) on a 101-sentence circulatory-system passage; gains 32% vs 22% pretest-to-posttest, interaction F(1, 22) = 5.1, p < .05; harder questions 22.6% vs 12.5%, t(22) = 2.64, p < .01; ability (CAT) did not predict gains. Self-explanations were "inferences that went beyond the text"; about 30% integrated new information with commonsense knowledge and 41% with prior sentences; "Roughly one fourth" were incorrect (excluding them did not change results). Within the prompted group, high explainers (mean 87 self-explanations) vs low (29): gain 38% vs 27% (t(6) = 2.14, p < .07); on harder categories 33% vs 17% (p < .05); 8 of 14 prompted vs 2 of 9 unprompted reached the target mental model. Time on task differed (reported as mean 2 hr 5 min vs 1 hr 6 min; assignment to groups not verified). Stated limitations: small volunteer sample, only 4 vs 4 high/low explainer comparison, unprompted students may have self-explained covertly. — [Chi et al. 1994 PDF](https://pcl.sitehost.iu.edu/rgoldsto/courses/cogscilearning/chiselfexplanation.pdf)
- **[WEAK–MODERATE; abstract only; science domain]** Gadgil, Nokes-Malach & Chi (2012, *Learning and Instruction*): learners who compared and contrasted a diagram of their own flawed mental model with an expert model were "more likely to acquire a correct mental model" and showed deeper understanding of the system of relations than learners who self-explained the expert model alone (which targeted false beliefs). N, domain and numeric results not on the page read. — [ASU abstract page](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/effectiveness-of-holistic-mental-model-confrontation-in-driving-c)
- **[WEAK; secondhand blog summary of a meta-analysis]** Barbieri et al. (2023): pairing worked examples with self-explanation prompts "may not be a fruitful design modification" (self-explanation "reduced learning on average"); authors speculate students' prior knowledge is often incorrect. Conflicts in tone with Bisra et al.; not verified against the primary paper. — [Learning & the Brain](https://learningandthebrain.com/blog/?p=7218)
- **[WEAK–MODERATE; preprint, N = 92, calculus learning environment, 60-minute practice session]** Chen et al. (arXiv 2604.00142; submitted 2026-03-31, v2 2026-05-30), "Practice Less, Explain More": control (no self-explanation, no AI), menu-based self-explanation, or open-ended self-explanation with LLM feedback. All conditions improved within the session; post-test performance did not differ significantly between conditions; open-ended+LLM beat control on transfer-explanation quality for "Not Enough Information" problems (β = +11.9 percentage points, p = .030), multiple-choice accuracy difference p = .183, all open-ended post-test explanations β = +7.3%, p = .057; open-ended learners completed substantially fewer practice problems in the same time. — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00142)
- **[MODERATE; Kumar et al., see section 2]** In high-school-level math, LLM explanations helped most for students who attempted problems on their own first. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4641653)

### Inferences
- Average self-explanation effects are medium and look similar in math (g = .441) to other subjects; the immediate-test caveat (Rittle-Johnson et al.) and the contradictory secondhand report (Barbieri) mean durability in classrooms is not established.
- Bisra et al.'s result that self-explanation beat instructor-provided explanation (g = .35) and that it beat no-added-explanation (g = .67) bears on whether prompts without added information can help, but nearly all included studies give learners some materials/examples to explain, so this is not the same as prompting a student to explain or recheck their own wrong attempt with no new content.
- The own-model-vs-expert-model comparison finding (Gadgil et al.) is the only retrieved study on comparing one's own work with a model answer, and it is abstract-level, in science.

### Gaps
- Numeric results of Rittle-Johnson et al. (2017) not retrieved (a third-party mirror of the paywalled paper was denied by the permission system and not retried). Barbieri et al. (2023) primary paper not retrieved.
- No retrieved study of "self-correction" prompts (re-check, find your own error) in math without feedback; no math-specific study comparing students' own solutions against worked solutions; Siegler (2002) explaining correct and incorrect answers and Booth et al. (2013) incorrect-example work not retrieved.
- Gadgil et al. N, domain and effect sizes not retrieved.

## 5. Incubation and breaks: does setting a stuck problem aside (overnight, sleep) help?

### Takeaway
Meta-analysis finds a small positive incubation effect (random-effects mean d = 0.29, k = 117 studies) that depends on problem type, preparation time, and what fills the break; the paper does not examine sleep or overnight breaks. Sleep studies are mixed: more difficult remote-associates items and a hidden-rule task benefited, but sleep did not help classical insight problems or magic tricks (N = 77); cueing during sleep improved next-morning solution of previously unsolved puzzles (31.7% vs 20.5%). No math-practice study was found.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG; meta-analysis, mostly short lab breaks, non-math problems (creative/divergent, linguistic insight, visual insight)]** Sio & Ormerod (2009, *Psychological Bulletin*): 29 publications (20 refereed articles, 8 dissertations, 1 conference article), 117 independent studies, 3,606 participants (median 25 per study); 85 of 117 studies had positive effect sizes. Weighted random-effects mean d = 0.29 (reported 95% CI 0.21–0.39; extraction notes this CI is slightly inconsistent with the reported SE of 0.04, which would give 0.21–0.37); unweighted mean 0.36 (SD 0.51, 95% CI 0.26–0.45) after recoding outliers (0.41 before). Heterogeneity Q = 173.99, df = 113, p < .001. By problem type: creative (divergent thinking) k = 14, d = 0.29 [0.02, 0.55]; linguistic insight k = 65, d = 0.22 [0.13, 0.32]; visual insight k = 35, d = 0.26 [0.10, 0.41]. Moderators: preparation period length significant (B = .03, β = .35, p < .05); incubation period length not significant (B = .001; β = .055, z = 0.45; the paper notes "long" and "short" have no standard operationalization, e.g., a 15-min period called "long" in one study and 20 min "short" in another); filling the break with a low-demand task vs rest: B = .14 (n.s.), high-demand vs rest B = .16 (n.s.) across all studies, but for linguistic insight problems low-demand beat rest (B = .54, β = .43, p < .05) and high-demand did not (B = .06). The article does not mention sleep or overnight incubation. Publication-bias check: regression of effect size on sample size, standardized coefficient .08 (p = .41), but the authors acknowledge bias favoring significant results may still overstate effects. The abstract states high-cognitive-demand fillers produced a smaller effect and that using incubation practically "requires careful design." — [Sio & Ormerod 2009 PDF (Gwern mirror)](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/writing/2009-sio.pdf); [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19210055/)
- **[MODERATE–WEAK; abstract only; N and effect sizes not retrieved; lab, remote-associates items]** Sio, Monaghan & Ormerod (2013, *Memory & Cognition* 41(2):159–166), "Sleep on it, but only if it is difficult": after sleep, wake, or no delay, participants reattempted previously unsolved remote-associates problems that varied in difficulty. "The sleep group solved more difficult problems than the other groups, but no difference was found for easy problems." Authors conclude sleep facilitates problem solving "most likely via spreading activation." — [Sussex repository abstract](https://sro.sussex.ac.uk/id/eprint/51102/)
- **[MODERATE; abstract only; N not retrieved]** Wagner, Gais, Haider, Verleger & Born (2004, *Nature* 427), number reduction task (learning stimulus–response sequences with a hidden abstract rule): "more than twice as many subjects gained insight into the hidden rule after sleep as after wakefulness" (regardless of time of day); sleep did not enhance insight in the absence of initial training. — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v427y2004i6972d10.1038_nature02223.html)
- **[MODERATE; null result, N = 77, adults (mean age 23.8), 3-hour daytime design, EEG-monitored sleep]** Schönauer et al. (2018, *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 12:72; first-author name from memory, not on the page read), "Sleep does not promote solving classical insight problems and magic tricks": four groups (sleep or wake incubation; two no-incubation controls); 10 magic tricks plus matchstick arithmetic, nine-dot and eight-coin problems. Magic tricks retest: incubation F(1,64) = 0.008, p = 0.930; sleep/wake F(1,64) = 0.047, p = 0.829. Classical problems retest, pooled: incubation F(1,73) = 0.131, p = 0.719; sleep/wake F(1,73) = 0.641, p = 0.426. Initial solution rates: matchsticks 45.5 ± 5.7%, nine-dot 6.0 ± 2.9%, eight-coin 8.0 ± 3.1%. Power: could detect effects above ηp² = 0.1 with 80% power; effects near 1% of variance had about 14% power. Extraction notes a group-size inconsistency between tables (17/15/19/17 vs 19 and 17). Authors argue sleep benefits appear mainly for associative tasks (spreading activation/strengthening) rather than restructuring. — [Frontiers PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00072/pdf)
- **[MODERATE; abstract only; not preregistered per page note; N = 57, adults]** Sanders, Osburn, Paller & Beeman (2019, *Psychological Science* 30(11)): in the evening 57 participants saw puzzles each paired with a sound; overnight, half of the sounds for unsolved puzzles were played during sleep; next morning participants solved 31.7% of cued vs 20.5% of uncued puzzles ("a 55% improvement"). This tests cueing during sleep, not sleep vs same-day delay. — [SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797619873344)

### Inferences
- The meta-analytic incubation benefit is small (d ≈ 0.22–0.29 by problem type) with substantial heterogeneity; it is moderated by how long people worked before the break (preparation) but not by break length, and no overnight/sleep contrast was coded.
- Sleep-specific results are split by task type: positive for difficult remote-associates items and a hidden-rule number task; null for classical insight and magic-trick problems in a small daytime-nap design. None of these tasks is a calculus/algebra procedure problem.
- Reattempt-after-break studies (Sio 2013; Sanders 2019) used problems participants had already failed, which matches the "stuck, set aside, retry" structure, but with riddle-type puzzles.

### Gaps
- Sio, Monaghan & Ormerod (2013) N, delay length and solution rates not retrieved; Wagner et al. N and percentages not retrieved (PMC reviews of the number reduction task blocked by CAPTCHA).
- No study retrieved on incubation or sleep in math problem solving (algebra/calculus), nor on overnight delay versus same-day retry for failed practice items.
- Sanders et al. is abstract-only; page notes no permanent archive of data/materials.

## 6. Generative AI help vs no help: Bastani et al. (PNAS 2025) and follow-ups

### Takeaway
Bastani et al. (about a thousand Turkish high-school students) found that unrestricted GPT-4 access improved practice scores by 48% but cut later unassisted exam scores by 17% versus students who never had access; a hint-only tutor with teacher-supplied solutions raised practice scores by 127% and left exam scores statistically unchanged (not improved). Follow-ups are heterogeneous: observational evidence of declines in learning time and retention in an adaptive math platform after ChatGPT; evidence that answer-seeking use correlates with lower understanding while explanation after an attempt helps; and RCTs of AI hints/explanations with null or marginal delayed-test effects.

### Cited Findings

**6a. Bastani et al.**
- **[STRONG–MODERATE; classroom-randomized field experiment, grades 9–11, one school in Turkey; PNAS 122, e2422633122 (2025)]** PNAS abstract: "having GPT-4 access while solving problems significantly improves performance" (48% improvement in grades for GPT Base, 127% for GPT Tutor); "when access is subsequently taken away, students actually perform worse than those who never had access" (17% reduction in grades for GPT Base); "These negative learning effects are largely mitigated by the safeguards in GPT Tutor." — [IDEAS/RePEc (PNAS abstract)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html)
- **[Details below are from the SSRN/working-paper version "Generative AI Can Harm Learning", hosted on a third-party site; the PNAS version's full text was not retrieved]** Sample: "nearly a thousand students" (abstract); main analysis 839 students who completed the survey, 2,848 observations, ~50 classes in grades 9–11; classroom-level randomization (honors classes excluded because not randomized); sessions covered ~15% of the curriculum per grade. Design: four 90-minute sessions each with (1) teacher review, (2) assisted practice (the only randomized part), (3) closed-book, closed-laptop unassisted exam with problems closely matching practice problems. Arms: Control (books/notes, no devices); GPT Base (ChatGPT-like interface; GPT-4 prompted as a tutor with the current problem, no solution); GPT Tutor (prompt included correct solutions, common student mistakes and teacher guidance; instructed to give incremental hints without the full solution, ask students to show their work first, and say whether a submitted answer was correct). Results (coefficients; control means practice 0.284 (SD 0.287), exam 0.321 (SD 0.277)): practice GPT Base +0.137*** (SE 0.031), GPT Tutor +0.361*** (SE 0.032); unassisted exam GPT Base −0.054** (SE 0.022), GPT Tutor −0.004 (SE 0.013, n.s.). Pre-registered pairwise tests on the exam: GPT Base vs control −0.035 (95% CI −0.057 to −0.012, p = 0.003); GPT Tutor vs control −0.006 (p = 0.613); GPT Tutor vs GPT Base +0.028 (p = 0.019). The authors state GPT Tutor showed "no positive effect" on the exam. — [Working-paper PDF](https://nmdprojects.net/teaching_resources/bastani_generative_ai_can_harm_learning.pdf)
- Heterogeneity, perceptions, mechanism, and errors (same source, working-paper version): weaker students (lower prior GPA) and students with private tutors benefited more from GPT Base during practice. GPT Base students "did not perceive that they performed worse or learned less"; GPT Tutor students reported significantly better perceived exam performance (coef. 0.130**) and rated practice more valuable (0.271***) while their actual exam effect was null. Most common first message to GPT Base was "what is the answer?" (31% of first messages). GPT-4 answered correctly 51% of the time on 57 practice problems each queried 10 times ("What is the answer?"): 42% logical and 8% arithmetic errors. Authors' mechanism: students use GPT Base as a "crutch" and copy; logical errors reduced practice performance but did not spill over to matched exam problems (argued to indicate copying). GPT Tutor students spent about 2.0 more minutes per session (13% more) and more conversation was non-superficial. Caveats: intention-to-treat with five non-compliant class sessions, average absenteeism 12.3%, one school, mechanism evidence correlational, long-term effect "inferred, not measured," no dedicated limitations section.

**6b. Follow-ups and related studies (math first)**
- **[WEAK–MODERATE; quasi-experimental/observational, platform data, preprint; four of five authors McGraw Hill employees (the platform owner)]** Rismanchian, Uzun, Matayoshi, Cosyn & Kurd-Misto (arXiv 2605.21629; undated, data through 2025 Q3/Q4), "Faster Completion, Less Learning": 3,197,803 ALEKS learning interactions (Grade 5 to College Algebra, 2015 Q3–2025 Q3); identification compares AI-susceptible text word problems to AI-resistant graph problems before/after ChatGPT; AI use not observed directly. Cumulative change in learning time over 11 post-ChatGPT quarters: College Algebra −26.9% (CI −34.0, −19.0); high school −31.3% (−37.3, −25.4); middle school −9.0% (−14.9, −2.9); Grade 5 −1.3% (−8.8, +6.7; n.s.). Proctored retention items (66 randomly assigned items, 6,698 observations): odds of a correct answer down 25.0% cumulatively (OR 0.75, CI 0.56–0.98; β = −0.0260, p = 0.037; randomization p = 0.027 stratified, 0.056 unstratified); non-proctored retention shows +85% odds (interpreted as AI-assisted answering). Stated limitations: no direct AI-use measure, population-level only, pre-trends of opposite sign, small item sample, quadratic specification gives −42% for College (authors reject it). — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.21629)
- **[WEAK–MODERATE; 3 studies, Python programming, graduate and undergraduate students in Netherlands/Germany]** Lehmann, Cornelius & Sting (arXiv 2409.09047v2): LLM available only during a 45-minute learning phase. Authors: usage effects not causally identified. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.09047)
- **[MODERATE; randomized, N = 274, algebra and statistics; abstract only]** Pardos & Bhandari (2024, *PLOS ONE*), "ChatGPT-generated help produces learning gains equivalent to human tutor-authored help on mathematics skills": 3 hint conditions (control, human tutor, ChatGPT) × 4 subjects (Elementary, Intermediate, College Algebra, Statistics); "only the ChatGPT condition produces statistically significant learning gains compared to a no-help control"; no statistically significant differences in gains or time-on-task between ChatGPT and human-tutor help; ChatGPT-generated help "failed quality checks on 32% of problems," falling to nearly 0% (algebra) and 13% (statistics) after self-consistency. Recruitment, N per cell and effect sizes not retrieved. — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/42x5f0tm)
- **[MODERATE; large randomized field experiment, grades 6–8, 20 schools in Hamilton County, TN; 2×2×2 individually randomized at login; August 2026 working paper]** Oreopoulos, Liut, Sungu & Low, "Making AI Tutoring Productive: Evidence from a Mastery-Based Math Practice Experiment" (EdWorkingPaper 26-1552; NBER WP 35621). - **[MODERATE–WEAK; randomized, discrete mathematics (proofs), university, 148 analyzed (155 recruited), preprint]** Chen et al. ### Inferences
- Across Bastani, Lehmann, Kumar and the ALEKS data, the kinds of AI help associated with harm to later unassisted performance are those that supply answers/solutions before or in place of the student's own attempt (GPT Base "what is the answer?"; solution-seeking; AI-susceptible word problems). The retrieved cases where help appears neutral or beneficial supply hints/explanations, require an attempt first, or follow a mistake (GPT Tutor; Kumar Exp. 1; Pardos & Bhandari; Oreopoulos).
- "Preserved" does not mean "improved": GPT Tutor left unassisted exam performance statistically unchanged (−0.004, SE 0.013) despite +127% practice scores; Oreopoulos finds only marginal delayed gains; Chen et al. (calculus, discrete math) find no exam differences.
- Practice performance and learning dissociate in the evidence (practice +48% / exam −17%; ALEKS non-proctored retention +85% odds vs proctored −25%), and students' self-perceptions did not track actual learning (Bastani; Lehmann).
- These AI conditions differ from non-AI help in the proposed escalation (simpler problem, prerequisite problem, textbook pointer) because they supply new information adaptively; the retrieved evidence cannot separate effects of "AI" from effects of "information provided after an error."

### Gaps
- PNAS full text not retrieved (working-paper version used for details); no long-term follow-up in Bastani. The Turkish study has one school.
- Other widely discussed AI-tutoring studies were not retrieved (e.g., physics RCT by Kestin et al., Ghana math Rori study, Tutor CoPilot); Kumar et al. N, population and effect sizes not retrieved; Pardos & Bhandari recruitment and effect sizes not retrieved.
- No RCT retrieved with college calculus students comparing AI hints vs no help on a later unassisted exam.

## 7. Adjacent evidence relevant to a graduated help ladder (step-level feedback, hints, scaffolding, mastery workflow)

*(Added because the proposed escalation — simpler problem on the failing step, prerequisite problem, textbook pointer — sits next to the evidence on feedback granularity and hints. Coverage is shallow; other researchers may cover this.)*

### Takeaway
Tutoring syntheses show larger effects when feedback is given at the level of steps than at the level of final answers (0.76 vs 0.31). A large cluster-randomized trial of immediate-feedback math homework raised grade-7 scores (improvement index 7). Hints plus multiple tries raised in-session performance in the two studies retrieved, with only mixed evidence on delayed outcomes; mastery progression alone did not raise a one-week-delayed test.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG; review/meta-analysis of tutoring systems; mixed STEM domains]** VanLehn (2011, *Educational Psychologist*): effect sizes vs no tutoring: answer-based tutoring (CAI) 0.31 (165 effects, 40% reliable); step-based (ITS) 0.76 (28 effects, 68%); substep-based (ITS) 0.40 (26 effects, 54%); human tutoring 0.79 (10 effects, 80%). Granularity definitions: answer-based = student solves on paper and enters only the answer; step-based = hints and feedback on the steps normally taken in the tool; substep-based = tutor can discuss reasoning behind each step. The ERIC abstract gives human tutoring d = 0.79 and "the effect size of intelligent tutoring systems was 0.76" (no breakdown for the three granularities there; slide-deck breakdown above is the more detailed source). — [VanLehn slide deck](https://quality.mit.edu/files/2012/01/Quality-Symposium-Kurt-VanLehn.pdf); [ERIC abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ946764)
- **[STRONG; cluster RCT, 2,850 grade-7 students, 43 Maine schools (23 intervention, 22 comparison); WWC "Meets standards without reservations"]** Roschelle, Feng, Murphy & Mason (2016, *AERA Open* 2(4)): ASSISTments online homework (immediate feedback to students; hints per SRI; plus teacher performance reports) vs usual homework: TerraNova math mean 690.79 vs 681.95, significant, improvement index 7; "Students with low prior mathematics achievement benefited most." The retrieved pages give no effect size in SD units, and the intervention bundles student feedback with teacher reports and training, so the student-feedback component is not isolated. — [SRI page](https://www.sri.com/publication/education-learning-pubs/online-mathematics-homework-increases-student-achievement/); [WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/86375)
- **[MODERATE]** Hints with multiple tries: Schwerter et al. (college statistics, hints improved learning-phase correctness and one-week-delayed test, but not exam grades) and Attali (2015) via secondhand (hints+MTF > MTF without hints) — see 1c.
- **[MODERATE]** Mastery progression: in Oreopoulos et al. (section 6), requiring three correct in a row raised mastery attainment (+0.287, SE 0.016) but mastery alone did not change the delayed test (−0.010, SE 0.034); the CAL-only control already showed worked solutions after incorrect answers. — [EdWorkingPaper](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1552.pdf)
- **[WEAK; secondhand]** Worked-examples effect in math: g = 0.48 (43 studies) per a blog summary of Barbieri et al. (2023). — [blog](https://learningandthebrain.com/blog/?p=7218)

### Inferences
- In the tutoring-system literature, the evidence favors feedback that engages with intermediate steps over answer-only checking; the retrieved syntheses do not test "unlimited retries without hints" as a category.
- The retrieved evidence on hints is short-term; effects on delayed tests are mixed.

### Gaps
- Razzaq & Heffernan (scaffolding vs hints in ASSISTments), Ma et al. (2014) and Kulik & Fletcher (2016) ITS meta-analyses, mastery-learning meta-analyses, and prerequisite-remediation studies were not retrieved. ASSISTments SD-unit effect size not on the pages read. Evidence on "pointing to textbook sections" after failure not found.
