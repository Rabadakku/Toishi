# Speed training on multi-step math: mechanisms, interventions, individual differences, extra-time effects

Scope note: research session was limited to roughly 17 tool calls. Several named sources were not reached (see Gaps). Read depth per source is stated as FULL-TEXT-EXCERPT (WebFetch of the document, which can be truncated), ABSTRACT, or SNIPPET. No design recommendations. Cross-reference: timed_practice.md covers timed vs untimed practice, K-6 fluency drills, math anxiety, pacing.

## 1. How speed improves with practice and what drives it

### Takeaway
Average practice curves look like power laws, but per-participant fits favour exponentials, and averaging across learners with different rates can create a spurious power law. For multi-step procedures, large speed gains come mainly from a strategy shift (multi-step algorithm to memory retrieval), but that gain was highly specific to the practiced problems in the one lab study read.

### Cited Findings
- Evans, Brown, Mewhort & Heathcote (2018, Psychological Review, DOI 10.1037/rev0000105) state early work favoured the power law but this was "an artifact of biases"; averaging over exponential functions with differing rates can approximate a power function. Type: theory plus hierarchical-Bayesian reanalysis of 17 published data sets (about 309 participants by their Table 1 sum; the text mentions 18 data sets in places). Exponential forms won in 15 of 17 data sets; a delayed-onset variant was preferred in 13; the standard power law was never selected. Read: FULL-TEXT-EXCERPT. Populations are mixed lab tasks, not math. — [Evans et al. 2018 (UTAS repository PDF)](https://figshare.utas.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Refining_the_Law_of_Practice/22964183/1/files/40706153.pdf)
- The same paper says Heathcote et al. (2000) fitted each subject and condition separately and found "practice curves were better fit by the exponential function in the majority of participants and conditions." This is secondhand (via Evans et al.); the 2000 paper itself was not read. — [Evans et al. 2018](https://figshare.utas.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Refining_the_Law_of_Practice/22964183/1/files/40706153.pdf)
- Rickard (1997, J. Exp. Psychol.: General 126(3), 288-311), lab experiments. Practice moved people from slow multi-step procedures to fast retrieval. The power law held within each strategy but not in the overall data. Exp 1: N=18 intro-psychology students solving "pound arithmetic" (3-4 step algorithm) over 6 sessions; retrieval dominant by about block 60; instance theory r2=.94 with systematic deviations, CMPL r2=.94 fitted per strategy. Exp 2: N=21 intro-psychology students, alphabet arithmetic, 4 sessions; CMPL r2=.97 vs instance theory .95. Transfer to reversed-type and new problems was minimal; learning "highly specific to the practiced problems." One participant who never shifted strategy showed a clean power-law speed-up. Read: FULL-TEXT-EXCERPT (first 100k of 109.6k characters). Artificial arithmetic, not calculus. — [Rickard 1997 PDF (author lab site)](https://rickardlab.ucsd.edu/pdf/Rickard1997.pdf)
- A Memory & Cognition-type article (Springer page BF03193086; authors and year not shown in the fetched excerpt, so attribution unverified) had participants practise complex multiplication (e.g., 3 x 18) pre/post; before practice the most common strategy was a mental multidigit algorithm with high working-memory load; after practice "retrieval was used almost exclusively" and working-memory load fell to minimal; problem automaticity (proportion of retrieval trials), error rates and participant variability predicted latency. N not in excerpt. Read: FULL-TEXT-EXCERPT (partial). — [Springer BF03193086](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03193086)

### Inferences
- If gains come mostly from replacing a multi-step procedure with retrieval, they accrue on repeated or near-identical items; Rickard's specificity result suggests varied problems would show slower aggregate gains. This is consistent in direction with the small slope (about 0.03-0.035 per ln(1+n)) in the owner's logs, but no source here measured varied calculus-type problems directly.
- A log-count fit is a descriptive curve; Evans et al. imply that pooled-learner curve shape does not identify individual-level learning form.

### Gaps
- Newell & Rosenbloom 1981, Anderson 1982, Neves & Anderson 1981, Logan 1988 instance theory, Delaney et al. 1998, Heathcote et al. 2000 original: not read; only secondhand via the two papers above.
- Quantified size of speed gains on varied (non-repeated) multi-step math problems: not found.
- PMC article PMC3098361 (Power laws of learning and forgetting) was returned as a CAPTCHA page; not accessed, no workaround attempted.

## 2. Interventions that make students faster (measured on later performance)

### Takeaway
Direct, well-powered evidence that speed drills on sub-skills transfer to faster whole-problem solution is thin in what was reached; the one component-composite study found was a 4-student single-case design with mixed results. Worked-example evidence for time was not obtained (blocked).

### Cited Findings
- Stocker & Kubina (2021, Behavior Analysis in Practice 14:608-622): N=4 sixth graders (suburban charter school, no special-education services), alternating-treatments design, 10 days, composite skill = order of operations; practice on an element skill (fractions, decimals, or integers) in 3 x 1-min or 1 x 3-min timings with feedback. 3 of 4 students did better in intervention conditions than baseline; one worsened; the paper could not tell the two formats apart. It does not test element-to-composite transfer directly (reviews it, recommends future component analyses); retention/application not assessed. Text and Table 4 disagree on one student's improvement index (x6.48/x4.86 vs x7.8/x4.9). Evidence type: single-case experiment. Read: FULL-TEXT-EXCERPT. — [Stocker & Kubina 2021 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8458516/)
- Same paper (authors' claim, not tested there): "Students who lack fluency in element skills often experience difficulty combining element skills into a compound skill." — [PMC8458516](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8458516/)
- Corral, Quilici & Rutchick (2020, Psychological Research; online 2019): random assignment to 4 conditions varying when schema training occurred and worked-example:practice ratio; outcomes near and far transfer on GRE-style word problems (work rate, distance-ratio, percent). Schema training at the outset beat training after practice, especially on far transfer; a higher worked-example ratio gave better test performance. N and population not shown in the fetched page. Outcome is accuracy-type performance; solution time not reported in the excerpt. Evidence type: RCT-like lab experiment. Read: ABSTRACT/page excerpt. — [Springer s00426-019-01164-8](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00426-019-01164-8)

### Inferences
- The behavioural-fluency literature surfaced here is dominated by small single-case designs in K-12; I found no controlled college-calculus test of sub-skill speed drills transferring to whole-problem time.

### Gaps
- Sweller & Cooper 1985 / Cooper & Sweller 1987 (worked examples and solution time): the only copy reached was blocked by the tool permission classifier (flagged as a copy of walled content); not read, no workaround attempted. Search snippet gave no usable numbers.
- Binder 1996, Johnson & Layng 1992, Locke & Latham goal setting on speed, deadlines during practice, repeated practice of one type: no primary source reached.
- Any study measuring later exam speed after speed-feedback or time-limit practice: not found.

## 3. Individual differences in speed-up

### Takeaway
Ackerman's theory predicts the correlation of general ability with performance falls with practice on consistent tasks while perceptual speed matters more; support was mixed. No source reached directly tested whether initially slow students speed up more or less.

### Cited Findings
- Summary of Ackerman (1988) skill-acquisition theory (secondhand, Hambrick-written summary on a third-party site): Phase 1 general ability predicts performance; Phase 2 perceptual speed gains importance as production systems form; Phase 3 psychomotor ability. Only consistent tasks progress through all phases; complexity raises reliance on general ability. The decline in general-ability correlations with practice replicated across experiments; perceptual-speed trends were significant in only 3 of 8 experiments; the most complex task (air traffic controller) did not convincingly fit. Sample sizes not given. Page does not state converge/diverge explicitly. Read: summary page only. — [CogSci Summaries, Ackerman 1988](https://www.jimdavies.org/summaries/ackerman1988.html)
- Rickard (1997): one participant who never made the algorithm-to-retrieval shift showed smooth power-law speed-up (N=1 within an N=18 study); participants differed in whether and when the strategy transition occurred. — [Rickard 1997](https://rickardlab.ucsd.edu/pdf/Rickard1997.pdf)
- Evans et al. (2018): when learners differ in learning rates, averaged curves can mimic power laws, i.e., rate heterogeneity is the premise of the artifact argument. — [Evans et al. 2018](https://figshare.utas.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Refining_the_Law_of_Practice/22964183/1/files/40706153.pdf)

### Inferences
- Strategy differences (algorithm vs retrieval) are documented as a major source of between-person latency differences in the arithmetic studies above; whether slow-accurate calculus students differ in strategy rather than raw speed was not tested in any source reached.

### Gaps
- Ackerman 1987, 1988 originals (a DTIC technical report ADA213190 appeared in search but was not opened); empirical convergence/divergence rates; processing speed trainability; slow-accurate vs fast-accurate strategy comparisons: not found.

## 4. Automaticity of sub-skills and complex problem solving

### Takeaway
Practice reduces working-memory load as retrieval replaces algorithms (one study), but the specific causal claim that automating basic facts frees capacity for harder problems was not confirmed from a primary source here.

### Cited Findings
- In the Springer BF03193086 study (authors unverified), practice cut working-memory involvement to minimal levels, and the abstract-level finding was "no relationship between automaticity level and working memory load" after practice. — [Springer BF03193086](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03193086)
- Stocker & Kubina authors claim, untested, that stronger element skills went with more fluent order-of-operations performance. — [PMC8458516](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8458516/)

### Gaps
- Tronsky & Royer 2002: search returned nothing identifiable; not read. Cumming & Elkins 1999: a UMN Conservancy page failed to load; not read. Cognitive-load-theory automation evidence and algebra-fluency to calculus-performance links: not obtained.

## 5. Extra time and scores for students without disabilities

### Takeaway
Extra time raises math scores for students without disabilities, but the size in the large randomized ETS studies read was small (about 7 GRE-Q points for 1.5x time); a 1999 meta-analysis (via secondhand summary) reported larger standardized effects. The 2005 meta-analysis was not reached.

### Cited Findings
- Bridgeman et al. (ETS, 2003 report): N=15,948 GRE examinees, random assignment to standard time or 1.5x time on a research quantitative or verbal section; extra time raised quantitative scores about 7 points on the 200-800 scale; gains similar across gender and ethnic groups; lower-ability examinees gained slightly more on quantitative. Evidence type: RCT. Read: ABSTRACT. Population: GRE test-takers (graduate applicants). — [ETS 2003 hygi](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2003/hygi.html)
- ETS 2005 SAT extended-time study (authors not shown in fetched abstract): four conditions (standard, 1.5x with separate section timing, 1.5x without section breaks, 2x); effects "more pronounced for the mathematics section"; medium- and high-ability students benefited, low-ability students gained little or nothing; "too much" time may be detrimental. Point gains not in abstract; N not shown. Read: ABSTRACT. — [ETS 2005 hzdq](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2005/hzdq.html)
- NCEO literature review (Sireci, Li & Scarpati) summarising Chiu & Pearson (1999) meta-analysis (secondhand): 30 studies found, 20 analysed; extended time gave students with disabilities +.37 SD and students without disabilities +.30 SD; authors warn variation across studies was significant. Zuriff (2000): 5 experiments, N=31-126 each, extra time helped both groups. Read: FULL-TEXT-EXCERPT (first 100k of 123k characters). Note: the fetched document is a literature review, and it does not itself describe a "Sireci, Scarpati & Li 2005 meta-analysis"; that paper was not reached. — [NCEO review](https://nceo.umn.edu/docs/onlinepubs/testaccommlitreview.pdf)
- Same review: Bridgeman, Trapani & Curley (in press at that time) estimated about 20 SAT-M points for non-disabled students with time-and-a-half (N not given in review); Camara et al. SAT-M observational data (about 9,000 students with learning disabilities vs about 700,000 others): +38.1 vs +11.8 points, not randomized. — [NCEO review](https://nceo.umn.edu/docs/onlinepubs/testaccommlitreview.pdf)

### Inferences
- A 1.5x-time effect of about 7 points on an 600-point range is small in score units, yet these are standardized admissions tests with their own time design, not college calculus exams; transfer to course exams is unverified.

### Gaps
- Sireci, Scarpati & Li 2005 (Review of Educational Research) original; Lu & Sireci 2007 (search returned unrelated speededness papers); share of students limited by time on math exams; any 2x-time effect size in college math: not found. Rigorous check of the "90% with twice the time" premise: not found.

## 6. Speed-accuracy trade-off in learning

### Takeaway
No relevant primary source was reached on whether pushing speed during practice lowers accuracy or learning, or on timing of speed introduction.

### Cited Findings
- Rickard (1997): within-strategy speed-up occurs alongside a strategy shift to retrieval; error rates were reported as a significant predictor of latency in the Springer BF03193086 study. Neither isolates a speed-emphasis manipulation. — [Rickard 1997](https://rickardlab.ucsd.edu/pdf/Rickard1997.pdf); [BF03193086](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03193086)
- ETS 2005: "too much" extra time may be detrimental, a time-pressure-direction signal only. — [ETS 2005 hzdq](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2005/hzdq.html)

### Gaps
- The search for speed-emphasis vs accuracy-emphasis training returned mostly motor-learning and popular pages (not opened); no math or cognitive-skill experiment found. Evidence on sequencing accuracy-then-speed: not found.
