## 1. Mechanisms of speed-up on multi-step procedures: knowledge compilation, instance retrieval, strategy shifts, repeated vs varied problems, specificity

### Takeaway
Theory and lab data agree that multi-step procedures speed up through several mechanisms at once: steps merge and stop needing retrievals into working memory (compilation), the same steps run faster (strengthening), steps are skipped, and computation is replaced by retrieval of stored answers. The largest speed-ups come from the retrieval route, which is specific to the items practiced. In the one direct contrast found, training on repeated problems left learners slower and less accurate on new problems than training on unique problems. Gains at the level of shared components (productions, steps) did transfer to new tasks. In a large field log of varied adaptive practice, median response time fell only about 6-8%.

### Cited Findings

**Knowledge compilation and production learning (Anderson; Neves & Anderson; ACT-R)**
- Anderson (1982), "Acquisition of cognitive skill", Psychological Review 89(4):369-406. Three stages: a declarative stage in which general procedures interpret facts held in working memory; knowledge compilation ("the process by which the skill transits from the declarative stage to the procedural stage", p. 369); and a procedural stage of "further tuning and gradual speedup" (p. 370). Composition merges sequential productions into one; proceduralization builds retrieved facts into productions so they no longer have to be retrieved into working memory (p. 383). The paper says proceduralization reduces working-memory load, that composition is limited because composed conditions must fit in working memory (p. 384), and that declarative interpretation's working-memory demands explain many novice errors and slowdowns (p. 381). On specificity: proceduralization yields task-specific productions, and the system is "reluctant" to specialize without frequent repetition (p. 390); generalization eases transfer when the same components appear in two procedures (p. 391). Strengthening parameters in ACT: productions start at strength .1, gain .025 per successful application, and are multiplied by .25 on negative feedback (pp. 394-395), values the author calls "somewhat arbitrary". Data are qualitative protocols (three high school students on geometry proofs, p. 375). [Theory with protocol data; Part: OCR text; the abstract says the mechanisms explain the power law, but the derivation section was not in the extracted text] — [Anderson 1982 PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/iq/1982-anderson-2.pdf)
- Anderson, Greeno, Kline & Neves (1980), "Acquisition of Problem-Solving Skill", ONR Technical Report 80-5, written for Anderson (Ed.), Cognitive Skills and Their Acquisition (1981). States that "skill develops by collapsing together multiple steps in one", that proceduralization "eliminates retrieval of information from long term memory", and that "people's ability to use knowledge can be specific to how the knowledge is evoked"; cites Greeno and Magone's observation that students who generate proofs well still make gross errors at proof checking, a skill they had not practiced. Students initially recite a postulate before applying it; "With repeated use, however, application of the postulate smooths out." No timing data or N for compilation; geometry evidence is one 14-year-old studied for 30 sessions over about two months plus six students interviewed weekly. Speed-up accounts are referred to Neves & Anderson in the same volume. [Theory with case data; Part: about 93% of text read] — [DTIC ADA091686](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA091686.pdf)
- Anderson (1981), "Acquisition of Cognitive Skill", ONR Technical Report 81-1: composition "collapses sequences of productions into single productions"; proceduralization "embeds factual knowledge into productions"; if composition always pairs productions, the production count halves with each repetition (generalized to N·aⁿ). Composed productions are "more specific in [their] constraints" than the originals because variable bindings are kept. [Theory; Part: about 83% read; the power-law section was not in the read portion] — [DTIC ADA103283](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA103283.pdf)
- Anderson, Boyle, Corbett & Lewis (1986), "Cognitive Modelling and Intelligent Tutoring" (ONR-86-1): in the LISP tutor, time to type code for a production showed a large drop from its first to its second application and then gradual speed-up (practice grouped as opportunities 1, 2, 3-4, 5-8), which the authors attribute to compilation followed by strengthening. The LISP model had about 500 productions; the authors report that students differ "along only a single dimension of how well they learn". No N, exponents or transfer test reported. The tutor was used in a university course. [Obs (tutor data) and Theory; Part] — [DTIC ADA165981](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA165981.pdf)
- Taatgen & Lee (2003), Human Factors 45(1):61-76: ACT-R production compilation (one step that combines composition and proceduralization, keeping at most one declarative retrieval per rule) modelled learning of the Kanfer-Ackerman air-traffic-controller (ATC) task from instructions, using a task analysis that decomposes it into unit tasks (move, land, queue) with functional and keystroke levels. Fit to an existing data set of 65 college undergraduates (18 ten-minute trials): r² = .97 overall over 10 trials, .98 for landing, .91 for queuing, .87 at the keystroke level of landing. The paper does not separate how much speed-up comes from compilation (no ablation). [Model; Full] — [Taatgen & Lee 2003 PDF](https://www.ai.rug.nl/~niels/publications/HF-published.pdf)
- Lee & Anderson (2001), Cognitive Psychology (title "Does learning a complex task have to be complex? A study in learning decomposition"): reanalysing Ackerman (1988) ATC data from the global level down to keystrokes, learning in the complex task reflected learning of smaller keystroke-level parts, and an eye-tracking experiment showed much of the keystroke-level learning reflected learning at a lower, attentional level. [Obs reanalysis + Lab; Abs (repository record)] — [CMU KiltHub record](https://kilthub.cmu.edu/articles/journal_contribution/Does_Learning_a_Complex_Task_Have_to_Be_Complex_A_Study_in_Learning_Decomposition/6614459)
- Carlson, Khoo, Yaure & Schneider (1990), JEP: General 119(2):193-214: 6 college students practised diagnosing faults in simulated digital circuits for about 50 hours (347 problems); skill was traced at three levels (strategy, subgoals, operators) with tests of working-memory use; the authors conclude restructuring is "goal-sensitive and strategic" and propose a multi-level account. [Lab, small-N longitudinal; Abs] — [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/acquisition-of-a-problem-solving-skill-levels-of-organization-and/)

**Instance retrieval and strategy shifts (Logan; Rickard; Delaney)**
- Logan (1988), "Toward an instance theory of automatization", Psychological Review: assumes obligatory encoding and retrieval of separate instances; "Performance is automatic when it is based on single-step direct-access retrieval of past solutions from memory" (p. 493); retrieval races the algorithm, and the minimum of N samples predicts power-function decreases in both mean and SD with a shared exponent. In the paper's simulations the algorithm won only about 21% of races after 16 presentations and about 16% after 32. Experiment 4 (alphabet arithmetic): each problem shown 72 times (6 per session for 12 sessions); means and SDs fell as power functions (an addend-5 discontinuity at about 24 presentations); a follow-up session with untrained letters showed the slope returning to about 400 ms per count. Summary: "Subjects learned specific responses to specific stimuli and did not transfer well to new stimuli" (p. 511). [Theory + Lab; Part: OCR scan, about 87% read; exact values should be checked against the published article] — [Logan 1988 PDF (IU course copy)](https://pcl.sitehost.iu.edu/rgoldsto/courses/cogscilearning/loganautomaticity.pdf)
- Rickard (1997): power law holds within strategy, not in the aggregate; transfer to new or reversed problems minimal (N = 18 and 21 introductory-psychology students on artificial arithmetic). Details in `speed_training.md` §1 and `curves_time_and_forgetting.md` B4. [Lab; Part] — [Rickard 1997 PDF](https://rickardlab.ucsd.edu/pdf/Rickard1997.pdf)
- Delaney, Reder, Staszewski & Ritter (1998): strategy-specific power curves fit individuals better than one curve; retrieval and calculation differ in intercept, not slope. Details in `timed_practice.md` §3. [Lab; Full] — [CMU PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/memorylab/publications/98_pd_lmr_js_fr.pdf)
- Curve shape: unaveraged learning series favour exponential over power functions; pooling learners with different rates mimics a power law (Heathcote et al. 2000; Evans et al. 2018). Details in `curves_time_and_forgetting.md` B2-B3. [Meta-style survey; Abs / Part] — [Heathcote 2000 PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10909131/); [Evans et al. 2018 PDF](https://figshare.utas.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Refining_the_Law_of_Practice/22964183/1/files/40706153.pdf)
- Deliev (2026), CogSci proceedings: adaptive multiplication-fact practice logs from 540 children aged 6-10 (315,690 trials, 17,575 sessions over 197 days, 11 schools). **Repeated vs varied problems, and how specific the gains are**
- Anderson, Fincham & Douglass (1997), JEP: LMC, "The role of examples and rules in the acquisition of a cognitive skill" (time-relationship rules, e.g., an event "+2 days, -1 hour" later). Exp 1: 28 Carnegie Mellon undergraduates, 4 days; Exp 2: 22 undergraduates, 5 days, 32 training blocks per session; Exp 3: 45 participants (mostly CMU undergraduates plus some high school students), 5 days, trained on all-repeated, all-unique or mixed items. Latency fell across days in every experiment (Exp 1 F(3,72) = 91.96). A cost for applying a rule in the reverse direction appeared only after practice (reverse minus practiced: -0.04 s for rules reversed on days 1-2 vs 0.39 s on days 3-4 in Exp 1; 0.04 vs 0.60 s in Exp 2), and the original study example was answered faster than other examples (F(1,27) = 41.04). Exp 2 repeated vs unique items: 7.74 vs 8.01 s and 4.1% vs 5.1% errors overall; 6.33 vs 6.80 s and 2.5% vs 3.6% on day 5. Exp 3, final all-unique transfer test: those trained only on repeated items took 11.19 s with 10.1% errors vs 9.58 s and 4.0% for those trained on unique items. Participants could not recall 37.2% (Exp 2) and 36.7% (Exp 3) of the original study examples despite about 3% training errors. Authors: four overlapping stages (analogy to the example, declarative rules, production rules, retrieval of specific examples); "performance in a skilled task can reflect a complex mixture of processes". [Lab; Full] — [Anderson, Fincham & Douglass 1997 PDF (course copy)](https://app.nova.edu/toolbox/instructionalproducts/edd8124/articles/1997-AndersonFinchamDouglass.pdf)
- Carlson & Lundy (1992), JEP: LMC 18(1):127-141: mental-arithmetic sequences in which the data and the order of operations were each consistent or varied; consistency at each level had separable benefits; consistent data improved final performance (and, the authors conclude, led learners to restructure), while a consistent sequence improved performance without restructuring (and did not help when the sequence did not match the goal structure, Exp 2). [Lab, 3 experiments; Abs; N not on record] — [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/consistency-and-restructuring-in-learning-cognitive-procedural-se/)
- Carlson, Sullivan & Schneider (1989), JEP: LMC 15(3):517-526: 8 Penn State undergraduates, about 20 hours and over 8,000 trials (24 sessions) of speeded judgments on digital logic gates. Mixing gates raised latency from 673 ms (blocked) to 1,562 ms (first randomized block); randomized latency then fell roughly as a power function (r = .96, log RT vs log trial) and reached the blocked level only after more than 8,000 trials. Gate-specific costs shrank but persisted (multiple- vs single-input gap about 340 to 145 ms; negated and verification judgments slower throughout). Accessing a held memory set cost roughly 800 ms at both practice levels tested (336 and 1,232 trials per gate). Adding a new gate (XOR) after 464 trials per gate gave a large first-block cost (1,233 ms) without disrupting the other six rules. Authors: "Extended practice may simply increase the speed of a cascade of sequential processes." [Lab, within-subject; Full] — [Carlson et al. 1989 PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/1989-carlson.pdf)

For Elio (1986, multistep arithmetic procedures, 50 training then 50 transfer problems) there was "transfer from the initial procedure to the transfer procedure in all conditions", including a control in which all calculations differed; identical steps transferred more. [Review + Model, reporting Lab data; Part: about 83% read; N not stated] — [Taatgen manuscript PDF](https://www.ai.rug.nl/~niels/publications/Transfer130226.pdf)
- Healy & Wohldmann (chapter, "Specificity and transfer of learning", Psychology of Learning and Motivation vol. 57, in press at the time of the copy): in digit data entry, repetition priming (old numbers typed faster than new) appeared in execution time at immediate test in all experiments; initiation (cognitive) time showed no repetition priming and no consistent transfer; in speeded aiming with a reversed mouse, varying the reversal gave little or no positive transfer. [Review of Lab studies; Full; N not given] — [Healy & Wohldmann chapter PDF (course copy)](https://home.cs.colorado.edu/~mozer/Teaching/syllabi/TopicsInCognitiveScienceSpring2012/HealyWohldmann.pdf)

**Step skipping**
- Blessing & Dronek (2006), CogSci proceedings vol. 28: 26 undergraduates solved 96 problems in "Symbol Fun", an algebra analogue with explicit rules; half were required to skip an intermediate step from problem 1, half from problem 48. On recognition probes (problems 24, 48, 72, 96), late skippers' performance on 2-step items diverged by the end (F(1,24) = 4.38), which the authors read as overt skipping while still mentally performing the steps; they argue that well-practised component steps may make composition less likely. Time saved is shown only in a figure. [Lab; Full] — [Blessing & Dronek 2006 PDF](https://repositories.cdlib.org/content/qt85k1z163/qt85k1z163.pdf?t=op2m51)

[Meta; Full / Abs] — [Arthur et al. 1998 PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/1998-arthur.pdf); [Tatel & Ackerman 2025 DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000481)

### Inferences
- Arithmetic on the logged average (my calculation): a log-time slope of 0.03-0.035 per unit of ln(1 + n) means time is multiplied by (1 + n)^-0.03 to (1 + n)^-0.035. That is 7-8% faster after 10 practices and 13-15% faster after 100. Halving time at that average rate would take on the order of 10^8 or more practice opportunities. The average rate therefore cannot by itself close a twofold gap. The large reductions reported above came from lab tasks with repeated items, consistent sequences or structure training: 68% on the ATC task (§3), about 57% on logic gates over 8,000 trials, 46% on equation solving after perceptual training (§2), and 67% on facts over 25 weeks (§2).
- The field log closest to ordinary varied practice (Deliev 2026, 6-8% median reduction) is of the same order as the logged EdNet/Statics slope. Neither shows what individual learners do, because pooled curves can hide strategy shifts and heterogeneous rates (Delaney 1998; Evans et al. 2018).
- Mechanism matters for transfer. Gains from retrieval are tied to specific items: Logan, Rickard, and the repeated-item training in Anderson et al. 1997, which left learners about 17% slower with 2.5 times the error rate on new items (my arithmetic). Gains from compiled or strengthened shared components carry to new tasks to the extent the components are shared (editors 61-105%; Elio 1986). Varied problems of one type can only benefit through the second route.
- Some step costs persist after very long practice (accessing held information in working memory about 800 ms; negation and verification steps in Carlson et al. 1989). Not every slow step converges on retrieval-like speed.

- Neves & Anderson (1981, knowledge compilation chapter) not read; it is referred to only in the two technical reports above. Anderson's (1982) power-law derivation was not in the extracted text. Logan (1992), Rickard (1999), Compton & Logan (1991), Palmeri (1997), Haider & Frensch (2002), Frensch & Geary (1993), Charness & Campbell (1988), Elio (1986, primary), Blessing & Anderson (1996, JEP: LMC), Sweller, Mawer & Ward (1983) and Kotovsky, Hayes & Simon (1985) were not reached (no open copy found in this pass).

## 2. Training methods that raise speed on complex cognitive skills, measured later

### Takeaway
Three methods showed clear time savings measured after training. Worked examples gave the same test performance with 46-69% less learning time in two classroom chemistry experiments; one secondhand account of algebra reports about half the posttest solution time, an advantage that disappeared on varied problems. Perceptual or structure-recognition training in algebra cut equation-solving time by 32-46% with no accuracy change, and the gain held at 3 weeks. Variable-priority (emphasis-change) training showed TR 1.27, g +0.74 in a meta-analytic report. Part-task training overall carried a small cost (TR 0.87, g -0.06), larger when concurrent subtasks were split apart and absent when sequential subtasks were segmented. Component-fluency evidence is mostly correlational or small-N, and is disputed when the amount of practice is controlled. Specific performance goals, and fast-paced practice early in learning a complex task, did not raise mean performance and in some reports impaired learning; goals introduced later in training helped. No study tested speed drills on algebra manipulation or derivative rules and then measured transfer to whole calculus problems.

### Cited Findings

**Part-task and whole-task training; segmentation and fractionation**
- Wickens, Hutchins, Carolan & Cumming meta-analysis, as reported in a DTIC document attributed to it (document identity flagged as unconfirmed in `above_exam_difficulty.md`; the increasing-difficulty results are there and not repeated here). Part-task training: 22 studies, 65 part- vs whole-task contrasts (65 transfer ratios, 35 Hedges' g). Overall "a 13% cost for part-task training" (TR = 0.87, g = -0.06). Sequential subtasks: neither cost nor benefit. Concurrent (time-shared) subtasks: "a strong cost" (TR = 0.71, g = -0.35). Moderators: smaller costs for more difficult tasks (TR = 0.83, g = -0.10) than less difficult ones (TR = 0.60, g = -0.49); experienced trainees g = -0.93 vs novices g = -0.25 (both TR = 0.84); instructor present TR = 0.85, g = -0.43 vs absent TR = 1.11, g = +0.40. Wightman & Lintern's (1985) psychomotor review is summarized as finding part-task training succeeded "only if the sub-tasks were performed sequentially" and "had limited empirical support overall". [Meta; Part: about 81% read] — [DTIC ADA602128](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA602128.pdf); [journal DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720812451994)
- Fabiani, Buckley, Gratton, Coles & Donchin (1989), Acta Psychologica 71:259-299, Space Fortress, 33 participants: a hierarchical part-task regime, which trained subtasks building an optimal strategy, gave the best performance. An integrated regime, which kept the whole game but emphasized components through instructions and feedback, scored below the hierarchical group, above the whole-task controls, and was the most resistant to interference from concurrent tasks. [Lab, 3 regimes; Abs; hours not on record] — [USF record](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/301)
- Kanfer & Ackerman (1990 final report, AFOSR), ATC task with U.S. Air Force trainees: part-task training alone raised Trial 1 performance "an average of 42%" above a no-training control (summarizing their earlier experiment). [Quasi/Lab (field lab); Part] — [DTIC ADA224569](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA224569.pdf)
- Ayres (2006), Applied Cognitive Psychology 20:287-298: 13-year-olds learned a multi-element mathematical task under Isolated (part-task, elements separated), Integrated (whole-task) or Mixed (part then whole) conditions. Part-tasks lowered cognitive load for all students but helped learning only for low-prior-knowledge students; higher-knowledge students learned more from whole tasks; Mixed was ineffective at both levels. No time measures. [Lab/classroom experiment; Abs; N not stated] — [UNSWorks record](https://unsworks.unsw.edu.au/entities/publication/12ef785d-783e-4c60-9cdc-a3e8126c7805/full)

[Lab; Abs; N not stated] — [Illinois record](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/training-for-attentional-control-in-dual-task-settings-a-comparis/)
- Boot et al. (2010), Acta Psychologica 135(3): Space Fortress, Variable Priority vs Full Emphasis Training; variable priority "accelerated learning and maximized task mastery", but evidence of broader transfer was weak and limited mainly to tasks most similar to the game. **Speed practice on sub-skills and transfer to whole problems**
- Kellman et al. (2008), Pragmatics & Cognition, algebra perceptual learning module (PLM): practice classifying algebraic transformations. Outcome is a separate test of actually solving equations. Exp 2: 30 Algebra I students (13 ninth, 17 eighth graders) in an independent school system; two days of PLM use at 40-45 min/day. Equation-solving accuracy went from 74.5% to 76.9% (n.s., F(1,26) = 2.816, p = .105); mean solution time went from 24.7 s to 13.2 s (a 46% drop); the delayed test covered only 5 students. Exp 3: 83 students (grades 8-11), 2-3 days of PLM use; solving time fell about 32% from pretest to posttest with no accuracy change and was "preserved" at a 3-week delayed posttest. Authors: "Practice in structure extraction and recognition of algebraic transformations also transferred" to solving. The PLM's speed criteria are covered in `speed_mastery_goals.md`. [Quasi (pre/post, no control group described in the read portion); Part: about 77% read] — [Kellman et al. 2008 PDF](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2025/09/kellman_et_al_2008.pdf)
- Pegg, Graham & Bellert (2005), PME 29 proceedings, QuickSmart: 12 persistently low-achieving pupils aged 11-13 (6 Year 5, 6 Year 7) at two rural New South Wales schools. Pairs worked with an instructor for five half-hour sessions per fortnight over about 25 weeks: modelled strategies, retrieval-strategy discussion, and timed independent practice. Addition response time fell from about 5.2 to 1.7 s (Year 5); multiplication from about 2.6 to 1.15 s (Year 7). PATMaths percentile ranks rose for all Year 5 and 5 of 6 Year 7 pupils (up to 63 points; paired t = 2.49, p < .05); gains were maintained at 12 months.   - Meta-analytic ranges: .52-.82 for goal difficulty and .42-.80 for specific difficult vs do-best goals.

  - On complex tasks, do-best sometimes yielded better strategies than specific difficult goals (Earley et al. 1989), while a specific difficult learning goal beat do-best (Winters & Latham 1996).
  - Tight deadlines produced a faster work pace than loose ones in lab and field studies, and goals plus feedback work better than goals alone.

- Kanfer & Ackerman (1988 final report, AFOSR-TR-88-0684): three experiments with 1,010 U.S. Air Force trainees on the ATC task. Exp 1: 316 enlisted basic trainees, goal condition assigned by flight (149 Early Goal, 167 No Goal); a score goal of 1,900 points per trial (about the 90th percentile in pilot data) on Trials 2-4, then "do your best". Feedback was requested only about 4.6 times across the three goal trials. [Cluster-assigned field-lab experiment; Part] — [DTIC ADA196450](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA196450.pdf)
- Kanfer & Ackerman (1990 final report) summary of the series:
  - Early goals (Trials 2-4) "exerted no effects on mean performance".
  - Goals in the intermediate stage (Trials 5-7) "enhanced task performance", more for lower-ability learners.
  - Declarative part-task training plus goals helped, especially for lower-ability learners; procedural part-task training plus goals hurt, with the larger harm for lower-ability learners.

  [Field-lab experiments; Part] — [DTIC ADA224569](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA224569.pdf)
- Gilliland & Landis (1992), J Applied Psychology 77(5):672-681: 160 undergraduates, a computer stock-selection task, with task difficulty, quantity-goal difficulty and quality-goal difficulty manipulated. Quantity goals affected performance quantity; quality showed a three-way interaction; several effects on search strategy. The record does not state the direction of any quantity-quality trade-off. [Lab; Abs] — [Arizona record](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/quality-and-quantity-goals-in-a-complex-decision-task-strategies-/)

### Inferences
- The methods with measured time savings work in different ways: worked examples replace search with study of solutions, structure-recognition practice trains seeing problem structure, and variable-priority practice trains allocating attention within the whole task. Methods that added load or time pressure early (fast-paced practice, performance goals before the task is understood, procedural part-task training plus goals) did not help acquisition, or helped only later.
- Splitting a task into parts costs most when the parts must later be time-shared (concurrent subtasks g = -0.35). Multi-step math problems are mainly sequential, which is the case that showed no cost and no benefit. This is an extrapolation from mostly perceptual-motor training studies.
- Evidence that component speed transfers to composite performance in math is (a) correlational and strongest for composite speed rather than composite accuracy (r = .745 vs .252; Lin & Kubina), (b) quasi-experimental with very small N (QuickSmart, n = 12), or (c) quasi-experimental pre/post for algebra structure training (Kellman, n = 30 and 83, no control group described in the portion read). The one RCT that isolated fact retrieval found no transfer to algebra (Fuchs 2009).
- Worked-example time savings are measured as learning time to equal outcomes (McLaren; Schwonke). The only report of faster posttest solving (Sweller & Cooper 1985, secondhand) vanished on varied problems, and one study found conventional problem solving faster at posttest (Tarmizi & Sweller 1988).

### Gaps
- Not reached: Gopher, Weil & Siegel (1989) and Gopher, Weil & Bareket (1994; the only record found had no abstract); Frederiksen & White (1989); Wightman & Lintern (1985) primary; Johnson & Layng (1992); Singer-Dudek & Greer (2005); Mayfield & Chase (2002, JABA: PMC returned a CAPTCHA and Europe PMC was rate-limited); Cooper & Sweller (1987) and Sweller & Cooper (1985) primaries; Zhu & Simon (1987: only a repository record, which does not state the often-cited "three-year course in two years" result); Gerjets, Scheiter & Catrambone (2004: HAL copy blocked by bot protection); Carroll (1994) primary (only a 1995 practitioner reprint without numbers was found).
- A PMC review of training principles (PMC3928555) returned a CAPTCHA, and Europe PMC was rate-limited twice; not read.
- No study of speed drills on algebra manipulation, derivative rules or other calculus sub-skills measured whole-problem time afterwards. No study measured later timed-exam completion after any of these training methods.

## 3. Individual differences in speed-up: convergence or divergence, ability, processing speed, and strategy differences in slow-but-accurate solvers

### Takeaway
On consistent tasks, differences between people shrank with practice: between-subject variability fell about 34% on average across 24 short experiments, and 77% on the ATC task. General ability predicted early performance more than late performance. In large tutor datasets learning rates on correctness looked similar across students, but that estimate roughly doubled in variability when the fit was restricted to shorter practice sequences. Speed and ability are separate person traits whose correlation ranged from -.25 to .30 across ability tests. Processing speed improved with targeted training in children, without transfer to reasoning. Where strategies were measured, slow-but-accurate performers mostly used slower strategies by choice rather than executing the same steps slowly: adults who retrieve less when free to choose, accurate children who still count, older adults who avoid retrieval, and decision-makers who set excess caution. Excess caution moved toward the reward-optimal setting with practice and feedback.

### Cited Findings

**Convergence and ability-performance relations (Ackerman; Kanfer & Ackerman)**
- Ackerman (1989), "Individual Differences in Learning and Cognitive Abilities", ONR final report 89-01:
  - Review of 24 short experiments: between-subject variability fell about 34% on average. The report: "changes in variability crucially depend on the consistency of the task information processing demands"; for inconsistent tasks, variability stayed constant or increased.
  - Exp 1: 100 University of Minnesota students, 810 trials of a nine-choice reaction-time task; between-subject SD fell 51%.
  - Exp 2: 65 students, ATC task for 180 minutes; RT 16.20 s (SD 9.20) at the start vs 5.11 s (SD 2.07) by session 6; between-subject SD fell 77%.
  - Exp 3: 103 recruited, 86 analysed; ATC between-subject SD fell from 8.82 to 1.35 s; nine-choice RT 909 ms (SD 158) to 653 ms (SD 101).
  - Theory: general-ability correlations fall with practice, perceptual-speed correlations rise then fall, and psychomotor correlations rise.

### Inferences
- Convergence is the documented pattern on consistent tasks, and divergence or no change on inconsistent ones (Ackerman). Multi-step math problems that vary in surface and structure mix both, so the literature does not predict whether initially slow students close the gap on such problems.
- In the datasets where strategy was measured (simple arithmetic, simple decisions), slow-but-accurate performance mostly reflected a strategy or a caution setting: retrieving less, counting, avoiding retrieval, or using a high threshold. In those tasks memory-access speed itself was similar across groups (Hecht 2006). The setting moved with practice plus explicit feedback in perceptual decisions (Evans & Brown) and with practice alone in Balci et al. It did not move for children's counting in a short practice study (Hopkins & Russo).
- Speed and accuracy are separate traits (van der Linden), so a high untimed score says little about speed. Low timed-untimed correlations in Goldhammer & Kröhne show that the same items under a time limit can rank people differently.
- The "regularity" in learning rate concerns correctness, not speed. No equivalent analysis of speed-up rates across students was found.

## 4. Automaticity of sub-skills and complex problem solving: does automating component steps free working memory and raise accuracy or speed on harder problems?

### Takeaway
At the level of single steps, practice that converts computation to retrieval cuts working-memory involvement to minimal levels and removes vulnerability to pressure. In one dataset, though, holding and accessing information in working memory still cost about 800 ms after extensive practice. On whole problems, structure-recognition fluency halved algebra equation-solving time without changing accuracy. Correlational studies link component speed strongly to composite speed and weakly to composite accuracy. Across grade 1 to college, faster fact retrievers scored higher on math tests (secondhand). Causal tests of fact fluency transferring to algebra were null, and no study tested whether algebra or derivative-rule fluency causally changes calculus accuracy or speed.

### Cited Findings
- Tronsky (2005), Memory & Cognition 33:927-940: complex multiplication (e.g., 3 × 18) practised pre/post with strategy reports and dual tasks. Before practice, participants mostly used a mental multidigit algorithm with high working-memory load, heavier for non-retrieval strategies. After practice they used retrieval almost exclusively, and "practice reduced working memory involvement to minimal levels"; automaticity level was unrelated to working-memory load. Participant variability, problem automaticity level and error rates predicted latencies. [Lab, pre/post; Abs; N and population not stated] — [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03193086)
- Beilock, Kulp, Holt & Carr (2004): high-working-memory-demand problems practised to retrieval (50 repetitions) did not choke under pressure; rarely practised ones did (see `timed_practice.md` §4). [Lab; Abs] — [PubMed 15584808](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15584808/)
- Carlson, Sullivan & Schneider (1989): irrelevant memory loads had little effect (about 41 ms), but accessing a held memory set to make a judgment cost about 800 ms at both practice levels (336 and 1,232 trials per gate), declining only slightly. The authors argue for "a distributed view of working memory". 8 undergraduates. [Lab; Full] — [Carlson et al. 1989 PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/1989-carlson.pdf)
- Anderson (1982): declarative interpretation's working-memory demands cause novice errors and slowdowns; proceduralization reduces the load (pp. 381-384). Kanfer & Ackerman's resource account: self-regulation draws on the same limited attention, so it lowers performance while a task is novel and demanding, and can improve it once the skill is proceduralized and spare capacity exists. [Theory; Part] — [Anderson 1982 PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/iq/1982-anderson-2.pdf); [DTIC ADA196450](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA196450.pdf)
- Ayres (2000), MERGA 23 proceedings: 62 Grade 8 students at a Sydney high school, streamed into three ability groups, each computed one designated operation in bracket-expansion problems (e.g., -3(-4 - 5x) - 2(-3x - 4)). [Obs/within-subject; Full] — [Ayres 2000 PDF](https://merga.net.au/wp-content/uploads/DOCS/common/Uploaded%20files/Annual%20Conference%20Proceedings/2000%20Annual%20Conference%20Proceedings/RP_Ayres_2000.pdf)

Fluency or speed was not separated from mastery. [Obs (national survey); Abs; N not on page] — [NCTM record](https://nctm.org/Publications/Journal-for-Research-in-Mathematics-Education/2018/Vol49/Issue3/The-Path-to-College-Calculus_-The-Impact-of-High-School-Mathematics-Coursework)
- QuickSmart (Pegg et al. 2005): faster fact retrieval accompanied PATMaths gains in 12 low-achieving pupils (see §2). [Quasi; Full] — [ERIC ED496946](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED496946.pdf)
- Null or no-transfer results (Fuchs 2009, no transfer from fact retrieval to algebra; Curry 2012, no relation in grade 8), and the WWC panel's claim that automatic retrieval frees "mental energy" for multistep procedures: see `timed_practice.md` §2. [RCT-bundle / Obs / Theory] — [ERIC EJ861181](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ861181); [ERIC ED547205](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED547205); [WWC 2021 guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC2021006-Math-PG.pdf)

### Inferences
- Automating a step frees working memory for that step (Tronsky; Beilock; Anderson). Whether that freed capacity raises accuracy on harder problems was not shown by any causal study found. The positive whole-problem result (Kellman) concerns speed with unchanged accuracy.
- In multi-step algebra, errors cluster on particular operations (Ayres 2000: double negatives with an x-term), consistent with step-specific load. No study measured whether speeding those steps lowers their error rate.
- Component speed and composite speed are tightly linked (r = .745), which is consistent with the speed of a whole problem depending partly on the speed of its steps. The weak link to composite accuracy suggests that component speed does not drive composite accuracy in that sample.
- The calculus-specific evidence found is about preparation (mastery of algebra and precalculus), not speed; the speed dimension is untested at the college level.

### Gaps
- Not reached: Tronsky & Royer (2002 chapter); Cumming & Elkins (1999; publisher page returned a client error); Royer et al. (1999) abstract (PubMed CAPTCHA); Price, Mazzocco & Ansari (2013; J Neurosci permission prompt not answered); Tolar, Lederberg & Fletcher (2009; no record found); Hieb et al. (2015); Cooper & Sweller (1987, rule automation); Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas (1998; course URL too long to fetch); Kotovsky, Hayes & Simon (1985).
- No study was found that tests whether algebra-manipulation or derivative-rule fluency predicts or causes faster or more accurate calculus problem solving.

## 5. Extra time and scores for students without disabilities; how speeded college math exams are; share of students limited by time

### Takeaway
For students without disabilities, extra time raised scores in most experiments, usually by small amounts on standardized tests: about 7 GRE-Q points with 1.5x time, under 30 SAT-M points with fewer items per section, and ES .43 on GRE essays at 1.5x. One 1999 meta-analysis reported +.30 SD. A 2004 ETS summary states that more time does "not significantly improve" scores. The differential-boost (interaction) hypothesis, that extra time helps students with disabilities more, was judged "on shaky ground". The Sireci, Scarpati & Li (2005) RER article and Lu & Sireci (2007) were not reached. No direct measurement of how time-limited U.S. college calculus exams are was found. The proxies:
- calculus final exams average about 25 items, 87% of them recall or procedure;
- engineering exams used 87% of the allotted time on average;
- 85.9% of English-native GMAT examinees finished the quantitative section;
- among accommodated exams at two Canadian universities, 35% used none of the extra time and 85% finished within 50% more time.

### Cited Findings

**Extra-time experiments and reviews**
- National Research Council (2004), Keeping Score for All, chapter 5:
  - The interaction hypothesis is "on shaky ground", and students without disabilities typically benefit, "particularly the accommodation of extended time" (citing Sireci et al. 2003, p. 48). Sireci et al. propose a weaker version in which targeted groups improve more than others.
  - Sireci et al. found that "all student groups" gained under accommodation. Thompson et al. (2002) counted four extended-time studies with a positive effect on scores and three with no significant effect.
  - Zuriff (2000, five studies): gains for students both with and without disabilities. Runyan (1991): greater gains for students with learning disabilities.
  - Schulte et al. (2001, extra time plus oral administration): medium effects for students with disabilities (0.40-0.80) vs small for others (under 0.40).
  - SAT studies found students with learning disabilities who got extended time had college grades below what their scores predicted, more so with more time. Willingham et al. (1988): longer time "may raise scores beyond the level appropriate to compensate for the disability".
  - No study Ns are given.
  
  [Review (expert committee); Full chapter page] — [NAP chapter](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/11029/chapter/7)
- First-pass and `timed_practice.md` findings, not repeated: an ETS GRE RCT (2003 report; N = 15,948; about +7 quantitative points with 1.5x time); Bridgeman, Trapani & Curley 2004 SAT (math gains under 30 points, none for scorers at 400 or below); an ETS 2005 SAT extended-time study (authors not shown in the abstract read; effects larger in math, little gain for low ability, "too much" time may hurt); Chiu & Pearson 1999 meta-analysis via the NCEO review (+.30 SD for students without disabilities); Portolese et al. 2016 (50% more time on online quizzes, no gain). Both high- and low-statistics-anxiety students scored higher untimed, with a larger benefit for high-anxiety students. No numbers on the record. - Under time limits, scores mix speed and ability: speed-ability correlations change sign across tests (van der Linden 2009), and timed and untimed scores on the same items correlated weakly unless time on task was modelled (Goldhammer & Kröhne 2014); see §3. ### Inferences
- The experimental evidence gives a consistent direction (extra time helps students without disabilities, modestly) on admissions tests designed to be mostly unspeeded. These tests are built so that most examinees finish (for example 85.9% finishing GMAT Quant), which caps how much extra time can add. Course exams are not built to that rule, and no study measured their speededness directly.
- The archival accommodation data (35% using none of the extra time; 85% within +50%) and the engineering-exam data (87% of time used on average) suggest that, for most students in those settings, time was not the binding constraint. Neither dataset identifies the subgroup that is accurate but slow.
- The premise "above 90% with twice the time" was not tested by any source found. The closest data (Powers & Fowles: slow writers gained no more from extra time than fast writers) concern essay writing, not math.

### Gaps
- Not reached: Sireci, Scarpati & Li (2005, RER), known here only through the NRC summary of Sireci et al.'s 2003 report; Lu & Sireci (2007, EM:IP; no open copy found); Stretch & Osborne (2005, PARE; Anubis bot protection); Gregg & Nelson (2012) meta-analysis; Cohen, Gregg & Deng (2005); Elliott & Marquart (2004).
- No data on the share of students who do not finish typical U.S. college calculus exams, on calculus exam durations, or on score gains from extra time in college math courses.
- A Remedial and Special Education record on postsecondary extended-time use (DOI 10.1177/0741932520972787) returned a client error.

## 6. Speed-accuracy emphasis during learning: does instructing or pressuring for speed during practice lower accuracy or later learning, and does the order (accuracy first, then speed) matter?

### Takeaway
In two implicit sequence-learning experiments (perceptual-motor tasks), speed vs accuracy instructions during training changed performance: the speed group was faster, the accuracy group near error-free. They did not change the knowledge measured later under equal instructions (Bayes factors about 0.3 favouring no difference). In a dynamic decision task, practice at a fast pace or with added load produced no practice-to-test gain on a fast test, while slow-paced practice did, especially for lower-ability learners. Time pressure in arithmetic shifted adults toward retrieval on simple facts, or toward simpler, error-prone strategies on equations and word problems. Early performance goals in complex skill learning did not raise mean performance and, per one review, interfered with knowledge acquisition; later goals helped. Accuracy-biased (too slow) decision settings were the default and moved toward optimal with practice and feedback. No study compared accuracy-first-then-speed sequencing with speed-from-the-start on multi-step math.

### Cited Findings

**Instructions emphasizing speed or accuracy during skill learning**
- Vékony, Marossy, Must, Vécsei, Janacsek & Nemeth (2020), Cerebral Cortex Communications 1(1):tgaa041 (read as the bioRxiv preprint "Skill learning can be independent of speed and accuracy instructions"): 61 healthy undergraduates in Budapest (accuracy group n = 31, speed group n = 30) did the Alternating Serial Reaction Time task. Instructions differed over Epochs 1-4; after a 10-minute rest, Epoch 5 used equal instructions.
  - During training the speed group was faster (F(1,59) = 51.84) and the accuracy group more accurate (F(1,59) = 117.60); the speed group's accuracy declined across epochs.
  - RT-based statistical learning did not differ by group during training (triplet × group F(1,59) = 0.48, BF10 = 0.32) or at test (F(1,59) = 0.27, BF10 = 0.29).
  - Accuracy-based learning showed only in the speed group during training (F(1,59) = 45.25) and did not differ at test (BF10 = 0.37).
  - Explicit knowledge was similar and largely implicit.
  - The authors caution that the result may be specific to implicit probabilistic learning.
  
  [Lab, two groups; Full (preprint)] — [bioRxiv PDF](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/726315.full.pdf); [Greenwich record](https://gala.gre.ac.uk/id/eprint/30821)
- Vékony, Pleche, Pesthy, Janacsek & Nemeth (2022), npj Science of Learning 7:27: on a cued probabilistic sequence-learning task, speed instructions enhanced the expression of probability-based knowledge but not serial-order knowledge during learning. After a retention period with equal instructions, acquired knowledge was comparable for both. The accuracy group reached near-ceiling accuracy, "suggesting that response errors are not needed for procedural learning". [Lab; Abs; N not on page] — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/17f51719017146909604889521511d9c); [Greenwich record](https://gala.gre.ac.uk/id/eprint/37898/)

**Time pressure during practice or problem solving**
- Gonzalez (2005): fast-paced or loaded practice gave no practice-to-test gain on a fast test; slow-paced practice did, most for low-Raven participants (51 university students; see §2). [Lab; Full] — [Gonzalez 2005 PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/ddmlab/papers/GonzalezHumanFactorsMarch2005.pdf)
- Caviola, Carey, Mammarella & Szucs (2017), Frontiers in Psychology 8:1488, review of 11 studies that manipulated time in math or reasoning:
  - McNeil et al. (2010; 184 undergraduates; equations a + b + c = d + __ shown for 1,000-1,500 ms): under time pressure adults used the add-all and add-to-equal strategies typical of children.
  - Gillard et al. (2009; 167 undergraduates in Study 1): a 17-second deadline, or a working-memory load, increased incorrect use of the proportional heuristic on non-proportional word problems.
  - Campbell & Austin (2002; 48 undergraduates): a fast deadline increased retrieval and reduced procedures for large simple-addition problems.
  - Luwel & Verschaffel (2003; 81 sixth graders): children adapted their strategies to time limits.
  - Kellogg et al. (1999) is described as 30 undergraduates with the timed condition set at 70% of each page's mean response time.
  - Review: "It is not clear whether time pressure interferes with strategy selection or whether it simply renders the optimal strategy impossible, due to an overload of working memory resources." It does not compare time pressure during learning with time pressure during testing.
  
  [Review of Lab studies; Full] — [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01488/full)
- Jazbutis et al. (2023): timed practice of unfamiliar times-table facts did not raise later accuracy; timed learners were less accurate on the immediate test, and later speed effects depended on spacing (211 adults). See `timed_practice.md` §1. [RCT; Full] — [Jazbutis 2023](https://jnc.psychopen.eu/index.php/jnc/article/view/7721)

**Speed or performance goals early vs later in learning**
- Kanfer & Ackerman reports (1988, 1990): goals on Trials 2-4 "exerted no effects on mean performance"; goals on Trials 5-7 "enhanced task performance", more for lower-ability learners; procedural part-task training plus goals hurt performance, more for lower-ability learners (U.S. Air Force trainees, ATC task; see §2). [Field-lab experiments; Part] — [DTIC ADA196450](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA196450.pdf); [DTIC ADA224569](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA224569.pdf)

[Review; Full] — [Locke & Latham 2002 PDF](https://goal-lab.psych.umn.edu/orgpsych/2020/readings/5.%20Motivation/Locke%20&%20Latham%20(2002).pdf)

**Default caution and its change with practice**
- Evans & Brown (2017): excess caution (too slow) by default; near-optimal settings within about an hour with practice and detailed feedback (see §3). Balci et al. (2011): accuracy bias early, greatly diminished with practice (see §3). [Lab; Full / Abs] — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-016-1135-1); [Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/acquisition-of-decision-making-criteria-reward-rate-ultimately-be/)

**Accuracy-first, then speed (sequencing)**
- Most timed-practice studies admitted students only after about 80% accuracy on the practiced items, and the WWC panel advises timing only after a concept is learned. Both are expert or inclusion practice, and no study varied the order (`timed_practice.md` §3). Fluency (rate) training vs accuracy-only training on component and composite skills: Bucklin et al. 2000 (`timed_practice.md` §2). Benefits of rate building beyond practice amount were judged sparse by Doughty et al. 2004 (§2). [Meta preprint / Theory / Lab / Review] — [Peltier 2024 preprint](https://doi.org/10.35542/osf.io/9bjtg); [WWC 2021 guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC2021006-Math-PG.pdf); [ERIC EJ629800](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ629800); [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF03392086)

**Motor-learning contrast (labelled; not cognitive-skill evidence)**
- Both Vékony studies use a perceptual-motor serial reaction-time task. In Healy & Wohldmann's review of speeded aiming with a reversed mouse (motor), varying the reversal gave little positive transfer (§1). In the Tatel & Ackerman (2025) meta-analysis of mostly motor procedural skills, gains measured as speed decayed more slowly than gains measured as accuracy (about 13 vs 6.5 months to half loss; `curves_time_and_forgetting.md` D2). [Lab / Review / Meta] — [Healy & Wohldmann PDF](https://home.cs.colorado.edu/~mozer/Teaching/syllabi/TopicsInCognitiveScienceSpring2012/HealyWohldmann.pdf); [Tatel & Ackerman 2025 DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000481)

### Inferences
- Across the studies found, speed emphasis during practice reliably changes performance during practice: faster responses, more errors, simpler strategies under deadlines. Where acquired knowledge was tested later under equal instructions (implicit sequence learning), it was unchanged. Where the task required discovering or executing a strategy (dynamic decisions; equations under brief presentation), pressure was associated with poorer learning or poorer strategies.
- The order question remains open: the timing of goals mattered (Kanfer & Ackerman: no benefit early, benefit later), but no study compared accuracy-first with speed-first sequencing on the same multi-step math skill.
- Caution settings (how much evidence a person waits for) are adjustable with practice and feedback in simple decisions. This is the closest experimental analogue found for "accurate but slow" as a setting rather than a capacity limit, and it comes from perceptual tasks, not math.

### Gaps
- No experiment on speed vs accuracy instructions during practice of multi-step math or STEM problems, with later learning measured, was found.
- No motor-learning study that manipulated speed vs accuracy emphasis during practice was reached in this pass (searches returned other designs). Older motor studies of this question were not located.
- Touron, Swaim & Hertzog (2007), on instructions and incentives that reduce older adults' retrieval reluctance, was not read (PMC CAPTCHA; the DukeSpace copy was behind bot protection).
- Starns & Ratcliff (2010), on older adults' caution in decisions, was not read (PMC).
