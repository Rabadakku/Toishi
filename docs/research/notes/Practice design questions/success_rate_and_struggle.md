# Optimal Practice Success Rate and the Limits of Unassisted Struggle in Multi-Step Problem Solving

Scope: facts only, no design recommendations. Evidence labels are mine: **Strong** = peer-reviewed primary source read with full numbers, controlled or randomized design, or large sample in its own domain. **Moderate** = peer-reviewed but correlational, quasi-experimental, small, outside math, or only partly read. **Weak** = preprint, vendor document, poster or secondary summary. **Theory** = derivation or simulation with no new human data. **Abstract only** = only an abstract or summary page was accessed. **Unverified** = a number or claim I could not confirm in a primary source. "First-try success" means a correct answer on the first attempt at an item; where a study defines "success" or "error rate" differently, the definition is stated.

## 1. The "85% rule" (Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen 2019): what it derives, its assumptions, and the evidence for or against it in human learning

### Takeaway
The 85% rule is a derived optimum (training error rate 15.87%, accuracy about 85%) for stochastic-gradient-descent learners on binary tasks with Gaussian noise, supported in the original paper only by simulations; it moves to roughly 82% or 75% accuracy under heavier-tailed noise. The only direct human learning test found (a motor-skill preprint, N = 194) put the best imposed error rate near 30% (about 70% success), and I found no test of the rule in math or multi-step problem solving.

### Cited Findings

#### What the paper derives
- **[Theory]** The abstract states "the optimal error rate for training is around 15.87% or, conversely, that the optimal training accuracy is about 85%", for stochastic-gradient-descent-based learning in binary classification tasks. — [Wilson et al. 2019, Nature Communications 10:4646](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579) (doi 10.1038/s41467-019-12552-4; read via Europe PMC full-text XML)
- **[Theory]** Derivation: the decision variable is h = Δ + n with Gaussian noise of precision β = 1/σ, so error rate ER = F(−βΔ); the learning rate is taken proportional to ∂ER/∂β; maximizing gives ER* = ½(1 − erf(1/√2)) ≈ 0.1587. — [Wilson et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579)
- **[Theory]** ER* does not depend on skill β, but the optimal difficulty Δ* = 1/β does, so difficulty has to be re-tuned online as skill changes in order to hold error at the optimum. — [Wilson et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579)
- **[Theory]** The number depends on the noise distribution: Laplace noise gives optimal error 0.1839 (about 82% accuracy) and Cauchy noise 0.25 (75% accuracy). The paper says "from 85% for Gaussian, to 82% for Laplacian noise, to 75% for Cauchy noise", and for Cauchy noise "while the rate of learning is still greatest at the optimal difficulty, the improvement is not as dramatic as in the other cases". — [Wilson et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579)
- **[Theory]** Fixed error rate vs fixed difficulty: with error held fixed, precision grows in proportion to √t; with fixed difficulty (Gaussian case) β grows in proportion to log t, described by the authors as "exponentially slower". — [Wilson et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579)
- **[Theory, simulation only]** Simulations: a perceptron (error rates 0.01 to 0.5; 1000 simulations x 1000 trials); a two-layer network on MNIST parity and magnitude tasks (5000 trials; error clamped between 5% and 30%; about a 10-point test-accuracy gap between the best and worst clamped rates); and the Law & Gold model of monkey random-dot-motion perceptual learning (100 simulations per rate). The data statement reads "no datasets were generated or analysed". — [Wilson et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579)

#### Stated assumptions and limits (authors' words)
- The analysis "ignores the effect of changing ϕ on the form of the noise". — [Wilson et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579)
- "not all models will exhibit a sweet spot of difficulty for learning"; the example given is a Bayesian learner with perfect memory, whose inference does not depend on example order. — [Wilson et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579)
- Batch learning: "If learning is slow, then selecting examples in a batch that satisfy the 85% rule may work, but if learning is fast, then mixing in more difficult examples may be best." — [Wilson et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579)
- On education theory: the work "offers a way of deriving the desired difficulty and the region of proximal learning in the special case of binary classification tasks for which stochastic gradient-descent learning rules apply", is "the first step towards a more mathematical instantiation of these theories", and "remains to be generalized to a broader class of circumstances, such as multi-choice tasks and different learning algorithms". — [Wilson et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579)
- "to the best of our knowledge no formal theoretical work has addressed the effect of training accuracy on learning, a test of which is an important direction for future work." Psychophysics staircasing procedures "are commonly designed to produce about 80–85% accuracy". — [Wilson et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579)

#### Human evidence
- **[Secondary; primary papers not read]** Wilson et al. cite human work as consistent with an intermediate-difficulty optimum: Vuorre & Metcalfe 2016 (subjective flow peaks on tasks rated intermediate in difficulty), Bauer et al. 2016 (self-reported "optimal difficulty" in a brain-computer-interface neurofeedback task peaks at the difficulty associated with maximal learning), and Baranes et al. 2014 (participants choose intermediate difficulty). — [Wilson et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579)
- **[Weak · preprint, not peer reviewed; adults; motor skill]** Al-Fawakhiri, Kayani & McDougle (bioRxiv 2023) extended the derivation to a one-dimensional motor task, predicting an optimal error rate of 31.73% ("an optimal training schedule should aim to maintain performance at a 68.27% success rate"). They imposed error rates of 15%, 30%, 50% or 65% in a modified Pong task (N = 194; 20-trial calibration, 10 baseline trials, 70 training trials). The 30% group had the largest Learning Index: vs 15% group t(89)=3.11, p=.003, d=0.65; vs 65% group t(87)=1.58, p=.12, d=0.33; vs 50% group t(94)=0.98, p=.32, d=0.20 (not significant). Re-binning by achieved error rate (sextiles), the best sextile (31.66% error) beat the 13.57% (d=0.83, p=.002), 23.09% (d=0.65, p=.02), 52.01% (d=0.59, p=.03) and 61.73% (d=0.66, p=.02) sextiles but not the 44.17% sextile (d=0.37, p=.15). Model-predicted vs observed learning r=0.19, p=0.01; baseline success averaged 77% (SD 17%). — [Al-Fawakhiri et al. 2023](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.07.19.549705)
- **[Moderate · N = 36; motor skill]** Bootsma, Hortobágyi, Rothwell & Caljouw 2018 (Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise 50(9):1842–1849): mirror star-tracing at low, medium or hard difficulty; "Task difficulty affected motor performance, but did not affect motor learning and transfer". Not a direct test of the 85% rule, and it does not support a single optimal challenge point. — [Bootsma et al. 2018](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10056012/)
- **[Abstract only · engagement outcomes, not learning]** Koskinen et al. 2022 (N = 167 Finnish fifth-graders, fractions game): downward difficulty adaptation raised situational interest and substantial upward adaptation lowered it. — [Koskinen et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2022.104694). Ramos Muñoz et al. 2022 (N = 2,581, stroke rehabilitation): greatest perseverance with moderate impairment and "high but not perfect success" in week 1. — [Ramos Muñoz et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2022.896298). Both are works that cite Wilson et al.

### Inferences
- The 85% value is the output of one model (binary choice, Gaussian noise, gradient-descent learner) and the paper itself shows it moves with the noise distribution (75% to 85%); it is not an empirical constant.
- In a binary task chance accuracy is 50%; on open-ended multi-step problems chance first-try success is near zero, so the same accuracy number does not correspond to the same relative difficulty (reasoning from the model setup; not tested in any source found).
- The one human learning test (N = 194, motor) had its best group at about 30% error (about 70% success): significantly better than the 15% error group, not significantly different from the 50% group, and its own two-sided-error theory predicted 68.27% success rather than 85%. Together with the non-significant 44% sextile this points to an intermediate, fairly flat optimum rather than a sharp peak.
- Neither the derivation nor the human test measures delayed retention, transfer or multi-step reasoning; the model's "learning rate" is the speed at which the precision of a single decision improves.

### Gaps
- No test of the 85% rule, or of any specific optimal success rate, on math or multi-step problem solving was found.
- No peer-reviewed human replication of the 15/30/50/65% result found; I did not check whether the preprint has since been published.
- Vuorre & Metcalfe 2016, Bauer et al. 2016 and Baranes et al. 2014 were not read; Koskinen 2022 and Ramos Muñoz 2022 were read at abstract level only.

## 2. What do empirical studies that varied practice difficulty or success rate show about learning?

### Takeaway
No experiment was found that varied first-try success on multi-step problems in high school or college math and measured delayed learning. Adjacent evidence points in different directions depending on the outcome: delayed recall peaked when practice was scheduled at 86–94% predicted recall (adults, paired associates, time costs counted); Math Garden's easiest pre-set success rate produced the largest arithmetic improvement; harder number-line variants produced faster learning curves but less engagement; and self-reported "appropriate" difficulty peaked at about 65–75% real success.

### Cited Findings

#### Retrieval-practice and scheduling-threshold experiments
- **[Strong in its context · randomized between-subjects experiment; adults on Mechanical Turk; Japanese–English paired associates]** Eglington & Pavlik (2020) used a memory model to practice, on each trial, the item closest below a predicted-recall threshold (the "optimal efficiency threshold", OET), and compared OET 40, 70, 86, 94 and 98 with a conventional schedule repeating items about every 15 trials (S15), with a final test 3 days later. 322 recruited, 291 analysed (cell n: S15 59; OET 40 53; OET 70 57; OET 86 50; OET 94 56; OET 98 16); each had 30 word pairs and a 22-minute session. Final memory followed "a skewed inverted-U shaped pattern": Kruskal–Wallis H(4)=16.5, p<0.01; OET 86 and OET 94 were significantly higher than OET 40, OET 98 and S15; OET 86 (M=0.48) vs OET 94 (M=0.47) p=0.64; no significant differences among OET 70, 86 and 94 (p>0.17); quadratic term LR χ²=23.47, p<0.001; OET 94 gave "approximately 40% higher memory retention" than S15 and OET 40; Cohen's d=0.64 vs conventional spacing, "comparable in magnitude to that of the spacing effect itself" (spacing effect d=0.42). — [Eglington & Pavlik 2020, npj Science of Learning](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7567101/)
- **[Strong in its context · simulation]** The OET is a scheduling rule, not the realized success rate: "an OET does not dictate the actual probability of recall for practiced items but is a threshold determining what is practiced (closest item underneath threshold)". In the simulation (200 simulated students per condition) "The best OET in this simulation was 0.94" and "OETs of ≥0.8 provided 39–55% better memory at final test than the next best conventional schedule." — [Eglington & Pavlik 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7567101/)
- **[Strong in its context · authors' interpretation]** The authors attribute the high optimum to time costs: "the optimal zone that we found indicates imposing less difficulty than previously suggested ... because our methodology considered the time costs of difficulty" (failed trials cost additional time; 4 s of feedback in the experiment). They contrast simulations that practiced items near 0.40 recall probability (Khajah et al.) and Bjork's "about to be forgotten" principle, say the OET "will vary across learning materials and types of feedback", and say efficiency matters "unless the learning gains and time costs of successes and failures are equal". — [Eglington & Pavlik 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7567101/)
- **[Abstract only · meta-analysis]** Rowland 2014 (testing effect): effortful processing supports the testing effect, and recall tests give larger benefits than recognition tests. Effect sizes not retrieved. — [Rowland 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559)
- **[Abstract only · experiments]** Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009: unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhance subsequent learning. — [Kornell et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015729)
- **[Abstract only · experiments]** Pashler, Zarow & Triplett 2003: greater lags between tests drastically lowered performance on the second test but dramatically improved performance on a later test, even for items that elicited errors. — [Pashler et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.29.6.1051)
- **[Strong · preregistered cluster RCT; grade 7 math; not a success-rate manipulation]** Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung: 54 seventh-grade classes did interleaved or blocked practice assignments over 4 months; on an unannounced test one month later the interleaved group scored 61% vs 38%, d = 0.83. The abstract does not report practice-phase accuracy. — [Rohrer et al.](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000367)

#### Success-rate manipulations in adaptive practice systems and games
- **[Moderate · field experiment; children; abstract plus a secondary description of the conditions]** Jansen et al. 2013 (Learning and Individual Differences 24:190–197): 207 children (grades 3–6) practiced in Math Garden for six weeks in a control and three experimental conditions with different pre-set success rates (60%, 75% and 90% per Papoušek & Pelánek; the abstract lists no values). "Math performance, however, only improved in the experimental conditions. Moreover, the higher the pre-set success rate, the more problems were attempted, and the larger the improvement in math performance." The authors suggest success leads to more practice; math anxiety improved equally in all conditions. Papoušek & Pelánek summarize it as "the easiest condition led to the best learning (mediated by a number of solved problems)". — [Jansen et al. 2013](https://dare.uva.nl/id/789fc422-4935-49ca-bcba-803239522951); [Papoušek & Pelánek 2015](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/aied15.pdf)
- **[Moderate · system description; abstract]** Klinkenberg et al. 2011 (Computers & Education 57(2):1813–1824): Math Garden selects items "with a mean success probability of .75, making the tasks challenging yet not too difficult"; 3,648 children and over 3.5 million problems in ten months. The abstract reports no comparison across target rates. — [Klinkenberg et al. 2011](https://dare.uva.nl/id/de65bb65-d198-4dbc-ac7b-bc25ca23e627)
- **[Moderate · large online A/B tests on a geography practice site (slepemapy.cz); outcomes are engagement and self-reported appropriateness, not learning]** Papoušek & Pelánek 2015 (AIED): default target success rate 75% ("similarly to other applications"); in computerized adaptive testing "the optimal success rate is 50%", which gives the most informative answers about skill, but "50% success rate does not seem very encouraging" for practice. Targets ranged 50–95% yet achieved success varied only 65–90%, and there was no statistically significant trend in number of answers vs target. Self-perceived difficulty showed a U-shape against previous real success (more than 1,700,000 answers and 12,000 feedback records): the "Appropriate" peak and equal "Too Easy"/"Too Difficult" votes occurred at 65% real success; in-school users preferred easier questions (optimum around 75%) and were less satisfied overall. Against the algorithm's target (176,000 answers; 1,450 feedback records), maximum satisfaction was at target 60–65%, "which corresponds to the 70% from the perspective of the real success rate". Dynamic adjustment of the target from the last ten answers raised the "Appropriate" ratio (68% vs 63%) and median answers (28 vs 21; p = 0.02) with average success about 75% in both variants. — [Papoušek & Pelánek 2015](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/aied15.pdf)
- **[Moderate · large online A/B tests; grades 4–8 number-line game; engagement plus learning curves]** Lomas et al. 2013 (CHI), Battleship Numberline. Experiment 1 (10,478 game sessions): target size moved average success from 29% (smallest) to 63% (largest); larger targets were more engaging (34% more estimates, 18% more time); the relationship was linear ("the quadratic fit was not significant, p=0.8"), though the easiest conditions still had under 70% success, so "we only measured the left side of the Inverted U" is possible. Experiment 2 (69,642 play sessions; the unit of assignment was the session, not the person; players anonymous): "low levels of challenge (high success rates) never appear to negatively affect player engagement", and "the most engaging design conditions produced the slowest rates of learning". Learning-curve analysis (1,392 players with random sequencing and more than 30 decimal-domain trials; first 30 trials only): larger, easier targets gave a slower learning rate (interaction p = 0.02). Stated limitations: selective attrition, only players with more than 30 trials, and possibly different kinds of learning at different target sizes. — [Lomas et al. 2013](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~brian/463-663/project-part-one/Optimizing%20Challenge.pdf)
- **[Abstract only · engagement]** Lomas et al. 2017 (CHI, "Is Difficulty Overrated?"): "even moderate difficulty can reduce player engagement"; three design experiments (over 20,000 play sessions) tested choice, novelty and suspense as factors that may account for the purported benefits of difficulty. — [Lomas et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3025638)
- **[Abstract only · enjoyment, not learning]** Abuhamdeh & Csikszentmihalyi (Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin): in Internet chess, perceived challenge "strongly predicted enjoyment", games against superior opponents were more enjoyable than against inferior ones, and close games more enjoyable than blowouts; across everyday activities the challenge–enjoyment relation was moderated by activity motivation and activity type. — [Abuhamdeh & Csikszentmihalyi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167211427147)

#### Region of proximal learning (Metcalfe)
- **[Strong for verbal learning by adults; not problem solving]** Metcalfe 2002 (JEP: General 131:349), five experiments on self-regulated study: with more study time, people shift toward more difficult items, and experts study harder items than novices. — [Metcalfe 2002](https://psychology.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/2016-11/349.pdf)
- **[Strong for verbal learning by adults]** Metcalfe & Kornell 2003 (JEP: General 132:530): given free choice, people devote most time to medium-difficulty items and study the easiest first; when study time was experimentally allocated, "best performance resulted when most time was given to the medium-difficulty items". Easy items show steep initial learning with little subsequent increase; medium items show smaller but more sustained gains. — [Metcalfe & Kornell 2003](https://psychology.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/2016-11/530.pdf)
- **[Strong for verbal learning by adults]** Metcalfe & Kornell 2005 (JML 52:463), eight experiments: a stopping rule based on judged rate of learning (jROL); study continues until jROL approaches zero. — [Metcalfe & Kornell 2005](https://psychology.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/2016-11/Metcalfe.Kornell.2005_0.pdf)

#### ZPD operationalizations
- **[Weak · secondary]** Definition as summarized on Wikipedia: the space "between what a learner is capable of doing unsupported and what the learner cannot do even with support"; Vygotsky never used the word "scaffolding" (Bruner, Wood and Ross developed it); Wass & Golding are cited as suggesting that the hardest tasks a student can complete with scaffolding produce the greatest gains (primary not read); the page lists readiness and identifying each student's foundation knowledge as practical difficulties. — [Wikipedia: Zone of proximal development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zone_of_proximal_development)
- **[Weak · vendor white paper]** ALEKS (knowledge space theory) operationalizes it as the "outer fringe", "the set of items the student is 'ready to learn'". — [ALEKS: A Practical Perspective on Knowledge Space Theory](https://www.mheducation.com/content/dam/mhe/research/prek-12/essa-tier-4/aleks-practical-perspective-knowledge-space-theory.pdf)
- **[Moderate · observational platform data; read via a fetch-tool summary, so numbers are as reported by that summary]** Baker, Ma, Zhao, Wang & Ma, "The Results of Implementing Zone of Proximal Development on Learning Outcomes" (venue not stated in the PDF; replicates Zou et al. 2019): tasks labeled Ready-to-Learn (all prerequisites in the knowledge graph mastered), Unready-to-Learn (one or more prerequisites missing) or Already Mastered, on the LearntaRTAD K-12 platform in China (7,913 students; 250,783 task cards; 2019; Math and English). Logistic regression odds ratios for the paper's learning-outcome measure: Ready vs Unready OR = 1.710 (Math) and OR = 7.709 (English), both p < 0.001; Ready vs Already Mastered OR = 0.241 (Math) and 0.185 (English), p < 0.001. These are odds ratios, not risk ratios. — [Baker et al. (Learnta)](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/paper%20115b.pdf)
- **[Weak · secondary and unverified]** Math Academy cites Zou et al. 2019 (AIED) as reporting that a student is "about 3-4x as likely to be successful in mastering that topic if it lies along their knowledge frontier (as opposed to residing beyond their frontier)", and itself states that a student's ZPD "coincides with their knowledge frontier or edge of mastery, the set of new topics for which they have mastered the prerequisites". The Zou et al. primary was not accessed. — [The Math Academy Way](https://justinmath.com/files/the-math-academy-way.pdf)
- **[Abstract only]** Chounta et al. 2017 ("The 'Grey Area'") models the ZPD from a student model's predicted probability of a correct answer during reflective dialogue in a high-school physics natural-language tutor; the "Grey Area" is "the area of uncertainty in which the student model cannot predict with acceptable accuracy" whether the student can answer correctly without support. No dataset size or quantitative results were available. — [Chounta et al. 2017](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-66610-5_1)

#### Adaptive tutoring systems and vendor reports
- **[Weak · vendor draft, non-peer-reviewed]** Math Academy: "we also calibrate the quiz difficulty to make them tougher if you've been doing well on them, because the 80-85% accuracy range is the sweet spot for learning"; quizzes are "timed, fully interleaved, and some forgetting has set in, so it's expected that you might miss some questions". A margin note in the draft says "Reminiscent of The Eighty Five Percent Rule for optimal learning (Wilson et al., 2019)". No published experiment supporting the 80–85% calibration was found in the document. — [The Math Academy Way (working draft)](https://justinmath.com/files/the-math-academy-way.pdf)
- **[Weak · vendor claim]** Math Academy: "our students pass lessons 95% of the time on the first try and 99% of the time within two tries"; "The way we raise pass rates is by introducing more scaffolding into lessons to further reduce cognitive load"; to check that higher pass rates are not just priming it tracks review and quiz performance ("less priming for review tasks, and no priming for quizzes"); about 1000 steps in its calculus course vs about 100 in a typical textbook. — [The Math Academy Way (working draft)](https://justinmath.com/files/the-math-academy-way.pdf)
- **[Moderate · meta-analyses; vendor-hosted abstract for Sun et al.]** ALEKS: Fang et al. 2019 (15 studies, 24 samples): "as good, but not better than, traditional classroom teaching", with shorter use giving greater effects — [Fang et al. 2019](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/facpubs/7254). Sun et al. 2021 (K-12 and higher education; 33 studies, 56 effect sizes, 9,238 students): g = 0.05, 95% CI [−0.01, 0.20] vs traditional instruction; g = 0.43, 95% CI [0.02, 0.83] as a supplement (numbers as printed on the vendor page) — [Sun et al. 2021](https://www.mheducation.com/prek-12/resources/research/library/math/the-effects-of-aleks-on-mathematics-learning-in-k-12-and-higher-education.html)
- **[Strong · RCT; abstract level]** Cognitive Tutor Algebra I at scale (Pane et al. 2014): no first-year effect, positive second-year effect of about 8 percentile points; significant in high schools, not middle schools. — [Pane et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.3102/0162373713507480)
- **[Strong · RCT; abstract level; effect size unverified]** ASSISTments homework tool (Roschelle et al. 2016): 2,850 seventh-graders in 43 Maine schools; the tool with timely feedback and hints significantly raised end-of-year standardized math scores; low prior achievers benefited most. — [Roschelle et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858416673968)
- **[Strong · meta-analyses; general tutoring effects, not difficulty targeting]** Kulik & Fletcher 2016: median effect size 0.66 across 50 evaluations of intelligent tutoring systems — [Kulik & Fletcher 2016](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315581420). VanLehn 2011: human tutoring d = 0.79, ITS d = 0.76 — [VanLehn 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369).

### Inferences
- The "success rate" in these sources is not one quantity: predicted recall probability used as a scheduling threshold (Eglington & Pavlik); probability that a child answers an arithmetic item correctly (Math Garden); probability of a correct answer on a geography item (Papoušek & Pelánek); hit rate on a number-line target (Lomas); imposed motor error rate (Pong); and quiz accuracy (Math Academy). None is first-try success on a multi-step problem.
- Numeric values attached to "good" practice difficulty across sources: about 85% accuracy (Wilson theory), about 68–70% success (Pong preprint), 86–94% OET (paired associates, time-cost adjusted), 75% default target (Math Garden, slepemapy), 65% (peak of "Appropriate" votes), 75% (in-school preference), 80–85% (Math Academy quiz calibration, vendor), and 50% (information-optimal for assessment, per Papoušek & Pelánek). The ones tied to measured learning are the Pong preprint, the OET experiment, the Jansen experiment and the Lomas learning curves; they do not agree.
- Engagement and learning outcomes diverge in Lomas et al. (easier is more engaging, harder yields faster early learning curves), while in Jansen et al. the highest pre-set success rate gave the largest improvement and the authors attribute this to more problems being attempted; the outcome measure and the amount of practice completed change the apparent optimum.
- Eglington & Pavlik's high optimum depends on time costs (failures take longer); the authors state that lower thresholds win when failures yield larger gains or cost less. An OET is analogous to a retention target in a spaced-repetition scheduler, but for multi-step problems the unit is a problem attempt whose first-try correctness is not the same as recall probability (my inference).
- Vendor figures (Math Garden 75%, Math Academy 80–85% and 95% pass rates) are design choices reported without a published comparison against alternative rates in the sources found.

### Gaps
- No experiment varying first-try success in high school or college multi-step math with a delayed learning measure was found.
- The 60/75/90% condition values for Jansen et al. come from Papoušek & Pelánek's description; the Jansen abstract lists none and the Jansen full text was not read.
- Not retrieved: Pyc & Rawson 2009 numbers; Rowland 2014 effect sizes; Zou et al. 2019 primary; Chounta et al. 2017 beyond the abstract; the ASSISTments RCT effect size; Pane et al. sample size.
- MATHia's mastery threshold (Bayesian knowledge tracing criterion) and any Carnegie Learning statement of target success rate were not found.
- The Learnta paper's publication venue and its learning-outcome definition were not available in the fetched text.

## 3. Productive failure and productive struggle: conditions, effect sizes and the role of consolidation

### Takeaway
Problem solving before instruction (productive failure, PF) beats instruction-first on conceptual understanding and transfer in the Sinha & Kapur 2021 meta-analysis (Hedges g = 0.36, 53 studies, 166 comparisons; larger for high-fidelity designs), but PF designs expect students to fail at the generation problem and always add explicit instruction afterward. Unassisted discovery without such guidance shows a negative effect in a large meta-analysis (d = −0.38, 580 comparisons), and guided or enhanced discovery shows a positive one (d = 0.30).

### Cited Findings
- **[Strong · meta-analysis; abstract only, full text blocked]** Sinha & Kapur 2021 (Review of Educational Research): 53 studies, 166 comparisons, problem-solving-then-instruction (PS-I) vs instruction-then-problem-solving (I-PS), Hedges g = 0.36, 95% CI [0.20; 0.51]; effect sizes were higher when PF design principles were followed with high fidelity (g from 0.37 to 0.58); grade level, intervention time span and (quasi-)experimental nature contributed to efficacy; "Contrasting trends were, however, observed for younger age learners (second to fifth graders) and for the learning of domain-general skills, for which effect sizes favored I-PS"; after accounting for publication bias the abstract gives g = 0.87 as the "true effect size". — [Sinha & Kapur 2021 (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105); abstract text via [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105)
- **[Moderate · quasi-experiment; N = 140 ninth-graders; one Singapore all-boys public school; four 55-minute periods]** Kapur, "Productive Failure in Learning the Concept of Variance" (Cognitive Science Society proceedings): PF students spent two periods in triads on one unguided data-analysis problem before direct instruction (DI). PF students "were ultimately unsuccessful in developing the canonical formulation" yet on the posttest were on par with DI on procedural fluency (PF M=7.66 vs DI M=7.98, p=.367) and outperformed DI on data analysis (14.11 vs 11.38, partial η²=.07), conceptual insight (16.40 vs 8.20, partial η²=.27) and transfer (4.93 vs 3.07, partial η²=.10); MANCOVA F(4,134)=16.802, p<.001, partial η²=.33. — [Kapur (CogSci proceedings)](https://escholarship.org/content/qt16f7z2zq/qt16f7z2zq_noSplash_c1ea25a7bef003a69ba2e79f885b1d0a.pdf)
- **[Moderate · three quasi-experiments; ninth-graders]** Kapur & Bielaczyc 2011 (CogSci proceedings, "Classroom-based Experiments in Productive Failure"): Experiment 1 (74 ninth-graders; 39 PF, 35 DI; same teacher): PF on par with DI on procedural fluency and significantly better on data analysis and conceptual insight. Experiment 2 (solutions given to study and evaluate instead of generated): only the effects on conceptual insight and near transfer were significant. Experiment 3 (critical features simply told, no generation): only the effect on conceptual insight remained significant. — [Kapur & Bielaczyc 2011](https://repositories.cdlib.org/content/qt7761h4h2/qt7761h4h2.pdf)
- **[Moderate · design statement]** The same paper states the premise: "the generation process will invariably lead to failure, that is, students are rarely able to solve the problems and discover the canonical solutions by themselves. Yet, this very process can be productive for learning provided direct instruction on the [targeted concepts] is subsequently provided." — [Kapur & Bielaczyc 2011](https://repositories.cdlib.org/content/qt7761h4h2/qt7761h4h2.pdf); same wording in [Kapur (CogSci proceedings)](https://escholarship.org/content/qt16f7z2zq/qt16f7z2zq_noSplash_c1ea25a7bef003a69ba2e79f885b1d0a.pdf)
- **[Moderate · two RCTs; abstract level; sample sizes not extracted]** Kapur 2014 (Cognitive Science): both methods produced high procedural knowledge; problem solving first produced significantly greater conceptual understanding and transfer; the number of student-generated solutions predicted learning outcomes; learning from peers' failed attempts beat instruction-first but not problem-solving-first. — [Kapur 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/cogs.12107)
- **[Moderate · abstract plus a secondary summary]** Sinha & Kapur 2021 (Learning and Instruction): failure-driven vs success-driven vs no explicit scaffolding during problem solving prior to instruction. Posttest scores on non-isomorphic conceptual items were similar; failure-driven scaffolding showed higher-quality constructive reasoning than success-driven and benefits on transfer. A Columbia EPIC summary says the success-driven group had the highest performance during the problem-solving phase but the lowest reasoning quality and transfer. — [Sinha & Kapur 2021 (L&I)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2021.101488); [EPIC summary](https://epic.tc.columbia.edu/research/academic-alert/blog-posts/2022/is-failure-necessary-for-problem-solving/)
- **[Moderate · conceptual]** Kapur 2016 (Educational Psychologist) proposes a typology of productive/unproductive success and failure, arguing that initial performance and long-term learning can be incommensurable. — [Kapur 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2016.1155457)
- **[Moderate · review; paraphrased from a fetched abstract, not verbatim]** Loibl, Roll & Rummel (Educational Psychology Review): problem solving before instruction fosters learning only if specific design features are present, namely contrasting cases and building instruction on student solutions. — [Loibl et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-016-9379-x)
- **[Strong · meta-analysis; general domains]** Alfieri et al. 2011 (164 studies): unassisted discovery vs explicit instruction, 580 comparisons (108 studies), d = −0.38, 95% CI [−.44, −.31] (study level −0.38 [−.50, −.25]), N = 25,986; enhanced discovery vs other instruction, 360 comparisons (56 studies), d = 0.30 [.23, .36] (study level 0.30 [.15, .44]), N = 25,925. Within enhanced discovery: elicited explanation d = 0.36, guided discovery d = 0.50, generation d = −0.15. Adults benefited more than children. The abstract concludes that "feedback, worked examples, scaffolding, and elicited explanations" help, while unassisted discovery does not. — [Alfieri et al. 2011](https://www.fisme.science.uu.nl/publicaties/literatuur/2011_alfieri_discovery_learning.pdf)
- **[Strong · meta-analysis; inquiry-based learning]** Lazonder & Harmsen 2016 (72 studies): guidance effects of d = 0.66 [0.44, 0.88] on learning activities, d = 0.71 [0.52, 0.90] on performance success and d = 0.50 [0.37, 0.62] on learning outcomes. — [Lazonder & Harmsen 2016](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315627366)
- **[Secondary]** Kapur & Bielaczyc describe Klahr & Nigam (2004) as finding that students directly taught to design unconfounded experiments outperformed those "simply left alone" to design experiments, and cite Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) for the position that with novel information "learners should be explicitly shown what to do and how to do it". — [Kapur & Bielaczyc 2011](https://repositories.cdlib.org/content/qt7761h4h2/qt7761h4h2.pdf)
- **[Weak · vendor opinion]** Math Academy's FAQ states: "There does not seem to be any tangible, empirically-supported reason for a student to struggle with a problem for a long period of time as opposed to using that time to learn more content", invoking the expertise reversal effect and Sweller, Clark & Kirschner (2010); it asserts that an hour of many short, well-scaffolded problems at the edge of knowledge teaches more than one long struggle (no data given). — [The Math Academy Way (working draft)](https://justinmath.com/files/the-math-academy-way.pdf)

### Inferences
- PF evidence is about a design with planned failure plus consolidation: one extended unguided problem on a not-yet-taught concept, then explicit instruction. Outcomes are conceptual understanding and transfer; procedural fluency was equal in the one study that reported it. It does not test repeated daily practice at a lower success rate on already-taught skills.
- In Kapur's Experiments 2 and 3, replacing generation with given solutions or told features narrowed the advantage to conceptual insight (and near transfer in Experiment 2), which indicates the generation step itself carries part of the effect and that what follows the struggle matters.
- Alfieri's negative effect for unassisted discovery (d = −0.38) and for pure generation inside enhanced discovery (d = −0.15), alongside positive effects for feedback, worked examples, scaffolding and elicited explanation, indicate that struggle pays when it is followed or accompanied by guidance and not when it is left unassisted.
- The meta-analysis shows contrasting trends for second to fifth graders and for domain-general skills; the PF literature retrieved here is mostly ninth-grade mathematics, so it sits in the target age range but only for conceptual topics with a single extended problem.
- The unadjusted g = 0.36 and the publication-bias-adjusted g = 0.87 differ in an unusual direction (adjustment normally lowers estimates); I could not see the method.

### Gaps
- Full text of the Sinha & Kapur 2021 meta-analysis was blocked (403), so moderator effect sizes, participant counts and domain splits were not seen.
- Kapur 2014 sample sizes and the delay between instruction and posttest in any PF study were not extracted.
- First-attempt success rates on PF generation problems are described only qualitatively ("rarely able to solve", "ultimately unsuccessful"); no numeric rate was found.
- No PF study with repeated, spaced problem sets or varying the length of the generation phase was found.
- Primary sources for the opposing position (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006; Sweller, Clark & Kirschner 2010; Klahr & Nigam 2004) were not read.

## 4. Wheel-spinning and unproductive persistence: definition, prevalence, predictors and what helped

### Takeaway
Wheel-spinning is failing to master a skill "in a timely manner", operationalized in later work as no three correct in a row within the first 10 practice opportunities; measured prevalence ranges from 0.56% to 46.9% depending on the criterion and dataset. In the largest correlational study (ASSISTments, 23,896 students) help avoidance, consistently short gaps between problems, and repeated bottom-out hints were associated with wheel-spinning, but the best classifier reached only AUC 0.684, and no randomized test of an intervention was found.

### Cited Findings

#### Definitions
- **[Abstract only]** Beck & Gong 2013 (AIED, LNCS 7926:431–440): wheel-spinning is when students do not succeed in mastering a skill in a timely manner; "if a student does not master a skill in ASSISTments or the Cognitive Tutor quickly, the student is likely to struggle and will probably never master the skill." — [Beck & Gong 2013](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-39112-5_44)
- **[Moderate · secondary description]** "Beck & Gong [1] operationalized wheel spinning as failing to get three attempts correct in a row within the first 10 practice opportunities" (the "three correct in a row criterion"). — [Zhang et al. 2019](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED594575.pdf). Botelho et al. use "failing to reach mastery after seeing ten learning opportunities", and note that ASSISTments' default mastery is three consecutive first-attempt correct answers without computer aid. — [Botelho et al. 2019](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10095357)
- **[Moderate]** Gong & Beck 2015 (Learning at Scale): wheel-spinning is common in different tutoring systems, and a model using generic features generalizes to future students and detects wheel-spinning early with high accuracy. — [Gong & Beck 2015](https://doi.org/10.1145/2724660.2724673)
- **[Moderate · authors' critique]** Kai et al. argue the Beck & Gong definition "does not account for students who may have achieved mastery by eliciting external help or who obtained the correct answers from a teacher or fellow student" and "does not consider the possibility that some difficult skills may legitimately need more than 10 practice opportunities", so they add a delayed retention test to the definition. — [Kai et al. 2018](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/210/88)

#### Prevalence (sources disagree; primary Beck & Gong numbers not read)
- **[Moderate · secondary]** Kai et al. describe Beck & Gong as finding that "approximately 38% of students did not master a given skill after 10 practice opportunities". — [Kai et al. 2018](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/210/88)
- **[Moderate · secondary]** Zhang et al. report that in the two datasets used by Gong & Beck (2015) "the wheel spinning ratio was 16% and 6%". — [Zhang et al. 2019](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED594575.pdf)
- **[Moderate]** Zhang et al. (Carnegie Learning MATHia Algebra datasets CL1, CL2, and a high-school Geometry tutor; 132,551, 419,832 and 8,175 student–knowledge-component pairs): wheel-spinning frequency under the three-in-a-row criterion / under Predictive Stability PS++ was 6.6% / 24.2% (CL1), 0.56% / 2.17% (CL2) and 10.2% / 13.2% (Geometry). Agreement between the two criteria on flagged pairs was 22.2% (CL1), 14.1% (CL2) and 41.6% (Geometry). — [Zhang et al. 2019](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED594575.pdf)
- **[Moderate]** Kai et al.: on the same 75,481 ASSISTments student–problem-set pairs with retention (ARRS) information, their retention-adjusted definition flags 9.1% as wheel-spinning, whereas the Beck & Gong definition (more than 10 problems without three in a row) would flag 46.9%; on all 287,093 pairs the Beck & Gong definition gives 12.3%. They write that "Not all students who struggle are spinning their wheels." — [Kai et al. 2018](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/210/88)

#### Kai, Almeda, Baker, Heffernan & Heffernan 2018 (JEDM 10(1):36–71; ASSISTments Skill Builders; large, correlational)
- **[Moderate · correlational, large sample]** Data: 23,896 students, 298 middle schools, 619 Skill Builders, 2014–15; mastery = three correct in a row; retention tested with the ARRS single-item delayed test. Funnel: 287,093 student–problem-set pairs; 211,612 removed because they reached three in a row but had no ARRS test; 75,481 remaining; 8,948 "persistent" (10 or more problems); 66,533 non-persistent (56.2% mastered before problem 10; 43.8% quit before it). Among the persistent pairs, 2,093 were productive persistence (three in a row on or after problem 10 and passed ARRS) and 6,855 were wheel-spinning (three in a row on or after problem 10 but failed ARRS, or never got three in a row). — [Kai et al. 2018](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/210/88)
- **[Moderate]** The 10-problem cut-off follows system design: students "are stopped from working on the problem set after they have attempted 10 problems in a problem set within a day", and must return on a later day to reach the 11th problem; the median set length was 11 problems (half of students saw 6–22). — [Kai et al. 2018](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/210/88)
- **[Moderate]** Classifier: J48 decision tree on 15 features; AUC ROC 0.684 (student-skill-level cross-validation) and 0.636 (student-level); 3-feature model 0.628; best single feature (std-timeBetweenProblems) AUC 0.554; 95 leaves. — [Kai et al. 2018](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/210/88)
- **[Moderate]** Top feature combinations: (a) no hints requested in at least one problem plus more than one bottom-out hint within a window of 8 problems: 89.07% wheel-spinning (2544 of 2856 pairs); (b) no hints in at least one problem, at most one bottom-out hint, and standard deviation of days between problem-set visits at most 2.53: 77.85% (5027 of 6457). The 3-feature tree gave 76.49% (5958 of 7789) for no hints in one problem, never two or more bottom-out hints, and low delay variation, and 89.74% (2781 of 3099) for its highest-probability branch. Unconditionally, the wheel-spinning proportion fell steadily as the maximum number of bottom-out hints in the last 8 problems rose (Figure 5); the authors call the relationship "nuanced". Wheel-spinning proportion was highest where the standard deviation of time between problems was very low (consistently short delays). — [Kai et al. 2018](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/210/88)
- **[Weak · authors' hypothesis, untested]** The abstract concludes that "encouraging students to both engage in spaced practice and use bottom-out hints sparingly is likely helpful for reducing their wheel-spinning"; the text says a wheel-spinning student "should probably be encouraged to seek help, whether from the system or their teacher, whereas a productively persistent student should probably be left alone"; the authors state all findings are correlational and call for randomized trials. — [Kai et al. 2018](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/210/88)
- **[Moderate · as described by Kai et al.]** Other detectors: Matsuda et al. 2016 (Cognitive Tutor Geometry, 122 students, about 3000 student-skill sequences with 5 or more opportunities): high recall but precision around 25%. Physics Playground: wheel-spinning operationalized in part as attempts taking more than 15 minutes in a level; predictive accuracy 82.9% vs a 73.4% majority-class baseline. — [Kai et al. 2018](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/210/88)

#### Early detection (Zhang et al. 2019, EDM)
- **[Moderate]** Logistic regression on "Correct Response Percentage" alone reached 93.5% precision and 77.1% recall after 4 steps (PS++ label). Random forest at step 4: (precision, recall) of (77.2%, 63.5%) on the three-in-a-row label and (90.8%, 81.4%) on PS++. Nearly all models had under 20% precision and recall in the first three steps. The authors also report that the two criteria diverge substantially across datasets. — [Zhang et al. 2019](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED594575.pdf); Predictive Stability: [Käser et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2883851.2883961)

#### What helped (interventions)
- **[Weak · secondary summary]** Beck & Gong, as summarized by the Global Learning Council: designers should provide fallbacks for mastery-learning failures, and data from wheel-spinners can underestimate learning rates. — [Global Learning Council](https://www.globallearningcouncil.org/posts/wheel-spinning-when-do-instructors-need-to-intervene-in-online-courses)
- **[Weak · project description]** An NSF early-career project on the downsides of perseverance (2015–2020) aimed to build and test interventions for wheel-spinning; no results were retrieved. — [ECR Hub project page](https://ecrhub.org/ecr-projects?id=1535337)
- **[Abstract only]** The Help Tutor (feedback on help-seeking in a Cognitive Tutor) made students use on-demand help more deliberately "but not to achieve better learning outcomes". — [Help Helps, But Only So Much (review)](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/help-helps-but-only-so-much-research-on-help-seeking-with-intelli/)

### Inferences
- Prevalence is definition-dependent by a large factor even within one dataset (9.1% vs 46.9% of the same 75,481 pairs), and the two criteria Zhang et al. compared agree on only 14.1% to 41.6% of flagged pairs, so a wheel-spinning rate from one study cannot be transplanted to another system.
- Among Kai's pairs with 10 or more problems, 6,855 of 8,948 (76.6%, computed from reported counts) met the retention-adjusted definition and 2,093 (23.4%) reached mastery and passed retention after 10 or more problems; persisting beyond 10 problems is therefore mostly unproductive in that dataset, with a substantial productive minority.
- Useful prediction needed several attempts: in Zhang et al., models were under 20% precision and recall in the first three steps and reached 77–93% precision by step 4 on some labels. The ASSISTments daily cap of 10 problems shaped the persistence definition.
- Help avoidance and tight, uniform spacing were associated with wheel-spinning, while the bottom-out-hint relationship ran in both directions depending on conditioning (Figure 5 vs the tree); all of it is correlational, as the authors state.

### Gaps
- The Beck & Gong 2013 full text (and Gong's dissertation chapter on wheel-spinning) was not accessed; the 38% prevalence figure is second-hand.
- No randomized evaluation of a wheel-spinning intervention was found.
- MATHia's published mastery threshold was not found; the Zhang datasets do not state it in the retrieved text.
- No wheel-spinning study in college mathematics or outside ASSISTments, Cognitive Tutor/MATHia, Physics Playground was found.

## 5. How long should students struggle before help, and what kind of help preserves learning?

### Takeaway
No study retrieved experimentally varied how long a student works before help in math problem solving; the nearest facts are count-based detection thresholds (10 opportunities; useful prediction from about 4 steps) and meta-analytic evidence that guidance, feedback, worked examples and elicited explanation outperform unassisted discovery. For specific help types in math the evidence is thin: bottom-out hints correlated with learning gains when students spent time on them (N = 39, correlational), and vendor reports describe retry-later plus targeted prerequisite review without experimental evidence.

### Cited Findings

#### Thresholds used to detect unproductive struggle (not experiments on help timing)
- **[Moderate]** Ten practice opportunities and three correct in a row are the thresholds in the standard wheel-spinning definition; in ASSISTments the daily cap is 10 problems per set and the median set length 11 problems. — [Zhang et al. 2019](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED594575.pdf); [Kai et al. 2018](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/210/88)
- **[Moderate]** Prediction from early steps: precision and recall under 20% in the first three steps, and 93.5% precision and 77.1% recall for a Correct Response Percentage model after 4 steps (PS++ label). — [Zhang et al. 2019](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED594575.pdf)
- **[Moderate · as described by Kai et al.]** In Physics Playground, an attempt in a level taking more than 15 minutes was one operationalization of wheel-spinning. — [Kai et al. 2018](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/210/88)
- **[Weak · vendor]** Math Academy describes a retry-another-day policy: if a student fails a lesson they "make progress learning unrelated topics before asking them to re-attempt the failed lesson"; "Usually, all it takes to rebound is a bit of rest and a fresh pair of eyes. On average, students pass their first attempt 95% of the time, and within two attempts 99% of the time, without any further intervention"; if a student gets stuck again in the same place "without making any additional forward progress", they receive remedial reviews on key prerequisites linked in a knowledge graph; whenever a quiz question is missed it immediately follows with a remedial review of that topic. No experimental test was found in the document. — [The Math Academy Way (working draft)](https://justinmath.com/files/the-math-academy-way.pdf)

#### Help types and their effects on learning
- **[Strong · meta-analyses]** Guidance and feedback: Alfieri et al. (unassisted discovery d = −0.38; enhanced discovery d = 0.30; elicited explanation d = 0.36; guided discovery d = 0.50; generation d = −0.15; "feedback, worked examples, scaffolding, and elicited explanations" help) — [Alfieri et al. 2011](https://www.fisme.science.uu.nl/publicaties/literatuur/2011_alfieri_discovery_learning.pdf); Lazonder & Harmsen (guidance: d = 0.66 on learning activities, 0.71 on performance success, 0.50 on learning outcomes) — [Lazonder & Harmsen 2016](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315627366).
- **[Moderate · correlational; N = 39 with pre/post (20 control, 19 experimental); Geometry Cognitive Tutor log data]** Shih, Koedinger & Scheines 2008 (EDM) treat a bottom-out hint as "a simple worked example" and use time spent after it as a proxy for self-explanation. The response-time indicator of good bottom-out hint behavior (HINTs) correlated with posttest 0.53 and adjusted gain 0.48 in the control condition (both p < 0.05) and with posttest 0.38 (p < 0.10) and adjusted gain 0.41 (p < 0.05) in the experimental condition; correlations with the pretest were near 0; they conclude "some bottom-out hint use can be good for learning". — [Shih, Koedinger & Scheines 2008](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/philosophy/docs/scheines/Shih_Koedinger_Scheines_2008_EDM.pdf)
- **[Moderate · experiment; programming tutor; population not extracted]** Corbett & Anderson 2001: immediate feedback was the most time-efficient; the tutor-supported groups performed equivalently on tests; students did not prefer a particular feedback condition. — [Corbett & Anderson 2001](https://doi.org/10.1145/365024.365111)
- **[Abstract only]** The Help Tutor produced more deliberate use of on-demand help "but not … better learning outcomes". — [Help Helps, But Only So Much](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/help-helps-but-only-so-much-research-on-help-seeking-with-intelli/)
- **[Weak · preliminary poster]** Worked examples in math (University of Delaware meta-analysis poster): 40 articles, 49 studies, 185 effect sizes; robust-variance-estimation average g = 0.43, 95% CI 0.31–0.55, I² = 94.46%; no moderator significant. — [Barbieri et al. poster](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Worked-Examples-Meta-Poster_022722_SC.pdf)
- **[Moderate]** Supplying the answer before attempts reduces the benefit of generation: in Kapur & Bielaczyc Experiments 2 and 3, giving solutions or critical features without prior generation left only conceptual insight (and near transfer in Experiment 2) significant. — [Kapur & Bielaczyc 2011](https://repositories.cdlib.org/content/qt7761h4h2/qt7761h4h2.pdf)
- **[Weak · vendor]** Math Academy raises lesson pass rates by adding scaffolding that reduces cognitive load: improving an explanation or worked example, adding an intermediate knowledge point, or splitting a topic. — [The Math Academy Way (working draft)](https://justinmath.com/files/the-math-academy-way.pdf)

### Inferences
- The literature offers count-based and ratio-based criteria for identifying unproductive struggle (10 opportunities, three in a row, about 4 steps for early detection) and effect sizes for help types in general, but no experiment that varies time or number of attempts before help.
- Help is not uniformly harmful to learning: the time students spend on a bottom-out hint is associated with gains in a small correlational sample, and the meta-analytic contrast is between unassisted struggle and struggle with guidance, not between struggle and no struggle.
- In the one dataset analysed (Zhang et al.), models predicting wheel-spinning from the first three steps had under 20% precision and recall, so a classification made that early would be mostly false positives and would miss most wheel-spinning cases (inference from the reported figures).

### Gaps
- No experiment varying the time or attempts allowed before help (for math or otherwise) was found.
- No randomized evidence found for prerequisite-review remediation, retry-later policies or hint-level sequencing; the Math Academy figures are vendor statements.
- The assistance-dilemma literature (Koedinger & Aleven 2007) and Help Tutor details beyond the review abstract were not retrieved; the population in Corbett & Anderson 2001 was not extracted.
- The worked-example poster is preliminary; its moderators and population grade levels were not extracted.

## 6. Where does the evidence come from (population, sample size, effect size), and how much of it is high school or college math?

### Takeaway
Direct high-school or college math evidence in the retrieved sources is limited to tutoring-system outcomes and observational data (Cognitive Tutor Algebra I, ALEKS, ASSISTments, MATHia and Geometry log data) and Kapur's ninth-grade PF studies; the success-rate manipulations come from elementary arithmetic, geography, number-line games, paired associates and motor tasks. Effect sizes span d = −0.38 to d = 0.83 across incommensurable outcomes (delayed recall, conceptual transfer, standardized tests).

### Cited Findings

#### High school and college math (direct)
- **[Strong · RCT; abstract level]** Cognitive Tutor Algebra I at scale: no first-year effect, about 8 percentile points in year two; significant in high schools, not middle schools. — [Pane et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.3102/0162373713507480)
- **[Moderate · meta-analyses; K-12 and higher education]** ALEKS: g = 0.05 [−0.01, 0.20] overall (33 studies, 56 effect sizes, 9,238 students) and g = 0.43 [0.02, 0.83] as a supplement — [Sun et al. 2021](https://www.mheducation.com/prek-12/resources/research/library/math/the-effects-of-aleks-on-mathematics-learning-in-k-12-and-higher-education.html); 15 studies, 24 samples, "as good, but not better than" classroom teaching — [Fang et al. 2019](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/facpubs/7254).
- **[Moderate · quasi-experimental]** Productive failure, ninth-grade math in Singapore: N = 140 (conceptual insight partial η² = .27) and N = 74 (39 PF vs 35 DI) — [Kapur (CogSci)](https://escholarship.org/content/qt16f7z2zq/qt16f7z2zq_noSplash_c1ea25a7bef003a69ba2e79f885b1d0a.pdf); [Kapur & Bielaczyc 2011](https://repositories.cdlib.org/content/qt7761h4h2/qt7761h4h2.pdf).
- **[Moderate · correlational log data]** Geometry Cognitive Tutor, N = 39 with pre/post — [Shih et al. 2008](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/philosophy/docs/scheines/Shih_Koedinger_Scheines_2008_EDM.pdf); MATHia Algebra datasets (132,551 and 419,832 student–KC pairs) and a high-school Geometry tutor (8,175 pairs) — [Zhang et al. 2019](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED594575.pdf); ASSISTments Skill Builders (23,896 students, 298 middle schools, 619 Skill Builders) — [Kai et al. 2018](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/210/88).
- **[Strong · RCTs; middle school math]** ASSISTments: 2,850 seventh-graders, 43 schools — [Roschelle et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858416673968); interleaved practice: 54 seventh-grade classes, 61% vs 38%, d = 0.83 — [Rohrer et al.](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000367).

#### Elementary and middle school (success-rate manipulations)

- **[Strong in context / Weak]** Paired associates, 291 analysed adults — [Eglington & Pavlik 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7567101/); verbal study-time allocation by adults — [Metcalfe & Kornell 2003](https://psychology.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/2016-11/530.pdf); geography site users (more than 1.7 million answers) — [Papoušek & Pelánek 2015](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/aied15.pdf); motor tasks, N = 194 (preprint) and N = 36 — [Al-Fawakhiri et al. 2023](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.07.19.549705); [Bootsma et al. 2018](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10056012/); stroke rehabilitation, N = 2,581 — [Ramos Muñoz et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2022.896298).

### Inferences
- No retrieved study that manipulates success rate or difficulty uses high school or college multi-step math; the high-school and college math evidence concerns whole tutoring systems, observational wheel-spinning data, or conceptual PF lessons.
- Effect sizes are not commensurable across these rows: they differ in outcome (recall, conceptual transfer, standardized test), delay (immediate to one month), comparison condition and design (RCT, quasi-experiment, correlation).
- The largest samples (Math Garden, slepemapy, Battleship Numberline, ASSISTments logs) are observational or A/B tests of engagement and performance, while the experiments that measured delayed learning (Eglington & Pavlik, Rohrer, Kapur) are smaller or manipulate something other than success rate.

### Gaps
- No experiment on first-try success rate with a delayed learning measure in high school or college multi-step math.
- No wheel-spinning or help-timing study in college mathematics.
- Sample sizes for Pane et al. 2014, Kapur 2014 and the Sinha & Kapur 2021 meta-analysis (participants) were not extracted.
