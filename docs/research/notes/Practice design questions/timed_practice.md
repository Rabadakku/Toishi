# Timed Practice (Practicing Under Time Pressure / at Exam Pace) in Math and STEM Problem Solving

Scope: facts only, no design recommendations. Compiled 2026-11-02 from ERIC, PubMed/Europe PMC and Crossref records, plus full texts that could be retrieved (Fuchs et al. 2013 via PMC; Jazbutis et al. 2023; the WWC 2021 practice guide; two ASEE conference papers; one AEA conference draft) and web pages. Not searched: Google Scholar. Paywalled full texts were not read (abstract only). Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex were rate-limited and not used.

Evidence labels (my judgment of design strength for the specific claim, not journal prestige):
- **RCT-iso** = randomized contrast that isolates timing/speed (confounds noted where found)
- **RCT-bundle** = randomized or meta-analytic evidence in which a timed activity is one element of a multi-component intervention (timing not isolated)
- **QED** = quasi-experiment, non-random or historical comparison
- **Lab** = controlled laboratory experiment
- **Meta** = meta-analysis or systematic review
- **Corr** = correlational / observational / archival
- **SCD** = single-case design
- **Theory** = theory, narrative review, or expert-panel opinion
- **Secondary** = news/blog/summary page; primary not independently verified
- Read depth: **Full** = full text read; **Abs** = abstract/record only; **Sum** = read via an automated page summary (re-verify numbers before external use)

## 1. Does timed practice improve timed-exam performance (and learning) vs untimed practice? (RCTs / quasi-experiments in math, physics, engineering, standardized tests)

### Takeaway
No retrieved study randomizes timed vs untimed practice and then measures performance on a later timed exam in high-school or college math, physics, or engineering, or on SAT/ACT/GRE/AP. The nearest evidence is (a) one large first-grade RCT in which speeded practice beat non-speeded practice on arithmetic-type outcomes (but was confounded with strategy emphasis), (b) a WWC "strong" rating whose 27 K-6 studies bundle timed activities with other components and include only three timed-vs-untimed contrasts, (c) a 211-adult lab RCT in which timed learning did not raise accuracy on delayed tests, and (d) one small engineering quasi-experiment in which timed homework modules coincided with higher in-class exam scores but no final-exam difference.

### Cited Findings

**Randomized timed/speeded vs untimed/non-speeded contrasts**
- Fuchs et al. (2013), J Educ Psychol. At-risk first graders (random assignment at student level, stratified by class) received 16 weeks x 3 sessions/week x 30 min of tutoring: 25 min number-knowledge instruction plus 5 min of practice that was either speeded (quick responding, efficient counting procedures, many correct responses) or non-speeded (reinforced relations and principles). Analysed n = 195 speeded, 190 non-speeded, 206 at-risk no-tutoring control (plus 300 low-risk classmates; 648 at-risk students were originally assigned). Both tutoring conditions beat control on all 4 outcomes; speeded > non-speeded on simple arithmetic and 2-digit calculations; "comparable" on number knowledge and word problems. Reported ES (Discussion): non-speeded vs control .38; speeded vs non-speeded .51; speeded vs control .87; vs low-risk classmates .39 for speeded (gap narrowed) vs .07 favoring low-risk for non-speeded; number knowledge ES .11 favoring speeded; word problems ES .04 favoring non-speeded. A footnote (fn 4) gives different values for the same contrasts (speeded vs control ES = .76, t(351) = 8.10; non-speeded vs control ES = .28; speeded vs non-speeded ES = .50, t(348) = 5.30, p < .001; gap ES .36 vs .11); I could not tell which outcome/analysis that footnote refers to. Speeded practice produced 40 vs 7 correct responses per session, and the conditions also differed in strategy emphasis (speeded: efficient counting strategies only; non-speeded: a variety of number-principle strategies). The authors state they "found no evidence" that speeded practice harmed complex calculations, number knowledge or word problems. [RCT-iso, confounded; grade 1; Full] — [Fuchs et al. 2013 (PMC full text)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3779611/); [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1006608)
- Hechinger Report describes the same study (speed practice = flashcards within 60 seconds, "meet or beat" the previous score, 16 weeks) as "one of the few to directly test timed versus untimed conditions", and says more replication is needed. [Secondary; Sum] — [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-do-math-drills-help-children-learn/)
- WWC 2021 practice guide, Appendix C: only 3 of the 27 studies supporting Recommendation 6 contrasted a timed fluency activity with an otherwise identical untimed activity. "All impacts were positive, however not all were statistically significant": study 1 g = 0.42* (counting and cardinality) and g = 0.09 (whole-number computation); study 2 g = 0.36* (whole-number computation); study 3 g = 0.20 (not significant). [RCT-iso x3 inside a WWC review; K-6 struggling students; Full] — [WWC 2021006 practice guide, p. 130](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC2021006-Math-PG.pdf)
- Jazbutis, Wiseheart, Radvansky & McNeil (2023), J Numerical Cognition. Adults (n = 211 analysed; ages 18-40, M = 25.2, SD = 5.9; recruited via Prolific; timed n = 104, untimed n = 107) learned an unfamiliar 17- or 19-times table over 3 sessions in a 5 (spacing: 1, 2, 7, 10, 14 days) x 2 (timed vs untimed) design. Timed condition: goal of >= 15 correct in 30 seconds on 20-problem sets with immediate scoring and redo of misses; untimed: same sets, time not shown. Tests (immediate, ten-day delay, end of semester ~9 weeks after start) used a 90-second per-question limit that was not presented as a beat-the-clock. Results: no evidence that timed practice produced higher accuracy than untimed (F(1,186) = 1.98, p = .16, ηp² = .01, BF10 = 3.23; means in the opposite direction); timed learners were less accurate on the immediate test (test phase x time pressure F(2.71, 503.31) = 3.67, p = .015, ηp² = .019) but more accurate on a transfer test (M = .87 vs .80, F(1,207) = 4.12, p = .04); no main effect of time pressure on response time (F(1,193) = .46, p = .50) but a spacing x time-pressure interaction (F(4,193) = 3.44, p = .01, ηp² = .07): timed practice gave faster later response times with 1- and 2-day spacing and slower with 7-, 10- and 14-day spacing (F(1,199) = 13.60, p < .001, ηp² = .06). Time pressure elevated perceived stress; timed and untimed practice produced similar forgetting of practiced facts, while untimed practice allowed gradual improvement on unpracticed and conceptually related facts across test phases. [RCT-iso; lab, online adults; Full] — [Jazbutis et al. 2023](https://jnc.psychopen.eu/index.php/jnc/article/view/7721); [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1400785)
- Knowles (2010) dissertation: 8-week quasi-experiment, 9 sixth-grade classes; control vs weekly 3-minute timed multiplication drill vs daily 3-minute timed drill; daily drill group scored significantly higher on the posttest than control and weekly groups; weekly group had significantly higher gain scores than control. [QED; grade 6; not peer reviewed; Abs] — [ERIC ED521638](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED521638)

**Higher-education and STEM course evidence**
- Miller, Brauer & Sharber (2011), ASEE: first linear-circuits course (EE/ME/civil/environmental majors), enhanced section N = 19 (summer 2010, 10-week term) vs a traditional section (summer 2009, same instructor, same exam material; size not stated, F df imply ~35 total). "Timed homework practice" = 3 online modules (Ohm's law; op amps; phasors/complex numbers), 30 questions x 3 points = 90 accuracy points, plus a time score of 10 points if completed under a preset limit minus 1 point per minute over; unlimited attempts with randomized values; mean 3.47 attempts per module. In-class exam % correct: Exam 1 85.7 vs 79.7; Exam 2 81.4 vs 62.3; final 70.9 vs 71.1 (no difference); in-class exams F[1,33] = 6.969, p = 0.013. Exam 3 not comparable (format differed). Authors conclude "repeatable, timed modules focusing on core concepts are an effective way to raise student performance and learning" and acknowledge small N, no randomization, different term lengths, and that 13 of 19 students' final was their lowest exam. [QED, historical control; college engineering; Sum] — [Miller et al. 2011](https://peer.asee.org/18025.pdf)
- Brothen & Wambach (2004): 15-minute limits on 10-item multiple-choice online quizzes in a college human-development course; students "spent less time on quizzes and performed better on exams when they had time limits on their quizzes"; authors attribute this to time limits reducing opportunity to look up answers, not to pace practice. [QED/experimental; college; N not in abstract; Abs] — [ERIC EJ683151](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ683151)
- Portolese, Krause & Bonner (2016): same online tests, experimental group given 50% more time (Spring 2015, quizzes n = 600) vs control (Winter 2015, quizzes n = 573); "more time on tests did not enhance student performance in terms of higher scores." [QED; college online courses; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1123527](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1123527)

**Acute (within-session) effects of explicit timing, not learning effects**
- Rhymer et al. (1998): 40 African-American third graders; explicit timing increased problem-completion rates and a "decreasing trend in percentage of problems correct also occurred." [Within-subject/SCD-type; grade 3; Abs] — [ERIC EJ583707](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ583707)
- Grays, Rhymer & Swartzmiller (2017): 81 fourth and fifth graders completed more digits correct during explicit timing than at baseline and control; math anxiety (MA) related linearly to performance, but "digits correct and accuracy remained consistent during baseline, control, and explicit timing when condition and anxiety level were both considered." [Within-subject experiment; grades 4-5; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1141244](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1141244)

**Standardized tests: time limits and practice/coaching (none isolates timed vs untimed practice)**
- Bridgeman, Trapani & Curley (2004), SAT I: fewer questions per section (verbal 35 -> 27 or 23; math 25 -> 20 or 17), embedded in the 30-minute equating section of two national administrations, simulated more time per question. Verbal gains < 10 points; math gains < 30 points on the 200-800 scale; high scorers benefited more; "no increase in scores for students with SAT scores of 400 or lower." [Experimental embedded in operational tests; high-school test-takers; Abs] — [Bridgeman et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2004.tb01167.x)
- Hausknecht et al. (2007) meta-analysis of practice/retest effects on cognitive-ability tests: 50 studies, 107 samples, 134,436 participants, adjusted overall effect size .26; larger when practice was accompanied by coaching and when identical forms were used. [Meta; general; Abs] — [PubMed 17371085](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17371085/)
- Kulik, Kulik & Bangert (1984): 40 studies; students can raise aptitude/achievement test scores by taking practice tests; gain size depended on identical vs parallel forms, number of practice tests, and ability level. [Meta; Abs] — [ERIC EJ303658](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ303658)
- Bangert-Drowns, Kulik & Kulik (1983): 30 controlled studies of coaching for achievement tests; "Coaching typically raised achievement test scores by .25 standard deviations." [Meta; Abs] — [ERIC EJ294177](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ294177)
- Becker (1990): 23 reports / 48 studies of SAT coaching; on average coaching can raise SAT scores, with considerable variability. [Meta; Abs] — [ERIC EJ417569](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ417569)
- Powers & Rock (1999): >4,000 SAT takers, ~500 of whom attended formal coaching; all estimates indicated coaching effects "far less than is claimed by major commercial test preparation companies." [Corr/quasi; Abs] — [Powers & Rock 1999](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.1999.tb00549.x)
- Hembree (1987) meta-analysis of 120 studies of non-content variables on math test performance: testwiseness training, praise, word-problem pictures, and frequent testing enhanced performance (abstract gives no effect sizes or time-limit result). [Meta; Abs] — [ERIC EJ354719](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ354719)

### Inferences
- Direct evidence on the exact question (timed practice -> later timed exam performance, HS/college STEM) is absent in what was retrieved; all supporting evidence is indirect (K-6 fluency trials, adult arithmetic-fact lab work, one engineering pilot).
- The strongest controlled contrast (Fuchs 2013) bundles speed with a narrower strategy emphasis and 5.7x more correct responses per session, so its ES of .51 cannot be attributed to the clock alone; the advantage was confined to arithmetic-type outcomes and absent for word problems/number knowledge (without harm).
- WWC's "strong" rating attaches to the recommended bundle (timed activity + efficient strategy + feedback + progress charting); the three explicit timed-vs-untimed contrasts yielded four effect sizes, two significant (0.42, 0.36) and two not (0.09, 0.20).
- In the one adult RCT, timing changed later speed only conditionally (depending on spacing) and did not raise accuracy on delayed tests.
- The robust effect of explicit timing is acute (more problems per minute in-session, with accuracy flat or trending down), which is distinct from any learning effect.

### Gaps
- No RCT or quasi-experiment on timed vs untimed practice with exam outcomes in HS/college math, physics, or engineering was found (only Miller 2011, which is historical-control and small); no physics study at all.
- No SAT/ACT/GRE/AP trial isolating timed practice was found; coaching/retest meta-analyses bundle many components.
- Van Houten & Thompson (1976) on explicit timing and several WWC-included studies were not retrieved individually; the identity of the three WWC timed-vs-untimed studies was not resolved (endnotes could not be parsed).
- Jazbutis et al. used adults on an artificial task (unfamiliar times tables); generalization to multi-step problem solving is untested.

## 2. Fluency and automaticity: math-fact fluency research, Precision Teaching / SAFMEDS, fluency building after accuracy, transfer to multi-step problems

### Takeaway
Timed/speeded fluency components reliably raise fact and computation fluency in K-6 struggling students (WWC: strong evidence, 27 studies, bundled interventions), with transfer shown to procedural computation and 2-digit calculation but not to algebra or word problems in at least one RCT. Precision Teaching / SAFMEDS evidence is mostly single-case or small-sample, and reviews explicitly call for more research and note generalization problems. No retrieved study tests whether fact-level speed transfers to multi-step problem solving in high-school or college STEM.

### Cited Findings

**WWC 2021 practice guide, Recommendation 6 (Timed Activities)**
- Rated "strong" on 27 studies (21 meet WWC group standards without reservations, 6 with reservations); samples "collectively included 4,336 students and 403 schools"; K-6 students with or at risk for math difficulty; interventions ranged from ~8 days to 19 weeks (22 lasted >= 8 weeks). All 27 interventions included timed fluency activities as one component; 18 targeted math-fact fluency, 5 targeted other retrieval material (e.g., fractions equivalent to 1/2), 4 both. 19 studies taught an efficient strategy for the fluency activity, 17 had students track progress, 26 gave immediate feedback. [RCT-bundle / Meta; Full] — [WWC 2021006, pp. 51, 120-121](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC2021006-Math-PG.pdf)
- Domain-level fixed-effect g across the 27 studies (all statistically significant, none negative): algebra and algebraic reasoning 0.55 (k = 3, CI 0.29-0.81); counting and cardinality 0.27 (k = 2); general mathematics achievement 0.35 (k = 5, CI 0.24-0.47); rational-numbers computation 1.55 (k = 9, CI 1.42-1.67); rational-numbers knowledge 0.57 (k = 9); rational-numbers magnitude 0.97 (k = 8); whole-number computation 0.64 (k = 16, CI 0.54-0.74); whole-number knowledge 0.09 (k = 1); whole-number magnitude 0.26 (k = 3). Because timing was bundled, these are not timing effects. [RCT-bundle / Meta; Full] — [WWC 2021006, Table C.13, p. 120](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC2021006-Math-PG.pdf)
- Panel guidance (expert opinion layered on the evidence): timed activities last 1-5 minutes and "are not the entire focus of the intervention"; add them "once students have been working on a concept over many lessons"; "Do not use timed activities to introduce and teach mathematics concepts and operations"; "giving timed worksheets alone does not support fluency"; remind students accuracy is the goal when they "race through and guess"; for anxious students use flashcards instead of long worksheets and group "meet or beat" goals. Automatic retrieval is said to give "more mental energy to understand relatively complex mathematical tasks and execute multi-step mathematical procedures." [Theory / expert panel; Full] — [WWC 2021006, pp. 51-55](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC2021006-Math-PG.pdf)

**Individual trials and meta-analyses on fact fluency and transfer**
- Fuchs et al. (2009), J Educ Psychol: 133 third graders with math difficulty randomized to control, "Math Flash" (automatic retrieval of number combinations) or "Pirate Math" (word problems with attention to number combinations, procedural calculation, algebra); 16 weeks x 3 sessions x 20-30 min. Math Flash "enhanced fluency with NCs with transfer to procedural computation but without transfer to algebra or word problems"; Pirate Math enhanced word problems plus NC fluency, procedural computation and algebra. [RCT-bundle; grade 3; Abs] — [ERIC EJ861181](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ861181)
- Powell et al. (2009): 139 third graders randomized to fact-retrieval practice, conceptual fact-retrieval instruction with practice, procedural computation/estimation instruction, or control (45 sessions over 15 weeks); for math-difficulty-only students both fact-retrieval conditions "achieved comparably and outperformed" control and the procedural condition on fact retrieval; for students with math plus reading difficulty there were no significant differences among conditions. [RCT-bundle; grade 3; Abs] — [ERIC EJ827128](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ827128)
- Fuchs et al. (2013): effects of both practice conditions on arithmetic were partially mediated by increased reliance on retrieval, and "only speeded practice helped at-risk children compensate for weak reasoning ability"; speeded practice produced stronger learning on 2-digit calculations than non-speeded practice. [RCT-iso, confounded; Abs/Full] — [ERIC EJ1006608](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1006608)
- Woodward (2006): 58 fourth graders (15 with math IEPs), integrated approach (strategies + timed practice drills) vs timed drills only for multiplication facts; both effective for automaticity, but the integrated group "generally performed better on posttest and maintenance test measures that assessed the application of facts to extended facts and approximation tasks." [Experimental; grade 4; Abs] — [ERIC EJ786218](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ786218)
- Tournaki (2003): 84 students with and without learning disabilities taught addition by a minimum-addend strategy vs drill and practice; students with LD improved significantly only in the strategy condition; general-education students improved in both; "In a transfer task, students from both groups improved only in the strategy conditions." [Experimental; Abs] — [ERIC EJ676182](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ676182)
- Hasselbring, Goin & Bransford (1988): "Fast Facts" computerized drill and practice developed fact recall in 160 mildly handicapped and nonhandicapped students aged 7-14, but "only when prior training for developing a declarative knowledge network is implemented." [Experimental; ages 7-14; Abs] — [ERIC EJ367483](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ367483)
- Codding, Burns & Lukito (2011) meta-analysis: 17 single-case studies, 55 participants; drill and practice with modeling produced the largest effect sizes; treatments with more than 3 components yielded higher ES than those with fewer than 3; combinations of treatment agents beat a single agent. [Meta of SCD; Abs] — [ERIC EJ927934](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ927934)
- Burns et al. (2015): 15,402 third-to-fifth graders in a national database; students with lower math skills required significantly more repetitions (p < .001) than more skilled students; facts with 4s, 5s, 6s, 7s needed more repetition than 2s and 3s; sessions needed to reach mastery decreased with grade level. [Corr/archival; grades 3-5; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1167980](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1167980)
- Montani & Smith (2004): 131 fifth graders on timed vs untimed math tests; children with math disabilities performed poorly on math fluency but within the average range on calculation. [Corr; grade 5; Abs] — [ERIC EJ808035](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ808035)

**Dosage and format of timed practice (secondary, from a researcher's blog summary)**
- Duhon (2022) RCT: eight timing schedules (8x/day down to every other week) plus no-practice control, 120-second sessions; 120 seconds/day identified as the minimum with a statistically significant effect; 4x and 8x per day had higher post-test digits correct per minute but smaller growth per instructional minute. Poncy (2010) SCD: 240-second vs 60-second daily trial, 240 s gave faster fluency gains and students "did not generalize addition fact knowledge to related subtraction facts." Fontenelle (2022) SCD, second grade: 210-s daily trial produced faster learning rates than 60 s; taped problems and explicit timing produced similar rates. Wolfe (2018): without goal setting/feedback, growth did not differ by frequency. Schutte (2015) and Powell (2022): distributed 60-second trials outperformed massed; a 10-minute break was not enough. A Dowdy & Peltier meta-analysis exists as a preprint (numbers not given). [Secondary; Sum] — [Peltier, "Timed Math Practice"](https://coreypeltier.substack.com/p/timed-math-practice)
- Villanueva (2022) dissertation: 43 third graders randomized to 1, 8 or 12 opportunities-to-respond per set of computer-based explicit-timing subtraction practice over 26 school days; dose predicted growth in digits correct per minute. [RCT; grade 3; not peer reviewed; Abs] — [ERIC ED650003](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED650003)

**Precision Teaching / SAFMEDS / behavioral fluency**
- Binder (1996): defines behavioral fluency as "that combination of accuracy plus speed of responding that enables competent individuals to function efficiently and effectively"; reports Precision Teaching discoveries linking frequency to retention, endurance (resistance to distraction) and application/transfer, and "invites further controlled research." Narrative review, not a controlled test. [Theory/narrative; Abs] — [PubMed 22478257](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22478257/)
- Quigley et al. (2018) review of SAFMEDS: "a great deal of research is still needed to clarify the SAFMEDS procedures and the benefits of SAFMEDS over traditional instruction. These conclusions are in line with broader criticisms of fluency-based instruction." [Review; Abs] — [PubMed 32004366](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32004366/)
- Quigley et al. (2021): college students learning unfamiliar Russian words and Chinese characters; basic SAFMEDS produced some learning (more correct, fewer incorrect per 1-minute timing) and all four supplementary procedures produced larger increases in correct responses. Non-math content. [Experimental/SCD-type; college; Abs] — [PubMed 34631374](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34631374/)
- Meindl et al. (2013): college students; SAFMEDS promoted fluent responding but rates dropped on novel equivalent flashcards (generalization probes), and higher training rates correlated with larger drops; embedding multiple-exemplar training promoted generalization of accurate, high-rate responding. [Experimental; college; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1038191](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1038191)
- Gist & Bulla (2022) systematic review of frequency building and Precision Teaching with school-aged children: 11 studies, 170 participants, assessed against WWC and CEC standards; "Small to large effects were found for all included variables"; concluded the combination is effective for "a variety of academic skills" (not math-specific). [Meta/systematic review; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1328678](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1328678)
- Sleeman et al. (2021): between-group experiment, Year 5-6 New Zealand students (ages 9-10); a self-regulated-learning plus Precision Teaching basic-facts program produced rapid multiplication-fluency development that was maintained over time and changed out-of-school practice behavior (N not in abstract). [Experimental; ages 9-10; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1296240](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1296240)
- Bucklin, Dickinson & Brethower (2000): compared fluency training vs training to accuracy on acquisition and retention of a composite skill; "for skilled adult learners, fluency training can aid the acquisition and retention for both the component and composite skills" (N and effect sizes not in the record; ERIC flags it non-peer-reviewed). [Experimental; adults; Abs] — [ERIC EJ629800](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ629800)
- A PRISMA-style systematic review and meta-analysis of Precision Teaching was registered as a protocol (2023); no pooled estimate was found. [Protocol; Abs] — [PubMed 37132015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37132015/)

**Mechanism: what automaticity does to working-memory load**
- Tronsky (2005): participants practiced complex multiplication (e.g., 3 x 18); before practice the most common strategy was a mental multidigit algorithm with high working-memory (WM) load, heavier for non-retrieval strategies; after practice "retrieval was used almost exclusively," "practice reduced working memory involvement to minimal levels," and there was no relationship between automaticity level and WM load. [Lab, pre/post practice; adults; N not in abstract; Abs] — [PubMed 16383179](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16383179/)
- Beilock et al. (2004): see Section 4 (practiced-to-retrieval high-WM math problems showed no choking under pressure). [Lab; Abs] — [PubMed 15584808](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15584808/)

### Inferences
- Fluency components reliably improve the practiced skill and near transfer (procedural computation, 2-digit calculations), but the one test that targeted fact retrieval alone found no transfer to algebra or word problems (Fuchs 2009), and the WWC algebra effect (g = 0.55, k = 3) comes from bundled interventions.
- Positive timed-practice studies in this set layer timing onto previously taught content and strategies (WWC steps; Hasselbring: prior declarative network; Woodward and Tournaki: strategy + practice > practice alone on application/transfer).
- Precision Teaching/SAFMEDS claims about retention, endurance and application rest largely on narrative reviews and small/single-case studies; the field's own reviewers say the benefit over traditional instruction is unclarified, and generalization to novel stimuli can drop (Meindl 2013).
- Practice that converts a procedure to retrieval removes most of its WM load (Tronsky 2005), which is the proposed mechanism by which fluency could free capacity for multi-step problems; this mechanism was shown at the single-problem level, not in multi-step problem solving.

### Gaps
- No high-school or college STEM study of fact/component speed predicting or improving multi-step problem solving was found (Curry 2012 dissertation on 8th-grade multiplication speed-recall vs higher-level math is in ERIC but its results were not retrieved).
- Royer et al. (1999) and Tronsky & Royer (2002) on math-fact retrieval and complex math performance were not retrievable in this pass.
- Precision Teaching fluency "aims" (e.g., digits-correct-per-minute criteria) and their evidential basis were not retrieved.
- Duhon (2022), Poncy (2010), Fontenelle (2022) and others were read only through a blog summary; primary papers not verified.

## 3. When to introduce speed: accuracy-first vs speed-from-the-start; speed-accuracy tradeoff in skill acquisition (power law, ACT-R, Delaney et al. 1998 strategy shifts)

### Takeaway
Practice speed-ups are not a single smooth process: individual and strategy-level analyses show discrete strategy shifts (e.g., computation to retrieval) with within-strategy power-law (or exponential) improvement, and response deadlines push solvers toward retrieval. Real-world speed-accuracy relationships are inconsistent, and the sign of the time-on-task effect flips between routine and controlled-processing tasks. No retrieved study directly compares an accuracy-first-then-speed sequence with speed-from-the-start for multi-step problems; positive timed-practice studies all added timing after concept/strategy instruction.

### Cited Findings

**Learning-curve and strategy-shift evidence**
- Delaney, Reder, Staszewski & Ritter (1998), Psychological Science 9(1):1-7: fit power-law curves to aggregated data from four mental-arithmetic experiments, then separately per participant and strategy; strategy-specific fits explained individual solution times significantly better in all four experiments; the authors conclude practice on a specific strategy explains improvement better than practice on the task as a whole. Details from the PDF (via summary): retrieval was faster than calculation (intercepts differed, e.g., t(27) = 18.67, p < .001, for multiplication) while slopes did not differ significantly; aggregate fits were already very good (R² .998-.999), so the advantage appears in individual-level analyses. [Lab; adults; Sum] — [Delaney et al. 1998 (PSU record)](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-strategy-specific-nature-of-improvement-the-power-law-applies/); [CMU PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/memorylab/publications/98_pd_lmr_js_fr.pdf)
- Heathcote, Brown & Mewhort (2000): "The power function is treated as the law relating response time to practice trials. However, the evidence for a power law is flawed, because it is based on averaged data"; across 40 data sets (7,910 learning series, 475 subjects, 24 experiments) "the exponential function fit better than the power function in all the unaveraged data sets"; averaging biased results toward the power function. [Meta-style survey of lab data; Abs] — [PubMed 10909131](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10909131/)
- Donner & Hardy (2015): 25,280 individual learning curves (500 measurements each, four cognitive tasks); a piecewise power-law model fit significantly better than a single power law; the authors infer "gradual, smooth improvement, with diminishing gains within a specific strategy" plus "a discrete sequence of strategy shifts"; after a transition performance typically dropped briefly, then surpassed earlier levels; transition rate was negatively associated with age. [Corr/modelling of lab data; Abs] — [PMC4577530](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4577530)
- Dutilh et al. (2009): in a 10,000-trial lexical-decision task, practice "not only affects the speed of information processing, but also response caution, response bias, and peripheral processing time"; the authors warn that collapsing the practice effect into one output measure may be hazardous. [Lab; Abs] — [PubMed 19966251](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19966251/)
- Lemaire & Siegler (1995): longitudinal study of French second graders learning single-digit multiplication, assessed three times in the year; speed and accuracy gains reflected four kinds of strategic change (new strategies, more use of the most efficient existing strategies, more efficient execution, more adaptive strategy choice), with children using multiple strategies at every point. [Longitudinal; grade 2; N not in abstract; Abs] — [PubMed 7897342](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7897342/)
- Anderson's ACT-R / skill-acquisition stage theory and the original power-law-of-practice papers were not retrieved from primary sources (see Gaps).

**Time pressure and strategy choice**
- Campbell & Austin (2002): adults solved simple addition under a fast deadline (respond before a 750-ms beep) vs slow (2,500-ms beep) and reported retrieval vs procedural strategy each trial; as predicted, the fast deadline increased reported retrieval use, especially for large problems (deadline x size interaction). [Lab; adults; N not in abstract; Abs] — [PubMed 12450100](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12450100/)
- Beilock & DeCaro (2007): under high pressure, higher-WM individuals shifted from computationally demanding algorithms to simpler, less effective strategies and their accuracy suffered; when a simple strategy was optimal, lower-WM individuals did better under low pressure. (Pressure, not specifically time pressure; see Section 4.) [Lab; Abs] — [PubMed 17983308](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17983308/)

**Speed-accuracy tradeoff (SAT): general and in real-world data**
- Heitz (2014) review: "From insects to rodents to primates, the tendency for decision speed to covary with decision accuracy seems an inescapable property of choice behavior." [Review; Abs] — [PubMed 24966810](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24966810/)
- Domingue et al. (2022), J Educ Behav Stat: 29 data sets of cognitive tasks; "inconsistent relationships between time and accuracy; marginal increases in time use for an individual do not necessarily predict increases in accuracy"; the relationship "may depend on the underlying difficulty"; "respondents who exhibit more within-person variation in response speed are typically of lower ability"; response time "is typically a weak predictor of response accuracy." [Corr, secondary analysis of 29 datasets; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1350507](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1350507)
- Goldhammer et al. (2014), J Educ Psychol: 1,020 persons aged 16-65 (German PIAAC field test); in problem solving (controlled processing) the time-on-task effect was positive and increased with task difficulty (more time associated with success), shrinking with skill; in reading (routine processing) it was negative, most negative for easy tasks, and grew more negative with skill. "Time on task has no uniform interpretation but is a function of task difficulty and individual skill." [Corr; adults; Full abstract] — [ERIC EJ1054276](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1054276)
- Within-session SAT in explicit-timing studies: Rhymer et al. (1998, grade 3, N = 40) found completion rates up with a decreasing trend in percent correct. Jazbutis et al. (2023) timed learners were less accurate on the immediate test. WWC panel advice flags students who "race through and guess." [Abs / Full] — [ERIC EJ583707](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ583707); [Jazbutis 2023](https://jnc.psychopen.eu/index.php/jnc/article/view/7721); [WWC 2021006, p. 55](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC2021006-Math-PG.pdf)
- Ramos-Christian et al. (2008): 38 first and second graders; preoperational children "lacked the speed of concrete operational children but achieved comparable levels of accuracy" on arithmetic problems. [Corr; ages ~6-8; Abs] — [ERIC EJ796440](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ796440)

**Sequencing evidence (accuracy/strategy first vs speed first)**
- WWC panel: add timed activities after students have worked on a concept over many lessons; confirm students are competent in the strategy before the timed activity; do not use timed activities to introduce concepts (expert guidance supported by 27 bundled studies; no study varied the order). [Theory + RCT-bundle; Full] — [WWC 2021006, pp. 51-53](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC2021006-Math-PG.pdf)
- Hasselbring et al. (1988), Woodward (2006), Tournaki (2003): see Section 2 (declarative/strategy foundation preceded or accompanied timed drills; strategy-based conditions transferred better than drill-only). [Experimental; Abs]
- Fuchs et al. (2013): speeded practice was delivered inside number-knowledge tutoring with immediate error correction using an efficient counting procedure and an instruction to retrieve if confident, otherwise use the counting strategy; the authors contrast this with school practice "without sufficient scaffolding in number knowledge, in massed doses, and without support for correct responding." [RCT-iso; Full] — [Fuchs et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3779611/)

### Inferences
- Because practice speed-ups mix within-strategy improvement with discrete strategy shifts, a fixed time target on a multi-step task can be met by different routes (faster execution of the same strategy vs switching strategy); individual-level analyses are needed to see this, and aggregate curves can look smooth either way (Delaney 1998; Heathcote 2000; Donner & Hardy 2015).
- Deadlines measurably shift solvers toward retrieval on simple arithmetic (Campbell & Austin 2002); whether the same happens for multi-step algebra/calculus procedures was not tested in retrieved work.
- Time per item is a weak/inconsistent proxy for accuracy in real-world data (Domingue 2022) and has task-dependent meaning (Goldhammer 2014), which bears on using solve-time thresholds as a mastery signal for controlled-processing tasks vs routine ones.
- The "accuracy/strategy first, then timing" order appears in every positive study retrieved, but no study contrasts it with speed-from-the-start; the order claim is expert-panel opinion plus indirect evidence.

### Gaps
- No direct experimental comparison of accuracy-first-then-speed vs speed-from-the-start sequences in math or STEM problem solving was found.
- Primary sources for Anderson (1982, ACT*), Anderson et al. (2004, ACT-R), Newell & Rosenbloom (1981), Logan (1988), Rickard (1997) and Fitts & Posner stage theory were not retrieved (PubMed/ERIC lacked them; APA/book content not accessible); no ACT-R claims are made here.
- Delaney et al. (1998) numbers come from an automated summary of the PDF; total N was not stated in the summary.
- No retrieved evidence on speed-accuracy tradeoff under practice instructions ("speed emphasis" vs "accuracy emphasis") for cognitive-skill learning in educational tasks.

## 4. Costs: math anxiety and timed tests (Boaler's critique; Ashcraft; Ramirez), working memory under time pressure, choking under pressure (Beilock)

### Takeaway
Math anxiety (MA) is reliably negatively correlated with math performance (r = -.28 to -.34), more strongly for multi-step tasks and grade-bearing measures, and works through working-memory disruption. Experiments that manipulate timing show mixed, mostly short-term results: time limits lower performance for everyone in a 30-adult lab sample, raise state anxiety in grades 3-4 and perceived stress in adults, but have not shown differential harm to high-MA students or higher self-reported anxiety under overt timing. Boaler's headline claims (about one third of students start MA with timed testing) lack a primary citation in the retrieved material, while the rival literature does not show the opposite long-term effect either. Choking research shows pressure harms high-WM people on unpracticed, WM-heavy math problems, and practice to retrieval removes the effect.

### Cited Findings

**Boaler's claims and the critique (all secondary; verify before external use)**
- Boaler's "Fluency Without Fear" page (as read via automated summary) states "for about one third of students the onset of timed testing is the beginning of math anxiety" (cited to her own 2014 paper), that "the working memory becomes blocked" under time pressure (cites Beilock 2011; Ramirez et al. 2013), that "at least a third of students experience extreme stress around timed tests" (no source attached), that this "particularly occurs among higher achieving students and girls" (no source), and that timed tests are "a major cause" of MA (no direct citation); evidence is partly anecdotal (Stanford undergraduates naming timed tests; her daughter). She cites Delazer et al. (2005) that strategy learning beat drill memorization on transfer, and recommends no timed tests and no emphasis on speed. [Theory/advocacy; Sum] — [youcubed, Fluency Without Fear](https://www.youcubed.org/evidence/fluency-without-fear/)
- A blog critique (Ashman) reviewing the five new references in Boaler's PDF plus the references in Geist (2010) reports none tests whether timed tests cause MA: Suarez-Pellicioni et al. (2016) is a review relaying a suggestion; Estonanto & Dio (2019) surveyed 69 senior-high calculus students in the Philippines selected for moderate/high MA (no comparison group); Geist (2010) is a review calling timed testing a possible factor; Jackson & Leffingwell (1999) surveyed 157 trainee teachers, 146 of whom described a negative math experience, with timed tests one of many; Murtonen & Titterton (2004) and Newstead (1998) do not mention timed tests. Tsui & Mazzocco (cited in Geist) examines the reverse direction. [Secondary, blog; Sum] — [Filling the Pail](https://fillingthepail.substack.com/p/more-timed-test-and-maths-anxiety)
- Hechinger Report (Barshay): Boaler responds "I could counter their studies with many more that show the opposite"; Art Baroody: "I have seen the damage timed tests can do to some children" and timed tests "can be used to good, no, or bad effect"; the "Science of Math" group found two experimental studies of timed tests and anxiety that did not conclude timed tests produce anxiety; the article says the evidence is mixed "even within individual studies." [Secondary, journalism; Sum] — [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-do-math-drills-help-children-learn/)
- Caviola et al. (2017) review of stress, time pressure, strategy selection and MA: 19 relevant articles; "due to the sparsity of available literature our questions can only be partially answered and currently there is not much evidence of clear associations." [Review; Abs] — [PubMed 28919870](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28919870/)

**Experiments that manipulate timing / time pressure**
- Kellogg, Hopko & Ashcraft (1999): 30 participants solved arithmetic of varying complexity timed and untimed; "the timing manipulation negatively affected arithmetic performance in both high and low anxious participants, [but] anxiety groups were not differentially affected." [Lab; adults; Abs] — [PubMed 10688526](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10688526/)
- Tsui & Mazzocco (2006/2007), Roeper Review: mathematically gifted sixth graders had worse math performance in timed vs untimed testing, "statistically significant only when the timed condition preceded the untimed condition"; higher MA or perfectionism was associated with a smaller timed-untimed discrepancy; the timed-untimed difference was significant for girls, not boys (N not in abstract). [Within-subject; grade 6 gifted; Abs] — [ERIC EJ929821](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ929821); [PubMed 20084180](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20084180/)
- Grays et al. (2017): 81 fourth/fifth graders; students with low and medium MA completed more digits correct with higher accuracy than high-MA students, but accuracy and digits correct remained consistent across baseline, control and explicit timing when condition and anxiety level were both considered. [Within-subject; grades 4-5; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1141244](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1141244)
- Maki, Zaslofsky, Codding & Woods (2024), J School Psychology: within-subjects, 113 students (38 fourth, 75 fifth graders): no significant difference in reported MA between overt and covert timing for simple (p = .27) or complex problems (p = .42); MA was higher on complex than simple tasks (p = .01); students with medium-to-high baseline MA reported higher MA on complex problems under covert timing (p = .02, η² = 0.13). [Within-subject; grades 4-5; Abs] — [PubMed 39251303](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39251303/)
- Orbach et al. (2026): 311 third/fourth graders (51.4% girls) took reading and math achievement tests timed and untimed; "Time pressure increased state anxiety in both reading and math"; math performance was negatively related to trait and baseline state MA in both conditions; for reading, anxiety-performance links appeared only under time pressure; fluid intelligence was the strongest predictor of performance. [Within-subject; grades 3-4; Abs] — [PubMed 42199315](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42199315/)
- Yao et al. (2026): production-type arithmetic (generating answers) elicited higher state MA than decision-type tasks, especially for high-trait-MA individuals; state MA decreased during arithmetic (habituation) and after (relief); higher trait MA was linked to slower solving of problems with a carry or borrow. [Lab; adults (sample not in abstract); Abs] — [PMC12847727](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12847727/)
- Schwartz et al. (2015): 81 of 84 Master of Physical Therapy students took a timed and an untimed anatomy practical; "Students performed significantly higher on the untimed test (P = 0.005), with a significant reduction in test anxiety (P < 0.001)"; 83% (n = 69) preferred untimed; untimed took ~8 minutes longer. (Test-taking, graduate health professions, not practice.) [Within-subject; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1079574](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1079574)
- Bentley et al. (2026): N = 35 undergraduates, randomized crossover of bell-paced (60 s/station) vs self-paced anatomy lab exams; performance did not differ (82.8% ± 6.8% vs 81.1% ± 8.1%, β = 2.07, 95% CI [-2.45, 6.58]); bell pacing increased "reactive" test anxiety (M = 5.67, p < .01); 23/35 (66%) preferred bell pacing. [RCT crossover; undergraduates; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1506111](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1506111)
- Jazbutis et al. (2023): time pressure during learning elevated perceived stress (adults, n = 211; details in Section 1). [RCT-iso; Full] — [Jazbutis 2023](https://jnc.psychopen.eu/index.php/jnc/article/view/7721)

**MA-performance correlations (meta-analyses)**
- Barroso et al. (2021): 747 effect sizes (research 1992-2018), r = -.28 (small-to-moderate), significant in all moderator subgroups except the approximate number system; moderators included grade level, math ability level, scale and assessment types; published studies reported stronger correlations than unpublished. [Meta; Abs] — [PubMed 33119346](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33119346/)
- Namkung, Peng & Lin (2019): 131 studies, 478 effect sizes, r = -0.34; stronger for advanced multistep tasks than foundational tasks and for measures that affected/reflected student grades (final exam, course grade, GPA) than research-administered measures. [Meta; school-aged; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1213924](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1213924)
- Hembree (1990) meta-analysis (abstract only): MA "depresses performance." Hembree (1988) meta-analysis of 562 studies on test anxiety (correlates, causes, effects, treatment). [Meta; Abs] — [ERIC EJ407520](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ407520); [ERIC EJ375719](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ375719)
- Community-college data: Jamieson et al. (2021), 478 community-college students from 30 math classrooms: higher MA associated with perceiving more demand and fewer coping resources in exams, lower testosterone on exam days relative to baseline, and worse exam performance, partially mediated by stress appraisals. [Corr; community college; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1307985](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1307985)
- Jenifer, Levine & Beilock (2023): 72 introductory-calculus college students retrospectively reported how they studied for the final; the study tested whether high-MA students use effortful study strategies (including solving practice problems) less; the abstract in the retrieved record is cut off before results. [Corr; college calculus; Abs, results unread] — [ERIC EJ1368880](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1368880)

**Working memory and choking under pressure**
- Ashcraft & Kirk (2001): high-MA individuals had smaller WM spans, especially on a computation-based span task; the reduced capacity produced "a pronounced increase in reaction time and errors when mental addition was performed concurrently with a memory load task"; authors call the effect a "transitory disruption of working memory." [Lab; Abs] — [PubMed 11409101](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11409101/)
- Ramirez et al. (2016): large dataset of first and second graders; MA related to less use of advanced problem-solving strategies, which related to achievement, and this relation was strongest in children with the highest WM capacity ("children who have the highest cognitive capacity avoid using advanced problem solving strategies when they are high in math anxiety"). [Corr; grades 1-2; Abs] — [PubMed 26342473](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26342473/)
- Beilock & Carr (2005): "only individuals high in working memory capacity were harmed by performance pressure, and, furthermore, these skill decrements were limited to math problems with the highest demands on working memory capacity." [Lab; Abs] — [PubMed 15686575](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15686575/)
- Beilock, Kulp, Holt & Carr (2004), 3 experiments: pressure harmed only unpracticed problems with heavy WM demands; practicing high-demand problems until answers were directly retrieved "eliminated choking under pressure"; with a high-pressure test on problems practiced 1, 2 or 50 times each, infrequently practiced high-demand problems were still performed poorly under pressure, "whereas problems practiced 50 times each were not." [Lab; Abs] — [PubMed 15584808](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15584808/)
- DeCaro et al. (2011): pressure that induces distraction (performance-contingent outcomes) hurts rule-based, WM-reliant learning heavily; explicit-monitoring pressure harms other skills (abstract truncated in retrieved record). [Lab; Abs] — [PubMed 21574739](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21574739/)
- Mattarella-Micke et al. (2011): for higher-WM individuals high in MA, higher post-task salivary cortisol went with worse math performance; for higher-WM individuals low in MA, higher cortisol went with better performance; lower-WM individuals' performance did not depend on cortisol or MA. [Lab; Abs] — [PubMed 21707166](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21707166/)
- Note on construct: the retrieved Beilock-lab abstracts describe "pressure" (e.g., performance-contingent outcomes), not time pressure specifically; the pressure manipulations were not read for each paper.

**Facts about reducing pressure effects (included only as cost-modifier facts)**
- Oudejans & Pijpers (2009): 17 expert basketball players (5 weeks of free throws) and 17 expert dart players trained with or without induced anxiety; only after training with anxiety did performance not deteriorate in an anxiety posttest. Sensorimotor tasks, experts, small N. [RCT; Abs] — [PubMed 19123115](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19123115/)
- Ramirez & Beilock (2011): two laboratory and two randomized field experiments; a brief expressive-writing task immediately before a high-stakes test improved exam scores, "especially for students habitually anxious about test taking." Park et al. (2014): N = 80; writing about exam worries reduced the high-MA/low-MA performance gap. [RCT; Abs] — [PubMed 21233387](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21233387/); [PubMed 24708352](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24708352/)
- Jamieson et al. (2016): 93 community-college developmental-math students randomized to stress reappraisal vs placebo; reappraisal students reported less math evaluation anxiety and had improved math exam performance, mediated by coping-resource appraisals. Jamieson et al. (2010): reappraisal improved GRE-math scores on a practice GRE and on actual GRE scores 1-3 months later. [RCT; Abs] — [ERIC ED566260](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED566260); [PubMed 20161454](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20161454/)
- Bellinger, DeCaro & Ralston (2015): Study 2, undergraduate engineering majors in calculus: mindfulness indirectly benefited high-stakes quizzes and exams by reducing cognitive test anxiety, but not lower-stakes homework. [Corr/mediation; college; Abs] — [PubMed 26372885](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26372885/)

### Inferences
- The causal claim "timed practice/tests cause the onset of MA" was not tested by any retrieved study; what exists is short-term: time pressure raises state anxiety/perceived stress (Orbach 2026; Jazbutis 2023; Schwartz 2015) and lowers performance in some designs (Kellogg 1999; Tsui & Mazzocco; Schwartz), but without evidence of differential harm to high-MA individuals (Kellogg 1999; Grays 2017) and without an anxiety difference between overt and covert timing in grades 4-5 (Maki 2024).
- Reported effects are concentrated in children (grades 1-6) or small adult lab samples; the only college/community-college data retrieved are correlational or non-timing interventions (Jamieson 2016, 2021; Bellinger 2015).
- MA's association with performance is larger for multi-step tasks and grade-bearing exams (Namkung 2019), the very tasks an exam-pace requirement would concern.
- The choking literature supports a specific mechanism by which practice reduces vulnerability: high-WM-demand problems practiced to retrieval (50 repetitions) did not choke (Beilock 2004), while problems that still require computation remain vulnerable; this is about pressure generally.
- Whether anxiety effects of timing depend on how timing is framed (overt vs covert, individual vs group goal, rate goals) is only weakly addressed (Maki 2024; WWC panel advice); no study tested these framings experimentally.

### Gaps
- No longitudinal or developmental study tests whether timed practice/testing causes MA onset; Boaler's "one third" figures have no retrieved primary data.
- No study of timed practice's effect on anxiety or performance among high-school/college STEM students was found (Maki, Grays, Orbach are grades 3-5; Kellogg is a 30-person lab sample).
- Tsui & Mazzocco's N, Kellogg's population, and most Beilock-lab sample sizes/populations were not in the abstracts; Ramirez et al. (2013) and Ashcraft & Moore (2009) were not retrieved; Agarwal et al. (2014, retrieval-practice programs reduce middle/high-school test anxiety) was only verified bibliographically (J Applied Research in Memory and Cognition 3(3):131-139), results unread.
- Hembree (1990) specifics (number of studies, effect sizes, treatment results) were not retrieved.
- Secondary sources (youcubed, Filling the Pail, Hechinger) were read via automated summaries; claims about what Boaler cites should be checked against the originals.

## 5. Exam pacing: how students allocate time on exams; does practicing with a pace target change it?

### Takeaway
Observational data show a weak or non-monotonic relation between time used on an exam and score; extra time yields small average gains on speeded standardized sections (SAT math < 30 points) and none in two online-quiz settings; high scorers benefit most from extra time. No retrieved study tests whether practicing against a pace target changes exam pacing or exam scores.

### Cited Findings
- Mowrey et al. (2022), ASEE: 14 midterm/final exam sets in six undergraduate engineering courses (class sizes 11-54), 325 usable student-exam data points; Proportion Time Used = time spent / time available (allotted 50, 75 or 110 minutes; some accommodated up to 2x). Exam Grade = 94.642019 - 12.105213 x Proportion Time Used (p = 0.0058) but R² = 0.023; A and B students used significantly less time than D and F students (ANOVA p = 0.0196; F vs A difference 0.109 of allotted time, p = 0.0128); mean grade 84.07 (SD 14.31), mean proportion of time used 0.873 (SD 0.181). The authors conclude time "was not found to be a reliable predictor of performance." [Corr; college engineering; Sum] — [Mowrey et al. 2022](https://peer.asee.org/apropos-students-temporal-exam-effort-and-performance.pdf)
- Hlasny (draft, AEA 2014 conference): 631 exam-taking observations from 468 introductory-economics students at Michigan State and Ewha Womans University over nine semesters; mean time on exam 61.73 minutes (SD 22.07); a "significant fraction" submitted at the last minute; students who forsake 0% or more than 40% of allowed time score worse than those who leave only a few minutes early (non-monotonic); students who submit significantly early tend to score less. Draft/working paper, multiple-choice exams. [Corr; undergraduates; Sum] — [Hlasny draft](https://aeaweb.org/conference/2014/retrieve.php?pdfid=1267)
- Bridges (1982): college students, three course exams; "no linear or curvilinear relationships between rank or time and performance on individual tests"; average scores of first-quartile finishers did not differ significantly from the rest. [Corr; college; not peer reviewed; Abs] — [ERIC ED223661](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED223661)
- Chandler & Mako (1987): 88 college students; with ACT ability controlled, higher state anxiety went with lower scores on first and second exams but no difference in exam completion time. [Corr; college; Abs] — [ERIC ED290117](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED290117)
- Osgood et al. (2017): 362 students at two public universities; trait self-control (r = 0.174) and trait mindfulness (r = 0.311) predicted self-reported effective time management on timed exams. [Corr; college; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1213605](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1213605)
- Goldhammer et al. (2014): time-on-task effect on success is positive for controlled-processing problem solving and negative for routine reading tasks, and its size shifts with skill (details in Section 3). [Corr; ages 16-65; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1054276](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1054276)
- Extra-time evidence: SAT math gains < 30 points with more time per question, none for scorers <= 400, larger for high scorers (Bridgeman 2004); 50% more time on online quizzes did not raise scores (Portolese 2016); Alster (1997): 88 community-college students, algebra tests under timed vs extended time, students with learning disabilities' performance did not differ significantly from the timed or extended-time scores of students without LD; Lewandowski et al. (2007): time-and-a-half did not give students with ADHD a differential boost on a speeded math task (grades 5-7). [QED/experimental; Abs] — [Bridgeman et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2004.tb01167.x); [ERIC EJ1123527](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1123527); [ERIC EJ542711](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ542711); [ERIC EJ803225](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ803225)
- Pace-structured testing: bell-paced (60 s/station) vs self-paced anatomy lab exams gave equivalent scores (82.8% vs 81.1%), with higher reactive anxiety under bell pacing (Bentley 2026, N = 35); physical-therapy students scored higher and were less anxious on an untimed vs timed practical (Schwartz 2015, n = 81). [Abs] — [ERIC EJ1506111](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1506111); [ERIC EJ1079574](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1079574)
- Gernsbacher, Soicher & Becker-Blease (2020) argue time-limited tests are "less valid, less reliable, less inclusive, and less equitable" and recommend untimed power tests (abstract-level only; data not retrieved). [Theory/advocacy; Abs] — [Gernsbacher et al. 2020 (preprint)](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/7xmn8)
- Miller et al. (2011) used an explicit time score (10 points if under a preset limit, -1 per minute over) inside homework modules, with students' completion times falling across attempts (e.g., one student 20:12 to 10:29 while score rose 55.8 to 90); exam pacing itself was not measured. [QED; Sum] — [Miller et al. 2011](https://peer.asee.org/18025.pdf)

### Inferences
- In the retrieved exam data, time used is a weak predictor of score (R² = 0.023 in engineering exams; no relation in Bridges 1982), and the relation can be non-monotonic (Hlasny), so finishing early is not by itself a reliable marker of mastery.
- Extra exam time buys small average gains and mainly for stronger students (SAT), and no gains in two online-quiz settings; this bears on how much of a score is "pace-limited", but these studies manipulate exam time, not practice pace.
- No evidence was found on pacing behavior change from pace-target practice; the only practice-with-time-score example (Miller 2011) did not measure exam pacing.

### Gaps
- No study of pace-target practice -> exam time allocation (skipping, ordering, per-question time) was found.
- Most pacing data are conference papers/drafts or abstract-only records; Hlasny's regression coefficients for performance were not reported in the retrieved text.
- No data on pacing in physics, calculus, or AP exams; Gernsbacher et al.'s underlying data were not retrieved.
- Rapid-guessing/response-time-effort literature (e.g., Wise & Kong) was not retrieved.

## 6. Effect sizes, sample sizes, ages (prioritizing high school and college): quick-reference table

### Takeaway
Direct timed-vs-untimed contrasts come from grade 1-6 samples (n up to 591 analysed) and small adult/undergraduate lab or classroom samples (30-211); the only high-school-age evidence is SAT test-taker data, and the only college STEM practice evidence is a small engineering historical-control pilot. Effect sizes for speeded vs non-speeded practice are in the .5 range for arithmetic outcomes in grade 1 and 0.09-0.42 in the WWC's three timed-vs-untimed contrasts.

### Inferences
- Age coverage is thin where it matters most for a practice app aimed at HS/college STEM: no timed-vs-untimed practice trial was found at those levels, and the only STEM-course timed-practice evidence is one pilot.
- Where ES exist for speeded practice they come from first graders learning counting-based arithmetic; transfer of those ES to college algebra/calculus procedures is untested.
- Sample sizes in the timing-and-anxiety experiments are small (30 to 311), and effect sizes are rarely reported (η² = 0.13 in Maki 2024 is the main one retrieved).

### Gaps
- Effect sizes for Jazbutis et al. beyond reported ηp² values, and effect sizes for Kellogg 1999, Tsui & Mazzocco, Grays 2017, Woodward 2006 and Tournaki 2003, were not in the retrieved records.
- WWC study-level identities, grade distribution, and per-study ES for the 27 studies were not extracted (only domain-level pooled values and the three timed-vs-untimed contrast values).
- Rows marked "Abs" rest on abstracts only; rows marked "Sum" rest on automated summaries and should be rechecked against the source before being quoted in a report.
