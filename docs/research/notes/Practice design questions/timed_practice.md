# Timed Practice (Practicing Under Time Pressure / at Exam Pace) in Math and STEM Problem Solving

Scope: facts only, no design recommendations. Compiled 2026-11-02 from ERIC, PubMed/Europe PMC and Crossref records, plus full texts that could be retrieved (Fuchs et al. 2013 via PMC; Jazbutis et al. 2023; the WWC 2021 practice guide; Delaney et al. 1998; Peltier's 2024 timed-practice meta-analysis preprint; two ASEE conference papers; one AEA conference draft) and web pages (youcubed, Filling the Pail, Hechinger Report, a Peltier blog post). After drafting, the quotes and numbers were re-checked against those full texts and abstract records. Not searched: Google Scholar. Paywalled full texts were not read (abstract only). Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex were rate-limited and not used. Where an abstract does not state a design detail (for example whether practice was timed, or participant age), the entry says so rather than assuming.

Evidence labels (my judgment of design strength for the specific claim, not journal prestige):
- **RCT-iso** = randomized contrast that isolates timing/speed (confounds noted where found)
- **RCT-bundle** = randomized or meta-analytic evidence in which a timed activity is one element of a multi-component intervention (timing not isolated)
- **QED** = quasi-experiment, non-random or historical comparison
- **Lab** = controlled laboratory experiment
- **Meta** = meta-analysis or systematic review
- **Corr** = correlational / observational / archival
- **SCD** = single-case design
- **Theory** = theory, narrative review, or expert-panel opinion
- **Secondary** = news/blog/summary page; primary not independently verified
- Read depth: **Full** = full text read (web pages and conference papers re-read in full); **Abs** = abstract/record only (ERIC, PubMed, Crossref or Europe PMC record)

## 1. Does timed practice improve timed-exam performance (and learning) vs untimed practice? (RCTs / quasi-experiments in math, physics, engineering, standardized tests)

### Takeaway
No retrieved study randomizes timed vs untimed practice and then measures performance on a later timed exam in high-school or college math, physics, or engineering, or on SAT/ACT/GRE/AP. The nearest evidence is (a) one large first-grade RCT in which speeded practice beat non-speeded practice on arithmetic-type outcomes (but confounded with practice format and strategy emphasis), (b) a WWC "strong" rating whose 27 K-6 studies bundle timed activities with other components and include only three timed-vs-untimed contrasts, (c) a 211-adult lab RCT in which timed learning did not raise accuracy on delayed tests, and (d) one small engineering quasi-experiment in which timed homework modules coincided with higher in-class exam scores but no final-exam difference. A 2024 preprint meta-analysis of school-based timed-practice studies (grades 1-12, outcomes limited to fact and computation fluency) reports a pooled g = .71 for 7 group-design studies that isolate timing parameters, but only 2 of its 36 studies involved high-school students, none involved college students, and none measured math anxiety.

### Cited Findings

**Randomized timed/speeded vs untimed/non-speeded contrasts**
- Fuchs et al. (2013), J Educ Psychol. At-risk first graders (random assignment at student level, stratified by class) received 16 weeks x 3 sessions/week x 30 min of tutoring: 25 min number-knowledge instruction plus 5 min of practice. Speeded practice ("Meet or Beat Your Score") gave 90 seconds to answer a stack of flash cards (errors were corrected immediately by counting while the clock kept running), the score was charted, and the child then had two chances to meet or beat it; non-speeded practice was space-theme games with manipulatives reviewing the day's lesson, with tutors encouraging "a variety of number-principle strategies" and no deliberate practice of counting strategies. Analysed n = 195 speeded, 190 non-speeded, 206 at-risk no-tutoring control (plus 300 low-risk classmates; 648 at-risk students were originally assigned). Both tutoring conditions beat control on all 4 outcomes; speeded > non-speeded on simple arithmetic and 2-digit calculations; "comparable" on number knowledge and word problems. Reported ES (Discussion): non-speeded vs control .38; speeded vs non-speeded .51; speeded vs control .87; vs low-risk classmates .39 for speeded (gap narrowed) vs .07 favoring low-risk for non-speeded; number knowledge ES .11 favoring speeded; word problems ES .04 favoring non-speeded. A footnote (fn 4) gives different values for the same contrasts (speeded vs control ES = .76, t(351) = 8.10; non-speeded vs control ES = .28; speeded vs non-speeded ES = .50, t(348) = 5.30, p < .001; gap ES .36 vs .11); I could not tell which outcome/analysis that footnote refers to. The authors report that speeded practice generated many more correct responses ("40:7 correct responses per session"); the two conditions therefore also differed in practice format (flash cards vs games) and strategy emphasis, and the authors list the common elements as encouragement of strategic behavior and immediate corrective feedback. On complex calculations the authors report speeded vs non-speeded ES = .21, speeded vs control ES = .69, and speeded vs low-risk classmates ES = .01; they "found no evidence that speeded practice inhibits development of number knowledge or word-problem skill", and caution that findings "should be generalized only to speeded practice that incorporates similarly sound, theoretically motivated instructional design." [RCT-iso, confounded; grade 1; Full] — [Fuchs et al. 2013 (PMC full text)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3779611/); [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1006608)
- Hechinger Report (Jill Barshay, 30 May 2023) describes the same study as "one of the few to directly test timed versus untimed conditions" and says "we need more studies to replicate her findings before we can conclude that speed is considerably more effective and harmless to children." Its description (untimed games vs flash cards "within 60 seconds") differs from the study's method text (non-speeded games with manipulatives; 90-second flash-card turns). It also states that a multi-year randomized study of speed drills with later math achievement and math anxiety outcomes "doesn't exist." [Secondary; Full] — [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-do-math-drills-help-children-learn/)
- WWC 2021 practice guide, Appendix C: only 3 of the 27 studies supporting Recommendation 6 contrasted a timed fluency activity with an otherwise identical untimed activity. "All impacts were positive, however not all were statistically significant": study 1 g = 0.42* (counting and cardinality) and g = 0.09 (whole-number computation); study 2 g = 0.36* (whole-number computation); study 3 g = 0.20 (not significant). [RCT-iso x3 inside a WWC review; K-6 struggling students; Full] — [WWC 2021006 practice guide, p. 130](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC2021006-Math-PG.pdf)
- Peltier (2024), "We're Talking about Practice. Practice: A Meta-Analysis of Timed Math Practice" (EdArXiv preprint that "has not undergone peer-review"; single author; screening and coding by one person). Inclusion: pre-K to grade 12, school-based, intervention focused on timed math practice, group or single-case design; search July 2024. 18 group-design studies (N = 1,283; 146 effect sizes) and 18 single-case studies (N = 144; 64 ES) qualified. Studies "isolating timed practice": 7 group-design studies (N = 791; 77 ES), mean g = .71 (95% CI .35 to 1.07; 95% prediction interval -.32 to 1.74; Q[76] = 185.1, p < .0001), and 16 single-case studies (N = 138; 47 ES), mean g = .90 (95% CI .37 to 1.42; prediction interval -2.04 to 3.84). All group studies: g = .42 (CI .26 to .58; k = 18); all single-case studies: g = .96 (CI .50 to 1.42; k = 18). Where timed practice was the only isolated variable: group g = .50 (SE .11; CI .27 to .72; 5 studies, 62 ES); single-case g = 1.01 (SE .36; CI .29 to 1.74; 10 studies, 26 ES). By WWC rating: group studies meeting standards g = .74 (CI .185 to 1.30; 5 studies, 72 ES); single-case studies meeting standards g = .10 (CI -.56 to .76; 7 studies, 19 ES) vs g = 2.43 (CI 1.52 to 3.35) for single-case studies meeting standards with reservations; Egger tests showed funnel-plot asymmetry for the single-case models (isolating subset p < .0001) but not the group models (isolating subset p = .13). [Meta; K-12; preprint; Full] — [Peltier 2024 preprint](https://doi.org/10.35542/osf.io/9bjtg)
- What the Peltier "isolating" studies compared (from the preprint's study tables): the preprint describes "seven of the group design studies" as aiming to evaluate how the timing of math practice may influence learning rates (practice time per day: Duhon 2022, Knowles 2010, Hernandez-Nuhfer 2020, Wolf 2018; distributed vs massed practice: Powell 2022, Schutte 2015, Bullard 2019), with a treatment-as-usual control in Duhon 2022 and Knowles 2010; these appear to be the 7 isolating group studies (my inference; the preprint does not list them in one place). The single-case set included four overt-vs-covert timing comparisons (Grunke 2019; Miller 1995; Van Houten & Thompson 1976; Van Houten & Little 1982) and three timed-vs-another-intervention comparisons (cover-copy-compare, taped problems, Math to Mastery). Outcomes were "exclusively declarative facts and/or computational tasks", mostly digits correct per minute. Grades 1 to 12, most in grades 1-3, only two studies in grades 6-8 and two in high school; postsecondary samples were excluded by design. "Zero studies evaluated math anxiety" and 4 of 36 evaluated student perceptions. The author lists as limitations single-person screening and coding, eclectic single-case designs, and a literature drawn mainly from school psychology, special education and behavior analysis. [Meta; K-12; preprint; Full] — [Peltier 2024 preprint](https://doi.org/10.35542/osf.io/9bjtg)
- The two high-school-age studies in the Peltier set: Fuchs, Bahr & Rieth (1989), 20 high-school students with learning disabilities in computer-assisted math computation drill-and-practice, which compared assigned vs self-selected goals and contingent vs non-contingent gameplay ("Self-selected goals were more effective; no differences existed between contingency groups"; not a timed-vs-untimed contrast); and Van Houten & Little (1982), three adolescents (the preprint codes ages 14-17, intellectual disability) whose "accuracy and rate of correctly completed math problems increased" when the assignment time limit was shortened (the preprint's table lists 5-minute vs 20-minute conditions and rates the comparison "does not meet" WWC standards). [Group/single-case; adolescents with disabilities; Abs for the ERIC records, Full for the preprint tables] — [ERIC EJ402426](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ402426); [ERIC EJ276521](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ276521); [Peltier 2024 preprint](https://doi.org/10.35542/osf.io/9bjtg)
- Jazbutis, Wiseheart, Radvansky & McNeil (2023), J Numerical Cognition. Adults (n = 211 analysed; ages 18-40, M = 25.2, SD = 5.9; recruited via Prolific; timed n = 104, untimed n = 107) learned an unfamiliar 17- or 19-times table over 3 sessions in a 5 (spacing: 1, 2, 7, 10, 14 days) x 2 (timed vs untimed) design. Timed condition: goal of >= 15 correct in 30 seconds on 20-problem sets with immediate scoring and redo of misses; untimed: same sets, time not shown. Tests (immediate, ten-day delay, end of semester ~9 weeks after start) used a 90-second per-question limit that was not presented as a beat-the-clock. Results: no evidence that timed practice produced higher accuracy than untimed (F(1,186) = 1.98, p = .16, ηp² = .01, BF10 = 3.23; means in the opposite direction); timed learners were less accurate on the immediate test (test phase x time pressure F(2.71, 503.31) = 3.67, p = .015, ηp² = .019) but more accurate on a transfer test (M = .87 vs .80, F(1,207) = 4.12, p = .04); no main effect of time pressure on response time (F(1,193) = .46, p = .50) but a spacing x time-pressure interaction (F(4,193) = 3.44, p = .01, ηp² = .07): timed practice gave faster later response times with 1- and 2-day spacing and slower with 7-, 10- and 14-day spacing (F(1,199) = 13.60, p < .001, ηp² = .06). Time pressure elevated perceived stress; timed and untimed practice produced similar forgetting of practiced facts, while untimed practice allowed gradual improvement on unpracticed and conceptually related facts across test phases. [RCT-iso; lab, online adults; Full] — [Jazbutis et al. 2023](https://jnc.psychopen.eu/index.php/jnc/article/view/7721); [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1400785)
- Knowles (2010) dissertation: 8-week quasi-experiment, 9 sixth-grade classes; control vs weekly 3-minute timed multiplication drill vs daily 3-minute timed drill; daily drill group scored significantly higher on the posttest than control and weekly groups; weekly group had significantly higher gain scores than control. [QED; grade 6; not peer reviewed; Abs] — [ERIC ED521638](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED521638)

**Higher-education and STEM course evidence**
- Miller, Brauer & Sharber (2011), ASEE: first linear-circuits course (EE/ME/civil/environmental majors), enhanced section N = 19 (summer 2010, 10-week term) vs a traditional section (summer 2009, same instructor, same exam material; size not stated, F df imply ~35 total). "Timed homework practice" = 3 online modules (Ohm's law; op amps; phasors/complex numbers), 30 questions x 3 points = 90 accuracy points, plus a time score of 10 points if completed under a preset limit minus 1 point per minute over; unlimited attempts with randomized values; mean 3.47 attempts per module. In-class exam % correct: Exam 1 85.7 vs 79.7; Exam 2 81.4 vs 62.3; final 70.9 vs 71.1 (no difference); in-class exams F[1,33] = 6.969, p = 0.013. Exam 3 not comparable (format differed). Authors conclude "repeatable, timed modules focusing on core concepts are an effective way to raise student performance and learning"; they note the small number of participants, and for the absent final-exam difference suggest the gains on core concepts may have been "diluted" later in the term or that the final emphasized advanced material (for 13 of the 19 enhanced-section students the final was their lowest exam). The comparison section was taught in summer 2009 with no online homework modules and identical exam material; the enhanced section ran in a 10-week summer 2010 term (spring and fall terms are 15 weeks; the 2009 term length is not stated). [QED, historical control; college engineering; Full] — [Miller et al. 2011](https://peer.asee.org/18025.pdf)
- Brothen & Wambach (2004): 15-minute limits on 10-item multiple-choice online quizzes in a college human-development course; students "spent less time on quizzes and performed better on exams when they had time limits on their quizzes"; authors attribute this to time limits reducing opportunity to look up answers, not to pace practice. [Comparison of quizzes with and without time limits (design details not in abstract); course level and N not in abstract; Abs] — [ERIC EJ683151](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ683151)
- Portolese, Krause & Bonner (2016): same online tests, experimental group given 50% more time (Spring 2015, quizzes n = 600) vs control (Winter 2015, quizzes n = 573); "more time on tests did not enhance student performance in terms of higher scores." [Non-concurrent comparison across two terms; online courses (level not stated in abstract); Abs] — [ERIC EJ1123527](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1123527)

**Acute (within-session) effects of explicit timing, not learning effects**
- Rhymer et al. (1998): 40 African-American third graders; explicit timing increased problem-completion rates and a "decreasing trend in percentage of problems correct also occurred." [Design not stated in abstract; grade 3; Abs] — [ERIC EJ583707](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ583707)
- Grays, Rhymer & Swartzmiller (2017): 81 fourth and fifth graders completed more digits correct during explicit timing than at baseline and control; math anxiety (MA) related linearly to performance, but "digits correct and accuracy remained consistent during baseline, control, and explicit timing when condition and anxiety level were both considered." [Experimental conditions (baseline, control, explicit timing); grades 4-5; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1141244](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1141244)

**Standardized tests: time limits and practice/coaching (none isolates timed vs untimed practice)**
- Bridgeman, Trapani & Curley (2004), SAT I: fewer questions per section (verbal 35 -> 27 or 23; math 25 -> 20 or 17), embedded in the 30-minute equating section of two national administrations, simulated more time per question. Verbal gains < 10 points; math gains < 30 points on the 200-800 scale; "High-scoring students tended to benefit more than lower-scoring students, with extra time creating no increase in scores for students with SAT scores of 400 or lower"; "Ethnic/racial and gender differences were neither increased nor reduced with extra time." [Experimental embedded in operational tests; SAT takers (age not stated in abstract); Abs] — [Bridgeman et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2004.tb01167.x)
- Hausknecht et al. (2007) meta-analysis of practice/retest effects on cognitive-ability tests: 50 studies, 107 samples, 134,436 participants, adjusted overall effect size .26; larger when practice was accompanied by coaching and when identical forms were used. [Meta; general; Abs] — [PubMed 17371085](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17371085/)
- Kulik, Kulik & Bangert (1984): 40 studies; students can raise aptitude/achievement test scores by taking practice tests; gain size depended on identical vs parallel forms, number of practice tests, and ability level. [Meta; Abs] — [ERIC EJ303658](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ303658)
- Bangert-Drowns, Kulik & Kulik (1983): 30 controlled studies of coaching for achievement tests; "Coaching typically raised achievement test scores by .25 standard deviations." [Meta; Abs] — [ERIC EJ294177](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ294177)
- Becker (1990): 23 reports / 48 studies of SAT coaching; on average coaching can raise SAT scores, with considerable variability. [Meta; Abs] — [ERIC EJ417569](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ417569)
- Powers & Rock (1999): >4,000 SAT takers, ~500 of whom attended formal coaching; all estimates indicated coaching effects "far less than is claimed by major commercial test preparation companies." [Corr/quasi; Abs] — [Powers & Rock 1999](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.1999.tb00549.x)
- Hembree (1987) meta-analysis of 120 studies of non-content variables on math test performance: testwiseness training, praise, word-problem pictures, and frequent testing enhanced performance (abstract gives no effect sizes or time-limit result). [Meta; Abs] — [ERIC EJ354719](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ354719)

### Inferences
- Direct evidence on the exact question (timed practice -> later timed exam performance, HS/college STEM) is absent in what was retrieved; all supporting evidence is indirect (K-6 fluency trials, adult arithmetic-fact lab work, one engineering pilot).
- The strongest controlled contrast (Fuchs 2013) bundles speed with a different practice format (flash cards vs games with manipulatives), a different strategy emphasis, and a reported 40:7 ratio of correct responses per session, so its ES of .51 cannot be attributed to the clock alone; the advantage was confined to arithmetic-type outcomes (simple arithmetic and 2-digit calculations) and was absent for word problems and number knowledge (without harm).
- WWC's "strong" rating attaches to the recommended bundle (timed activity + efficient strategy + feedback + progress charting); the three explicit timed-vs-untimed contrasts yielded four effect sizes, two significant (0.42, 0.36) and two not (0.09, 0.20).
- In the one adult RCT, timing changed later speed only conditionally (depending on spacing) and did not raise accuracy on delayed tests.
- The robust effect of explicit timing is acute (more problems per minute in-session, with accuracy flat or trending down), which is distinct from any learning effect.
- The meta-analytic evidence that isolates timing compares timing parameters (time per day, session frequency, distribution) or overt vs covert timing, on fact and computation fluency in grades 1-12 (mostly 1-3). Its pooled estimates have wide prediction intervals (group: -.32 to 1.74), the single-case estimate depends heavily on WWC rating (g = .10 for studies meeting standards vs 2.43 with reservations), and the only high-school-age studies in it involve students with disabilities (n = 20 and n = 3); no college sample is included.

### Gaps
- No RCT or quasi-experiment on timed vs untimed practice with exam outcomes in HS/college math, physics, or engineering was found (only Miller 2011, which is historical-control and small); no physics study at all.
- No SAT/ACT/GRE/AP trial isolating timed practice was found; coaching/retest meta-analyses bundle many components.
- Van Houten & Thompson (1976, J Appl Behav Anal 9(2):227-230) is coded in the Peltier preprint as a group timed-vs-untimed comparison (grade 2; meets WWC standards with reservations), but its abstract and text were not retrievable (the PubMed record has no abstract; the PMC copy is a scanned PDF). Several WWC-included studies were not retrieved individually, and the identity of the three WWC timed-vs-untimed studies was not resolved (endnotes could not be parsed).
- The Peltier meta-analysis is an unreviewed preprint with single-person coding; its OSF data files and forest plots were not examined, and the comparison condition behind each pooled effect size (no practice, untimed practice, another timed schedule) cannot be separated from the retrieved text.
- Jazbutis et al. used adults on an artificial task (unfamiliar times tables); generalization to multi-step problem solving is untested.

## 2. Fluency and automaticity: math-fact fluency research, Precision Teaching / SAFMEDS, fluency building after accuracy, transfer to multi-step problems

### Takeaway
Timed/speeded fluency components reliably raise fact and computation fluency in K-6 struggling students (WWC: strong evidence, 27 studies, bundled interventions), with transfer shown to procedural computation and 2-digit calculation but not to algebra or word problems in at least one RCT. Precision Teaching / SAFMEDS evidence is mostly single-case or small-sample, and reviews explicitly call for more research and note generalization problems. No retrieved study tests whether fact-level speed transfers to multi-step problem solving in high-school or college STEM; the nearest, a grade-8 dissertation (Algebra I / Pre-Algebra, abstract only), reports no relationship between multiplication speed-recall and higher-level mathematics achievement.

### Cited Findings

**WWC 2021 practice guide, Recommendation 6 (Timed Activities)**
- Rated "strong" on 27 studies (21 meet WWC group standards without reservations as RCTs with low attrition; 6 meet them with reservations as less well-implemented RCTs, RCTs with high attrition, or QEDs); samples "collectively included 4,336 students and 403 schools"; K-6 students with or at risk for math difficulty; interventions ranged from ~8 days to 19 weeks (22 lasted >= 8 weeks). All 27 interventions included timed fluency activities as one component; 18 targeted math-fact fluency, 5 targeted other retrieval material (e.g., fractions equivalent to 1/2), 4 both. 19 studies taught an efficient strategy for the fluency activity, 17 had students track progress, 26 gave immediate feedback. [RCT-bundle / Meta; Full] — [WWC 2021006, pp. 51, 120-121](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC2021006-Math-PG.pdf)
- Domain-level fixed-effect g across the 27 studies (all statistically significant, none negative): algebra and algebraic reasoning 0.55 (k = 3, CI 0.29-0.81); counting and cardinality 0.27 (k = 2); general mathematics achievement 0.35 (k = 5, CI 0.24-0.47); rational-numbers computation 1.55 (k = 9, CI 1.42-1.67); rational-numbers knowledge 0.57 (k = 9); rational-numbers magnitude 0.97 (k = 8); whole-number computation 0.64 (k = 16, CI 0.54-0.74); whole-number knowledge 0.09 (k = 1); whole-number magnitude 0.26 (k = 3). Because timing was bundled, these are not timing effects. [RCT-bundle / Meta; Full] — [WWC 2021006, Table C.13, p. 120](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC2021006-Math-PG.pdf)
- Panel guidance (expert opinion layered on the evidence): timed activities last 1-5 minutes and "are not the entire focus of the intervention"; add them "once students have been working on a concept over many lessons"; "Do not use timed activities to introduce and teach mathematics concepts and operations"; "giving timed worksheets alone does not support fluency"; remind students accuracy is the goal when they "race through and guess"; for anxious students use flashcards instead of long worksheets and group "meet or beat" goals. Automatic retrieval is said to give "more mental energy to understand relatively complex mathematical tasks and execute multistep mathematical procedures." [Theory / expert panel; Full] — [WWC 2021006, pp. 51-55](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC2021006-Math-PG.pdf)

**Individual trials and meta-analyses on fact fluency and transfer**
- Fuchs et al. (2009), J Educ Psychol: 133 third graders with math difficulty randomized to control, "Math Flash" (automatic retrieval of number combinations) or "Pirate Math" (word problems with attention to number combinations, procedural calculation, algebra); 16 weeks x 3 sessions x 20-30 min. Math Flash "enhanced fluency with NCs with transfer to procedural computation but without transfer to algebra or word problems"; Pirate Math enhanced word problems plus NC fluency, procedural computation and algebra. [RCT-bundle; grade 3; Abs; the abstract does not state whether the retrieval practice was timed] — [ERIC EJ861181](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ861181)
- Powell et al. (2009): 139 third graders randomized to fact-retrieval practice, conceptual fact-retrieval instruction with practice, procedural computation/estimation instruction, or control (45 sessions over 15 weeks); for math-difficulty-only students both fact-retrieval conditions "achieved comparably and outperformed" control and the procedural condition on fact retrieval; for students with math plus reading difficulty there were no significant differences among conditions. [RCT-bundle; grade 3; Abs; the abstract does not state whether practice was timed, and the outcome reported is fact retrieval] — [ERIC EJ827128](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ827128)
- Fuchs et al. (2013): effects of both practice conditions on arithmetic were partially mediated by increased reliance on retrieval, and "only speeded practice helped at-risk children compensate for weak reasoning ability"; speeded practice produced stronger learning on 2-digit calculations than non-speeded practice. [RCT-iso, confounded; Abs/Full] — [ERIC EJ1006608](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1006608)
- Woodward (2006): 58 fourth graders (15 with math IEPs), integrated approach (strategies + timed practice drills) vs timed drills only for multiplication facts; both effective for automaticity, but the integrated group "generally performed better on posttest and maintenance test measures that assessed the application of facts to extended facts and approximation tasks." [Experimental; grade 4; Abs] — [ERIC EJ786218](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ786218)
- Tournaki (2003): 84 students with and without learning disabilities taught addition by a minimum-addend strategy vs drill and practice; students with LD improved significantly only in the strategy condition; general-education students improved in both; "In a transfer task, students from both groups improved only in the strategy conditions." [Experimental; Abs; grade/age and whether the drill was timed are not in the abstract] — [ERIC EJ676182](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ676182)
- Hasselbring, Goin & Bransford (1988): "Fast Facts" computerized drill and practice developed fact recall in 160 mildly handicapped and nonhandicapped students aged 7-14, but "only when prior training for developing a declarative knowledge network is implemented." [Experimental; ages 7-14; Abs; timing of the drill is not stated in the abstract] — [ERIC EJ367483](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ367483)
- Codding, Burns & Lukito (2011) meta-analysis: 17 single-case studies, 55 participants; drill and practice with modeling produced the largest effect sizes; treatments with more than 3 components yielded higher ES than those with fewer than 3; combinations of treatment agents beat a single agent. [Meta of SCD; Abs] — [ERIC EJ927934](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ927934)
- Burns et al. (2015): 15,402 third-to-fifth graders in a national database; students with lower math skills required significantly more repetitions (p < .001) than more skilled students; facts with 4s, 5s, 6s, 7s needed more repetition than 2s and 3s; sessions needed to reach mastery decreased with grade level. [Corr/archival; grades 3-5; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1167980](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1167980)
- Montani & Smith (2004): 131 fifth graders on timed vs untimed math tests; children with math disabilities performed poorly on math fluency but within the average range on calculation. [Corr; grade 5; Abs] — [ERIC EJ808035](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ808035)
- Curry (2012) dissertation: eighth graders in Algebra I and Pre-Algebra took timed multiplication-fact quizzes (speed-recall = number of accurate answers in a 45-second time frame; fluency = a score of 35 accurate answers in one minute 48 seconds); higher-level mathematics learning was defined as an average of 80% or higher on first- and second-semester mathematics assessments and a proficient or advanced Missouri Assessment Program descriptor. "No relationship was found between higher level mathematics, multiplication fact speed-recall, and reading grade equivalency for students in Algebra I and Pre-Algebra"; the abstract concludes that the data "did not support a major contribution from multiplication fact speed-recall and fluency to higher level mathematics achievement" and calls for study at other grade levels and over longer timelines (N is not in the abstract). [Corr; grade 8; not peer reviewed; Abs] — [ERIC ED547205](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED547205)

**Dosage and format of timed practice (secondary: a blog post by the author of the Peltier meta-analysis, cross-checked against the preprint's study tables)**
- Duhon (2022) RCT: eight timing schedules (8x/day down to every other week) plus no-practice control, 120-second sessions; 120 seconds/day identified as the minimum with a statistically significant effect; 4x and 8x per day had higher post-test digits correct per minute but smaller growth per instructional minute. Poncy (2010) SCD: 240-second vs 60-second daily trial, 240 s gave faster fluency gains and the blog reports students did not generalize from addition facts to the related subtraction facts as tested. Fontenelle (2022) SCD, second grade: 210-s daily trial produced faster learning rates than 60 s; taped problems and explicit timing produced similar rates. Wolfe (2018): without goal setting/feedback, growth did not differ by frequency. Schutte (2015) and Powell (2022): distributed 60-second trials outperformed massed; a 10-minute break was not enough. Hernandez-Nuhfer (2020): nine conditions crossing 120/240/480 s of daily practice with set sizes of 9/18/36; per the blog, similar gains across times at set size 9, 240 and 480 s above 120 s at set size 18, and 480 s above 120 s at set size 36. Peltier writes that across both design types "most effect sizes (and studies) tested timed math practice for 5 min per day or less". The post accompanies his meta-analysis preprint (Section 1). [Secondary; Full] — [Peltier, "Timed Math Practice"](https://coreypeltier.substack.com/p/timed-math-practice)
- Villanueva (2022) dissertation: 43 third graders randomized to 1, 8 or 12 opportunities-to-respond per set of computer-based explicit-timing subtraction practice over 26 school days; dose predicted growth in digits correct per minute. [RCT; grade 3; not peer reviewed; Abs] — [ERIC ED650003](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED650003)

**Precision Teaching / SAFMEDS / behavioral fluency**
- Binder (1996): defines behavioral fluency as "that combination of accuracy plus speed of responding that enables competent individuals to function efficiently and effectively"; reports Precision Teaching discoveries linking frequency to retention, endurance (resistance to distraction) and application/transfer; says the use of frequency aims by Haughton and associates "led to formulation of empirically determined performance frequency ranges that define fluency" (the ranges are not given in the abstract); states that fluency-based instructional methods have "led to unprecedented gains in educational cost effectiveness" (the author's claim); and "invites further controlled research". Narrative review, not a controlled test. [Theory/narrative; Abs] — [PubMed 22478257](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22478257/)
- Quigley et al. (published online 2017; 2018 issue) review of SAFMEDS: "a great deal of research is still needed to clarify the SAFMEDS procedures and the benefits of SAFMEDS over traditional instruction. These conclusions are in line with broader criticisms of fluency-based instruction." [Review; Abs] — [PubMed 32004366](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32004366/)
- Quigley et al. (2021): college students learning unfamiliar Russian words and Chinese characters; basic SAFMEDS produced some learning (more correct, fewer incorrect per 1-minute timing) and all four supplementary procedures produced larger increases in correct responses. Non-math content; the abstract also says the literature indicates SAFMEDS procedures "are not well documented and have multiple variations". [Design not stated in the abstract; college students; Abs] — [PubMed 34631374](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34631374/)
- Meindl et al. (2013): college students; SAFMEDS promoted fluent responding but rates dropped on novel equivalent flashcards (generalization probes), and higher training rates correlated with larger drops; embedding multiple-exemplar training promoted generalization of accurate, high-rate responding. [Experimental; college; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1038191](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1038191)
- Gist & Bulla (2022) systematic review of frequency building and Precision Teaching with school-aged children: 11 studies, 170 participants, assessed against WWC and CEC standards; "Small to large effects were found for all included variables"; concluded the combination is effective for "a variety of academic skills" (not math-specific). [Meta/systematic review; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1328678](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1328678)
- Sleeman et al. (2021): between-group experiment, Year 5-6 New Zealand students (ages 9-10); a self-regulated-learning plus Precision Teaching basic-facts program produced rapid multiplication-fluency development that was maintained over time and changed out-of-school practice behavior (N not in abstract). [Experimental; ages 9-10; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1296240](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1296240)
- Bucklin, Dickinson & Brethower (2000): compared fluency training vs training to accuracy on acquisition and retention of a composite skill; "for skilled adult learners, fluency training can aid the acquisition and retention for both the component and composite skills" (N and effect sizes not in the record; ERIC flags it non-peer-reviewed). [Experimental; adults; Abs] — [ERIC EJ629800](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ629800)
- A PRISMA-style systematic review and meta-analysis of Precision Teaching was registered as a protocol (2023); no pooled estimate was found. [Protocol; Abs] — [PubMed 37132015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37132015/)

**Mechanism: what automaticity does to working-memory load**
- Tronsky (2005): participants practiced complex multiplication (e.g., 3 x 18); before practice the most common strategy was a mental multidigit algorithm with high working-memory (WM) load, heavier for non-retrieval strategies; after practice "retrieval was used almost exclusively," "practice reduced working memory involvement to minimal levels," and there was no relationship between automaticity level and WM load. [Lab, pre/post practice; participant age and N not in abstract; Abs] — [PubMed 16383179](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16383179/)
- Beilock et al. (2004): see Section 4 (practiced-to-retrieval high-WM math problems showed no choking under pressure). [Lab; Abs] — [PubMed 15584808](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15584808/)

### Inferences
- Fluency components reliably improve the practiced skill and near transfer (procedural computation, 2-digit calculations), but the one test that targeted fact retrieval alone found no transfer to algebra or word problems (Fuchs 2009), and the WWC algebra effect (g = 0.55, k = 3) comes from bundled interventions.
- In this set, timed practice is typically layered onto, or delivered alongside, taught content and strategies (WWC panel steps; Fuchs 2013 inside number-knowledge tutoring; Hasselbring: prior declarative network); in the contrasts that varied strategy instruction, strategy-plus-practice beat drill-only on application or transfer measures (Woodward 2006; Tournaki 2003, where the abstract does not say whether the drill was timed).
- Precision Teaching/SAFMEDS claims about retention, endurance and application rest largely on narrative reviews and small/single-case studies; the field's own reviewers say the benefit over traditional instruction is unclarified, and generalization to novel stimuli can drop (Meindl 2013).
- Practice that converts a procedure to retrieval removes most of its WM load (Tronsky 2005), which is the proposed mechanism by which fluency could free capacity for multi-step problems; this mechanism was shown at the single-problem level, not in multi-step problem solving.

### Gaps
- No high-school or college STEM study of fact/component speed predicting or improving multi-step problem solving was found; the nearest retrieved record is Curry (2012; grade 8 Algebra I / Pre-Algebra; abstract only, N not stated).
- Royer et al. (1999) and Tronsky & Royer (2002) on math-fact retrieval and complex math performance were not retrievable in this pass.
- Precision Teaching fluency "aims" (the specific frequency ranges, e.g., digits-correct-per-minute criteria) and their evidential basis were not retrieved; Binder's abstract says only that such ranges were empirically determined.
- Duhon (2022), Poncy (2010), Fontenelle (2022) and others were read only through a blog post and the Peltier preprint's study tables; primary papers not read.

## 3. When to introduce speed: accuracy-first vs speed-from-the-start; speed-accuracy tradeoff in skill acquisition (power law, ACT-R, Delaney et al. 1998 strategy shifts)

### Takeaway
Practice speed-ups are not a single smooth process: individual and strategy-level analyses show discrete strategy shifts (e.g., computation to retrieval) with within-strategy power-law (or exponential) improvement, and response deadlines push solvers toward retrieval. Real-world speed-accuracy relationships are inconsistent, and the sign of the time-on-task effect flips between routine and controlled-processing tasks. No retrieved study directly compares an accuracy-first-then-speed sequence with speed-from-the-start for multi-step problems. The sequencing guidance found (WWC panel, experts quoted in a news report, the discussion of a meta-analysis preprint) is expert opinion; in the retrieved trials, timed practice was delivered alongside or after concept and strategy instruction, and the most frequent participant-inclusion criterion across the studies in that preprint was at least 80% accuracy on the targeted items.

### Cited Findings

**Learning-curve and strategy-shift evidence**
- Delaney, Reder, Staszewski & Ritter (1998), Psychological Science 9(1):1-7: reanalysed four mental-arithmetic data sets: three feeling-of-knowing experiments on relatively novel two-digit x two-digit problems (half using the multiply operator, half using plus or an invented "sharp" operator; strategy, retrieval vs calculation, was reported on every trial) and one expert mental calculator who practiced for more than 600 sessions. They plotted power-law learning curves for data aggregated over participants, then separately for each participant and strategy. Abstract: "In all four experiments, plotting separately by strategy produced significantly better fits to individual participants' data than did plotting a single power function", and "improvement of solution time is better explained by practice on a strategy than by practice on a task". In the feeling-of-knowing data, retrieval was faster than calculation (intercepts differed: multiplication t(27) = 18.67, p < .001; sharp t(17) = 9.13, p < .001), while "the different strategies in these tasks did not have significantly different slopes" (multiplication t(28) = 1.63; sharp t(17) = 1.21). Regular power-law fits to data aggregated over participants already had R² .998-.999 (Table 1), so the strategy-specific advantage appears in the individual-participant analyses: in stepwise regressions the individual-strategy estimate entered first in every free-entry analysis (mean R² .537-.665) and added a mean ΔR² of .265 to .510 beyond the regular power-function estimate (Table 3; 4 to 17 participants per analysis), whereas the regular power-function estimate augmented the model for only 1 participant (ΔR² .020). [Lab; Full] — [Delaney et al. 1998 (PSU record)](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-strategy-specific-nature-of-improvement-the-power-law-applies/); [CMU PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/memorylab/publications/98_pd_lmr_js_fr.pdf)
- Heathcote, Brown & Mewhort (2000): "The power function is treated as the law relating response time to practice trials. However, the evidence for a power law is flawed, because it is based on averaged data"; across 40 data sets (7,910 learning series, 475 subjects, 24 experiments) "the exponential function fit better than the power function in all the unaveraged data sets"; averaging biased results toward the power function. [Meta-style survey of lab data; Abs] — [PubMed 10909131](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10909131/)
- Donner & Hardy (2015): 25,280 individual learning curves (500 measurements each, four cognitive tasks); a piecewise power-law model fit significantly better than a single power law; the authors infer "gradual, smooth improvement, with diminishing gains within a specific strategy" plus "a discrete sequence of strategy shifts"; after a transition performance typically dropped briefly, then surpassed earlier levels; transition rate was negatively associated with age. [Corr/modelling of lab data; Abs] — [PMC4577530](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4577530)
- Dutilh et al. (2009): in a 10,000-trial lexical-decision task, practice "not only affects the speed of information processing, but also response caution, response bias, and peripheral processing time"; the authors warn that collapsing the practice effect into one output measure may be hazardous. [Lab; Abs] — [PubMed 19966251](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19966251/)
- Lemaire & Siegler (1995): longitudinal study of French second graders learning single-digit multiplication, assessed three times in the year; speed and accuracy gains reflected four kinds of strategic change (new strategies, more use of the most efficient existing strategies, more efficient execution, more adaptive strategy choice), with children using multiple strategies at every point. [Longitudinal; grade 2; N not in abstract; Abs] — [PubMed 7897342](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7897342/)
- Anderson's ACT-R / skill-acquisition stage theory and the original power-law-of-practice papers were not retrieved from primary sources (see Gaps).

**Time pressure and strategy choice**
- Campbell & Austin (2002): adults solved simple addition under a fast deadline (respond before a 750-ms beep) vs slow (2,500-ms beep) and reported retrieval vs procedural strategy each trial; as predicted, the fast deadline increased reported retrieval use, especially for large problems (deadline x size interaction). [Lab; adults; N not in abstract; Abs] — [PubMed 12450100](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12450100/)
- Beilock & DeCaro (2007), two experiments: with multistep math problems under low- or high-pressure conditions, higher-WM individuals used computationally demanding algorithms more often and were more accurate under low pressure, but under high pressure they "used simpler (and less efficacious) problem-solving strategies, and their performance accuracy suffered"; when a simpler strategy was optimal (Experiment 2), lower-WM individuals did better under low pressure and, under pressure, higher-WM individuals "performed optimally by using the simpler strategies lower WM individuals employed". (Pressure, not specifically time pressure; see Section 4.) [Lab; Abs] — [PubMed 17983308](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17983308/)

**Speed-accuracy tradeoff (SAT): general and in real-world data**
- Heitz (2014) review: "From insects to rodents to primates, the tendency for decision speed to covary with decision accuracy seems an inescapable property of choice behavior." [Review; Abs] — [PubMed 24966810](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24966810/)
- Domingue et al. (2022), J Educ Behav Stat: 29 data sets of cognitive tasks, analysed for the speed-accuracy trade-off (SAT), which the abstract defines as the suggestion "that time constraints reduce response accuracy", in observational settings where response time may not be constrained (its relevance there "is unclear"); "inconsistent relationships between time and accuracy; marginal increases in time use for an individual do not necessarily predict increases in accuracy"; the relationship "may depend on the underlying difficulty"; "respondents who exhibit more within-person variation in response speed are typically of lower ability"; response time "is typically a weak predictor of response accuracy." [Corr, secondary analysis of 29 datasets; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1350507](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1350507)
- Goldhammer et al. (2014), J Educ Psychol: 1,020 persons aged 16-65 (German PIAAC field test); in problem solving (controlled processing) the time-on-task effect was positive and increased with task difficulty (more time associated with success), shrinking with skill; in reading (routine processing) it was negative, most negative for easy tasks, and grew more negative with skill. "Time on task has no uniform interpretation but is a function of task difficulty and individual skill." [Corr; ages 16-65; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1054276](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1054276)
- Within-session SAT in explicit-timing studies: Rhymer et al. (1998, grade 3, N = 40) found completion rates up with a decreasing trend in percent correct. Jazbutis et al. (2023) timed learners were less accurate on the immediate test. WWC panel advice flags students who "race through and guess." [Abs / Full] — [ERIC EJ583707](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ583707); [Jazbutis 2023](https://jnc.psychopen.eu/index.php/jnc/article/view/7721); [WWC 2021006, p. 55](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC2021006-Math-PG.pdf)
- Ramos-Christian et al. (2008): 38 first and second graders; preoperational children "lacked the speed of concrete operational children but achieved comparable levels of accuracy" on arithmetic problems. [Corr; grades 1-2; Abs] — [ERIC EJ796440](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ796440)

**Sequencing evidence (accuracy/strategy first vs speed first)**
- WWC panel: add timed activities after students have worked on a concept over many lessons; confirm students are competent in the strategy before the timed activity; do not use timed activities to introduce concepts (expert guidance supported by 27 bundled studies; no study varied the order). [Theory + RCT-bundle; Full] — [WWC 2021006, pp. 51-53](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC2021006-Math-PG.pdf)
- Hasselbring et al. (1988), Woodward (2006), Tournaki (2003): see Section 2 (declarative/strategy foundation preceded or accompanied timed drills; strategy-based conditions transferred better than drill-only). [Experimental; Abs]
- Fuchs et al. (2013): speeded practice was delivered inside number-knowledge tutoring with immediate error correction using an efficient counting procedure and an instruction to retrieve if confident, otherwise use the counting strategy; the authors contrast this with school practice "without sufficient scaffolding in number knowledge, in massed doses, and without support for correct responding." [RCT-iso; Full] — [Fuchs et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3779611/)
- Peltier (2024 preprint; Section 1) tested initial baseline performance as a moderator, hypothesizing that "timed math practice would be more beneficial to students with higher initial fluency scores" (instructional hierarchy: acquisition first, then fluency building; the skill-by-treatment interaction of Burns et al.). Mean baseline performance was classified as frustrational, instructional or mastery using published cut points. Among studies isolating timed practice, only frustrational- and instructional-range samples appeared. Group designs: g = 1.00 (SE .315; CI .37 to 1.625; 3 studies, 5 ES) for frustrational vs g = .615 (SE .19; CI .24 to .99; 5 studies, 72 ES) for instructional (the counts exceed the 7 studies, so some studies contributed to both categories). Single-case designs: g = 1.15 (SE .43; CI .28 to 2.01; 9 studies, 19 ES) vs g = .79 (SE .39; CI -.0003 to 1.58; 7 studies, 26 ES). The preprint writes: "Counter to the hypothesis, effect sizes corresponding to students in the frustrational range were larger than instructional for group (F[df1 = 2, df2 = 75] = 8.94, p = .0003) and SCED design studies (F[df1 = 2, df2 = 43] = 5.61, p = .007)", and calls this "an unanticipated finding"; mean baseline performance as a continuous predictor had a small positive relation with effect size (group .02, CI .002 to .04; single-case .035, CI .0035 to .07). Across all studies, group-design g was .45 (frustrational; 8 studies, 34 ES), .45 (instructional; 9 studies, 105 ES) and .06 (mastery; 2 studies, 7 ES; SE .29, CI -.50 to .63), and the preprint reports the mastery-range difference as significant (F = 9.30, p < .0001). Separately, the preprint reports that the most frequently applied participant criterion across included studies was demonstrating "80%+ accuracy on the targeted items for timed practice" (sometimes a rate criterion such as 10+ digits correct per minute instead), and that the review "excluded studies focused on acquisition (e.g., teacher modeling, guided practice)". Its discussion offers, as a hypothesis rather than a result, that "it may be more beneficial to target accurate responding during initial acquisition" and then shift to fluency targets, citing Carr & Punzo (1993) as showing that reinforcing accuracy raised accuracy while productivity sometimes fell, and vice versa. The ERIC abstract of Carr & Punzo (three students aged 13-15 with behavior disorders, self-monitoring in reading, mathematics and spelling) instead reports "gains in academic accuracy, productivity, and on-task behavior"; this discrepancy was not resolved (primary article not read). [Meta; K-12; unreviewed preprint; Full for the preprint, Abs for Carr & Punzo] — [Peltier 2024 preprint](https://doi.org/10.35542/osf.io/9bjtg); [ERIC EJ470663](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ470663)
- Burns, Codding, Boice & Lukito (2010), School Psychology Review, the skill-by-treatment interaction meta-analysis that the Peltier preprint cites: "Meta-analytic procedures were used to analyze the link between skill proficiency and interventions categorized as addressing acquisition or fluency needs. Results suggest that the skill-by-treatment paradigm may be useful for matching skill levels in math to successful interventions and illustrate the need for additional research examining fluency interventions, particularly for students with instructional-level skills." (Number of studies and effect sizes are not in the ERIC abstract.) [Meta; math interventions; Abs] — [ERIC EJ886412](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ886412)
- Hechinger Report (Barshay, 30 May 2023), expert statements on sequencing (opinion, not trial evidence). The article reports that Art Baroody "thinks it's critical that children first understand conceptually what addition and subtraction mean and develop number sense before they are given timed tests," that once a child understands the math "he believes that timed worksheets are beneficial," and that he would administer timed tests at least once a week in an elementary classroom; that Lynn Fuchs holds that students "should have already demonstrated that they know the correct answers in an untimed setting" (the article's paraphrase) and favors flashcards because she fears a long list of problems overwhelms some children; and that the Science of Math group "says the research shows that timed activities or tests shouldn't start until after a child can calculate accurately" and that such tests should be low-stakes practice, never counted toward grades. Corey Peltier is quoted that timing students on "a skill they don't know" is "a waste of time" and "also can be demoralizing and harmful." Barshay writes that advocates "disagree about the details" and that "This is an area that needs more research to guide teachers on best practices." [Secondary, journalism; Full] — [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-do-math-drills-help-children-learn/)

### Inferences
- Because practice speed-ups mix within-strategy improvement with discrete strategy shifts, a fixed time target on a multi-step task can be met by different routes (faster execution of the same strategy vs switching strategy); individual-level analyses are needed to see this, and aggregate curves can look smooth either way (Delaney 1998; Heathcote 2000; Donner & Hardy 2015).
- Deadlines measurably shift solvers toward retrieval on simple arithmetic (Campbell & Austin 2002); whether the same happens for multi-step algebra/calculus procedures was not tested in retrieved work.
- Time per item is a weak/inconsistent proxy for accuracy in real-world data (Domingue 2022) and has task-dependent meaning (Goldhammer 2014), which bears on using solve-time thresholds as a mastery signal for controlled-processing tasks vs routine ones.
- In the positive studies retrieved, timed practice accompanied or followed strategy and concept instruction (WWC panel steps; Fuchs 2013, inside number-knowledge tutoring with a counting fallback; an at-least-80%-accuracy participant criterion most often in the Peltier set), but no study contrasts this with speed-from-the-start; the order claim rests on expert opinion plus indirect evidence.
- On baseline skill, the Peltier moderator results are mixed rather than supportive of a higher-baseline advantage: category comparisons ran opposite to the hypothesis among timing-isolating studies (frustrational > instructional, the former resting on 3 group studies), were equal across the two ranges in the all-studies group analysis, with smallest effects in the mastery range (2 studies), while continuous baseline performance had a small positive association. These are between-study comparisons on K-12 fact and computation fluency, not tests of accuracy-gated vs ungated timing.

### Gaps
- No direct experimental comparison of accuracy-first-then-speed vs speed-from-the-start sequences in math or STEM problem solving was found.
- Primary sources for Anderson (1982, ACT*), Anderson et al. (2004, ACT-R), Newell & Rosenbloom (1981), Logan (1988), Rickard (1997) and Fitts & Posner stage theory were not retrieved (PubMed/ERIC lacked them; APA/book content not accessible); no ACT-R claims are made here.
- Delaney et al. (1998): the PDF was read in full, but participant counts and ages for the three feeling-of-knowing experiments were not extracted (Table 3 gives 4 to 17 participants per individual-level analysis); one of the four data sets is a single expert calculator.
- No retrieved evidence on speed-accuracy tradeoff under practice instructions ("speed emphasis" vs "accuracy emphasis") for cognitive-skill learning in educational tasks.

## 4. Costs: math anxiety and timed tests (Boaler's critique; Ashcraft; Ramirez), working memory under time pressure, choking under pressure (Beilock)

### Takeaway
Math anxiety (MA) is negatively correlated with math performance in two large meta-analyses (r = -0.34 across 131 studies; r = -.28 across 747 effect sizes), but their moderator findings disagree: Namkung et al. report stronger correlations for multistep tasks and for grade-bearing measures, while Barroso et al. report smaller correlations for math exam grades and for grades 3-5 and postsecondary samples. Lab work links MA to working-memory disruption. Experiments that manipulate timing show mixed, mostly short-term results: timing lowered arithmetic performance in a 30-participant sample of unstated age (high- and low-anxious alike), in gifted sixth graders (significant only when the timed condition came first) and on a graduate anatomy practical; time pressure raised state anxiety in grades 3-4 and perceived stress in adults; overt vs covert timing did not change reported MA overall in grades 4-5. None of the 36 timed-practice studies in the Peltier preprint (Section 1) measured MA. Boaler's page cites her own 2014 article for the claim that for about one third of students the onset of timed testing is the beginning of MA; that article was not retrieved, a blog critique finds that the newer references offered in support do not test the claim, and a news report quotes experts confirming that no well-designed experiments prove it while also stating that there is no compelling scientific evidence that timed tests are not harming children. Choking research shows pressure harms high-WM people on unpracticed, WM-heavy math problems, and practice to retrieval (50 repetitions per problem) removed the effect.

**Boaler's claims and the critique (all secondary; verify before external use)**

(2017) review of stress, time pressure, strategy selection and MA: 19 relevant articles; "due to the sparsity of available literature our questions can only be partially answered and currently there is not much evidence of clear associations." [Review; Abs] — [PubMed 28919870](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28919870/)

**Experiments that manipulate timing / time pressure**
- Kellogg, Hopko & Ashcraft (1999), J Anxiety Disord: thirty participants completed arithmetic problems of varying complexity in both a timed and an untimed condition, testing the hypothesis "that time pressure may be one component contributing to worry, and subsequent performance deficits characteristic of high math-anxious individuals". "Although the timing manipulation negatively affected arithmetic performance in both high and low anxious participants, anxiety groups were not differentially affected"; the authors conclude "Researchers may therefore have to look to other variables to explain the nature of worrisome thoughts that are theorized to disrupt the performance of anxious individuals." [Within-subject (timed and untimed conditions); participant age and population not stated in the abstract; Abs] — [PubMed 10688526](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10688526/)
- Tsui & Mazzocco, Roeper Review 29(2):132-139 (PubMed dates it 2007; ERIC lists 2006): mathematically gifted sixth graders "had worse math performance during timed versus untimed testing, but this difference was statistically significant only when the timed condition preceded the untimed condition"; children with higher MA or perfectionism "had a smaller performance discrepancy during timed versus untimed testing"; there were no significant gender differences in overall performance, MA or perfectionism, but the timed-untimed difference was significant for girls, not boys (N not in abstract). [Within-subject, order varied; grade 6 gifted; Abs] — [ERIC EJ929821](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ929821); [PubMed 20084180](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20084180/)
- Grays et al. (2017): 81 fourth/fifth graders; explicit timing (per the abstract, a stopwatch plus an explicit statement of the time limit) increased digits correct compared with baseline and control; students with low and medium MA completed more digits correct with higher accuracy than high-MA students, but digits correct and accuracy "remained consistent during baseline, control, and explicit timing when condition and anxiety level were both considered." [Conditions baseline, control and explicit timing (assignment not stated in the abstract); grades 4-5; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1141244](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1141244)
- Maki, Zaslofsky, Codding & Woods (2024), J School Psychology: within-subjects, 113 students (38 fourth, 75 fifth graders): no significant difference in reported MA between overt and covert timing for simple (p = .27) or complex problems (p = .42); MA was higher on complex than simple tasks (p = .01); students with medium-to-high baseline MA reported higher MA on complex problems under covert timing (p = .02, η² = 0.13). [Within-subject; grades 4-5; Abs] — [PubMed 39251303](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39251303/)
- Orbach, Schmitz, Fritz & Willmes (2026), Front Child Adolesc Psychiatry: 311 third- and fourth-graders (51.4% girls) completed domain-specific state and trait anxiety questionnaires, reading and math achievement tests "administered under timed and untimed conditions", and a fluid-intelligence measure. "Time pressure increased state anxiety in both reading and math, where children with higher baseline state anxiety showed reduced situational adjustment." Math performance "was consistently negatively related to trait and baseline state MA across both testing conditions"; reading performance was negatively associated with trait, baseline-state and anticipatory-state reading anxiety under time pressure, "whereas no such relations emerged in the untimed condition"; fluid intelligence "emerged as the strongest predictor of performance, with only limited evidence for moderation by anxiety." [Timed and untimed conditions (assignment not stated in the abstract); grades 3-4; Abs] — [PubMed 42199315](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42199315/)
- Yao, Huber, Li, Findik, Nuerk & Artemenko (2026), "The dynamics of state math anxiety vary by paradigm and timing during arithmetic": "production paradigms, which require generating answers, elicited higher state math anxiety compared to decision paradigms, particularly for individuals with high trait math anxiety"; state MA "decreased during arithmetic due to habituation and after arithmetic due to relief"; "Individuals with higher trait math anxiety were slower in solving complex arithmetic with a carry or borrow operation." ("Timing" in the title refers to task phases; the Europe PMC abstract describes no time-limit manipulation.) [Design and sample not stated in the abstract; Abs] — [PMC12847727](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12847727/)
- Schwartz, Evans & Agur (2015), Anat Sci Educ: 81 of 84 Master of Physical Therapy students completed a comparison of timed and untimed anatomy practical tests (anxiety measured with the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory; paired-sample t-tests; test order and assignment not stated in the abstract). "Students performed significantly higher on the untimed test (P = 0.005), with a significant reduction in test anxiety (P < 0.001)"; students who were unsuccessful on the timed test showed the greatest improvement on the untimed test (mean 20.4 ± 10%); 83% (n = 69) preferred the untimed test and 8.4% (n = 7) the timed test; students took on average eight minutes longer on the untimed test. Test-taking in a graduate health-professions program, not practice. [Paired comparison, timed vs untimed practical; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1079574](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1079574)
- Bentley, Sotto & Lombardi (2026), Anat Sci Educ: N = 35 undergraduates across two academic years completed two anatomy laboratory exams under randomized crossover conditions, bell-paced (60 s/station, forward progression signaled by a bell) and self-paced ("balanced for total time", student-directed progression). Exam performance was "consistent between pacing conditions" (bell-paced 82.8% ± 6.8% vs self-paced 81.1% ± 8.1%, β = 2.07, 95% CI [-2.45, 6.58]). "Although the overall pacing effect on test anxiety was not significant", post-hoc comparisons found that bell pacing "significantly increased mean reactive test anxiety (M = 5.67, p < 0.01)" while self-pacing did not; 23/35 (66%) still preferred bell pacing. [RCT crossover; undergraduates; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1506111](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1506111)
- Jazbutis et al. (2023): time pressure during learning elevated perceived stress (adults, n = 211; details in Section 1). [RCT-iso; Full] — [Jazbutis 2023](https://jnc.psychopen.eu/index.php/jnc/article/view/7721)

**MA-performance correlations (meta-analyses)**
- Barroso et al. (2021), Psychol Bull: 747 effect sizes (research 1992-2018), r = -.28 (small-to-moderate), significant in all moderator subgroups except the approximate number system; grade level, math ability level, adolescent/adult MA scales, math topic of anxiety scale and math assessments were significant moderators. The authors write that the association "starts in childhood, remains significant through adulthood, is smaller for students in Grades 3 through 5 and postsecondary school, is larger for math anxiety than for statistics anxiety and for certain math anxiety scales, and is smaller for math exam grades and samples selected for low math ability", and note "a tendency for published studies to report significantly stronger correlations than unpublished studies, but overall, large, negative effect sizes are underreported." [Meta; Abs] — [PubMed 33119346](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33119346/)
- Namkung, Peng & Lin (2019), Rev Educ Res: 131 studies, 478 effect sizes, r = -0.34; stronger for "advanced mathematical tasks that require multistep processes" than for foundational tasks; stronger for measures that "affected/reflected student grades (e.g., final exam, students' course grade, GPA)" than for measures that did not affect grades (e.g., those "administered as part of research"); stronger when MA was assessed with both cognitive and affective dimensions than with an affective dimension only or mixed/unspecified dimensions. Barroso et al. (2021, above) report a smaller correlation for math exam grades; the two abstracts do not reconcile this. [Meta; school-aged; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1213924](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1213924)
- Hembree (1990) meta-analysis (abstract only): MA "depresses performance." Hembree (1988) meta-analysis of 562 studies on test anxiety (correlates, causes, effects, treatment). [Meta; Abs] — [ERIC EJ407520](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ407520); [ERIC EJ375719](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ375719)
- Community-college data: Jamieson et al. (2021), 478 community-college students from 30 math classrooms: higher MA associated with perceiving more demand and fewer coping resources in exams, lower testosterone on exam days relative to baseline, and worse exam performance, partially mediated by stress appraisals. [Corr; community college; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1307985](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1307985)
- Jenifer, Levine & Beilock (2023), ZDM Mathematics Education: 72 introductory-calculus college students completed anxiety surveys and retrospectively reported how they studied for the final exam. "Solving practice problems was viewed by students as being one of the most effortful ways to prepare for a mathematics examination, and ... mathematics anxiety was negatively associated with the proportion of study time students allocated to solving practice problems during exam preparation." [Corr; college calculus; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1368880](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1368880)

**Working memory and choking under pressure**
- Ashcraft & Kirk (2001): high-MA individuals had smaller WM spans, especially on a computation-based span task; the reduced capacity produced "a pronounced increase in reaction time and errors when mental addition was performed concurrently with a memory load task"; authors call the effect a "transitory disruption of working memory." [Lab; Abs] — [PubMed 11409101](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11409101/)
- Ramirez et al. (2016): large dataset of first and second graders; MA related to less use of advanced problem-solving strategies, which related to achievement, and this relation was strongest in children with the highest WM capacity ("children who have the highest cognitive capacity avoid using advanced problem solving strategies when they are high in math anxiety"). [Corr; grades 1-2; Abs] — [PubMed 26342473](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26342473/)
- Beilock & Carr (2005): "only individuals high in working memory capacity were harmed by performance pressure, and, furthermore, these skill decrements were limited to math problems with the highest demands on working memory capacity." [Lab; Abs] — [PubMed 15686575](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15686575/)
- Beilock, Kulp, Holt & Carr (2004), 3 experiments: pressure harmed only unpracticed problems with heavy WM demands; practicing high-demand problems until answers were directly retrieved "eliminated choking under pressure"; with a high-pressure test on problems practiced 1, 2 or 50 times each, infrequently practiced high-demand problems were still performed poorly under pressure, "whereas problems practiced 50 times each were not." The authors write that the findings "support distraction theories of choking in math, which contrasts with considerable evidence for explicit monitoring theories of choking in sensorimotor skills." [Lab; Abs] — [PubMed 15584808](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15584808/)
- DeCaro, Thomas, Albert & Beilock (2011), 4 experiments on non-math tasks: "pressure that induces distraction (involving performance-contingent outcomes) hurts rule-based category learning heavily dependent on attentional control", whereas "pressure that induces explicit monitoring of performance (monitoring by others) hurts information-integration category learning thought to run best without heavy demands on working memory and attentional control"; Experiment 3 used this knowledge to design interventions to eliminate choking, and Experiment 4 replicated the monitoring-pressure effect in the serial reaction time task. [Lab; category learning and serial reaction time, not math; Abs] — [PubMed 21574739](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21574739/)
- Mattarella-Micke et al. (2011): for higher-WM individuals high in MA, higher post-task salivary cortisol went with worse math performance; for higher-WM individuals low in MA, higher cortisol went with better performance; lower-WM individuals' performance did not depend on cortisol or MA. [Lab; Abs] — [PubMed 21707166](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21707166/)
- Note on construct: the retrieved Beilock-lab abstracts describe "pressure" (e.g., performance-contingent outcomes), not time pressure specifically; the pressure manipulations were not read for each paper.

**Facts about reducing pressure effects (included only as cost-modifier facts)**
- Oudejans & Pijpers (2009): 17 expert basketball players (5 weeks of free throws) and 17 expert dart players trained with or without induced anxiety; only after training with anxiety did performance not deteriorate in an anxiety posttest. Sensorimotor tasks, experts, small N. [Experimental; random assignment not stated in the abstract; Abs] — [PubMed 19123115](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19123115/)
- Ramirez & Beilock (2011), Science: two laboratory and two randomized field experiments; a brief expressive-writing task immediately before an important test "significantly improved students' exam scores, especially for students habitually anxious about test taking" (the PubMed record lists an erratum, Science 344(6180):151, 2014, whose content was not retrieved, and a published comment). Park, Ramirez & Beilock (2014), N = 80: in the control group (sitting quietly) high-math-anxious students performed significantly worse on math problems than low-math-anxious students, and "in the expressive writing group, however, this difference in math performance across HMAs and LMAs was significantly reduced" (assignment procedure not stated in the abstract). [Experimental incl. randomized field experiments (2011); experimental (2014); Abs] — [PubMed 21233387](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21233387/); [PubMed 24708352](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24708352/)
- Jamieson et al. (2016): 93 community-college developmental-math students randomized to stress reappraisal vs placebo; reappraisal students reported less math evaluation anxiety and had improved math exam performance, mediated by coping-resource appraisals. Jamieson et al. (2010): GRE preparers assigned to a reappraisal or a control condition (N and assignment procedure not in the abstract); reappraisal participants outperformed controls on the GRE-math section of a practice GRE and, 1 to 3 months later, on the GRE-math scores in the score reports they provided. [RCT (2016); experimental (2010); Abs] — [ERIC ED566260](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED566260); [PubMed 20161454](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20161454/)
- Bellinger, DeCaro & Ralston (2015), Conscious Cogn: Study 1 (high-pressure laboratory setting): mindfulness "indirectly benefited math performance by reducing the experience of state anxiety", selectively for problems requiring greater working-memory resources. Study 2 (a calculus course taken by undergraduate engineering majors): mindfulness "indirectly benefited students' performance on high-stakes quizzes and exams by reducing their cognitive test anxiety" but "did not impact performance on lower-stakes homework assignments." [Study 1 lab; Study 2 design not stated in the abstract, college engineering; Abs] — [PubMed 26372885](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26372885/)

### Inferences
- The causal claim "timed practice/tests cause the onset of MA" was not tested by any retrieved study (none of the 36 studies in the Peltier set measured MA); what exists is short-term. Time pressure raised state anxiety or perceived stress (Orbach 2026; Jazbutis 2023), and test anxiety was lower on the untimed test in Schwartz 2015. Timing lowered performance in some designs (Kellogg 1999; Tsui & Mazzocco; Schwartz 2015), left exam scores unchanged in the bell-paced vs self-paced comparison (Bentley 2026), and increased digits correct in Grays 2017. High-MA participants were not differentially harmed in performance in Kellogg 1999 and Grays 2017, and Maki 2024 found no overall difference in reported MA between overt and covert timing in grades 4-5, although students with medium-to-high baseline MA reported more MA on complex problems under covert timing.
- Reported effects are concentrated in children (grades 1-6) or small experimental samples; the college-level timing manipulations retrieved are test-taking comparisons in anatomy (Bentley 2026, N = 35 undergraduates; Schwartz 2015, 81 graduate physical-therapy students), while the community-college and engineering data are correlational or non-timing interventions (Jamieson 2016, 2021; Bellinger 2015).
- Namkung 2019 reports a larger MA-performance correlation for multistep tasks and for grade-bearing measures, whereas Barroso 2021 reports a smaller correlation for math exam grades and for postsecondary samples; the abstracts do not reconcile the two, so the size of the association for exam-type measures in college is not settled by these sources.
- The choking literature supports a specific mechanism by which practice reduces vulnerability: high-WM-demand problems practiced to retrieval (50 repetitions) did not choke (Beilock 2004), while problems that still require computation remain vulnerable; this is about pressure generally.
- Whether anxiety effects of timing depend on how timing is framed is only weakly addressed: overt vs covert timing was tested within-subjects in grades 4-5 (Maki 2024); individual vs group goals and rate goals appear only as WWC panel advice, and no retrieved study tested them.

### Gaps
- No longitudinal or developmental study tests whether timed practice/testing causes MA onset; Boaler (2014, Teaching Children Mathematics 20(8)), the source her page cites for the "one third" figure, was not retrieved, and no primary data for the "at least a third" stress figure were found.
- No study of timed practice's effect on anxiety or performance among high-school/college STEM students was found (Maki, Grays, Orbach are grades 3-5; Kellogg is a 30-participant sample of unstated age; the college-level timing comparisons, Bentley 2026 and Schwartz 2015, concern anatomy exams, not practice).
- Tsui & Mazzocco's N, Kellogg's population, and most Beilock-lab sample sizes/populations were not in the abstracts (the PMC record for Tsui & Mazzocco is metadata only; the publisher does not allow XML full-text download); Ramirez et al. (2013) and Ashcraft & Moore (2009) were not retrieved; Agarwal et al. (2014; "Classroom-based programs of retrieval practice reduce middle school and high school students' test anxiety", J Applied Research in Memory and Cognition 3(3):131-139) was only verified bibliographically, results unread.
- Hembree (1990) specifics (number of studies, effect sizes, treatment results) were not retrieved.
- The Namkung (2019) and Barroso (2021) moderator findings on grade-bearing/exam measures point in opposite directions in their abstracts; neither full text was read.
- The secondary sources (youcubed page, Filling the Pail post, Hechinger article) were read in full, but what they say about the papers they cite (Boaler 2014; Suarez-Pellicioni 2016; Estonanto & Dio 2019; Geist 2010; Jackson & Leffingwell 1999; Murtonen & Titterton 2004; Newstead 1998; the two studies the Science of Math group cites) was not checked against those papers. One of the two studies linked in the Hechinger article (Springer DOI 10.1007/s10864-016-9251-6) was not resolved here.

## 5. Exam pacing: how students allocate time on exams; does practicing with a pace target change it?

### Takeaway
Observational data show a weak or non-monotonic relation between time used on an exam and score; extra time yielded small average gains on SAT sections with fewer questions (math < 30 points), with high scorers benefiting more, and no gain in one comparison of online quizzes given 50% more time. No retrieved study tests whether practicing against a pace target changes exam pacing or exam scores.

### Cited Findings
- Mowrey et al. (2022), ASEE: 14 exams (midterms and finals) in six undergraduate engineering courses at one research university (class sizes 11-54; one 100-level, two 200-level, three 300-level course); 350 student-exam data points, 325 analysed after excluding 25 (missing time or grade, proportion of allowed time above 1.4 treated as a recording error, and one extreme low-grade outlier). Proportion Time Used = time spent / time available (allotted 50, 75 or 110 minutes; a few students with learning accommodations had up to two times the allotted time). Exam Grade = 94.642019 - 12.105213 x Proportion Time Used (p-value = 0.0058) with R² = 0.023, from which the authors conclude a correlation may exist but "it cannot predict exam performance". ANOVA of time used by letter grade p = 0.0196; A and B students used significantly less time than D and F students (F vs A difference 0.109 of allotted time, p = 0.0128; D vs A 0.094, p = 0.0136). Mean grade 84.07 (SD 14.31), mean proportion of time used 0.873 (SD 0.181). Exams with drawing and graphing questions used larger proportions of time (one-way ANOVA p = 0.0405) without a significant effect on grades (p = 0.2224). The conclusion states time "was not found to be a reliable predictor of performance". [Corr; college engineering; Full] — [Mowrey et al. 2022](https://peer.asee.org/apropos-students-temporal-exam-effort-and-performance.pdf)
- Hlasny, "Students' Time-Allocation, Attitudes and Performance on Multiple-Choice Tests" (draft retrieved from the AEA 2014 conference site; the header reads "DRAFT – Please contact the author before quoting"): "631 observations of exam-taking behavior were collected for 468 students over nine semesters at two large universities through in-classroom observation": six semesters (summer 2003 to summer 2005) of Principles of Microeconomics at Michigan State University (about half of the observations from online courses that met offline only three times, for two midterms and a final) and three semesters (spring 2008 to spring 2009) of Principles of Economics at Ewha Womans University, Seoul; multiple-choice exams. Time taken on the exam: mean 61.73 minutes (SD 22.07; range 18-120; n = 631). The distribution of time forsaken "appears censored at zero, with a significant fraction of observations submitting their answer sheets at the last minute." For grades on the exam or in the course, "there appears to be a non-monotonic relationship: students forsaking 0% or more than 40% of the allowed time perform clearly worse on average than students who forsake just a few minutes"; the author's summary is that "Students who submit significantly early tend to score less than students who stay longer. However, students who leave a few minutes early tend to outperform those who stay the entire allowed time." The author's interpretation offers productivity gains, self-selection and "attention erosion" as possible contributors. The Tobit regressions model the share of time forsaken, not exam score; the grade-time relationship is shown descriptively (Figure 2). [Corr; undergraduates; draft; Full] — [Hlasny draft](https://aeaweb.org/conference/2014/retrieve.php?pdfid=1267)
- Bridges (1982): college students (N and courses not in the abstract) on three course-based objective exams, with order of finishing and time to completion as predictors; "Statistical analyses indicated no linear or curvilinear relationships between rank or time and performance on individual tests"; "The average scores of those finishing in the first quartile were not significantly different from the last three"; the abstract adds that the results "suggested students should be given ample time to complete exams, as speed of response is not a primary objective in most college courses." [Corr; college; not peer reviewed; Abs] — [ERIC ED223661](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED223661)
- Chandler & Mako (1987): 88 college students; with ACT ability controlled, higher state anxiety went with lower scores on first and second exams but no difference in exam completion time. [Corr; college; Abs] — [ERIC ED290117](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED290117)
- Osgood et al. (2017): 362 students at two public universities; trait self-control (r = 0.174) and trait mindfulness (r = 0.311) predicted self-reported effective time management on timed exams. [Corr; college; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1213605](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1213605)
- Goldhammer et al. (2014): time-on-task effect on success is positive for controlled-processing problem solving and negative for routine reading tasks, and its size shifts with skill (details in Section 3). [Corr; ages 16-65; Abs] — [ERIC EJ1054276](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1054276)
- Extra-time evidence: SAT math gains < 30 points with more time per question, none for scorers at or below 400, larger for high scorers (Bridgeman 2004); 50% more time on online quizzes did not raise scores (Portolese 2016, Winter vs Spring 2015 terms); Alster (1997): 88 community-college students with and without learning disabilities (LD) took algebra tests under timed and extended-time conditions, and with extended time "the LD students' performance did not differ significantly from the timed or extended-time scores of the students without learning disabilities"; Lewandowski et al. (2007): fifth- to seventh-graders with and without ADHD, time and one-half on a speed-based math task, "the ADHD group did not make more gains than the control group with extended time" (the ADHD group had lower processing speed, math fluency and achievement). [Experimental / comparison designs; Abs] — [Bridgeman et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2004.tb01167.x); [ERIC EJ1123527](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1123527); [ERIC EJ542711](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ542711); [ERIC EJ803225](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ803225)
- Pace-structured testing: bell-paced (60 s/station) vs self-paced anatomy lab exams gave consistent scores (82.8% vs 81.1%; β = 2.07, 95% CI [-2.45, 6.58]), with bell pacing raising reactive anxiety in post-hoc comparisons although the overall pacing effect on anxiety was not significant (Bentley 2026, N = 35); physical-therapy students scored higher and had lower test anxiety on an untimed than a timed practical (Schwartz 2015, n = 81). [Abs] — [ERIC EJ1506111](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1506111); [ERIC EJ1079574](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1079574)
- Gernsbacher, Soicher & Becker-Blease, "Four Empirically-Based Reasons Not to Administer Time-Limited Tests" (PsyArXiv preprint record, posted 2 April 2020): the abstract "cautions instructors against administering tests that have time limits because time-limited tests are less valid, less reliable, less inclusive, and less equitable" and encourages "untimed power tests, which are tests without any time limits or with time limits so generous all students are assured of completing all items" (abstract only; the underlying data were not retrieved). [Theory/advocacy; Abs] — [Gernsbacher et al. 2020 (preprint)](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/7xmn8)
- Miller et al. (2011) used an explicit time score (10 points if completed under a pre-determined limit, minus 1 point per minute over) inside homework modules; the authors' Table I shows three example students' completion times falling across attempts on the Ohm's law module (Student A: 20:12, 14:01, 10:29 with scores 55.8, 66, 90; Student B: 36:27, 12:02 with scores 68.4, 75.6; Student C: 50:05, 14:50 with scores 51.6, 87.6); these are selected examples, and exam pacing itself was not measured. [QED; Full] — [Miller et al. 2011](https://peer.asee.org/18025.pdf)

### Inferences
- In the retrieved exam data, time used is a weak predictor of score (R² = 0.023 in engineering exams; no linear or curvilinear relation in Bridges 1982), and the relation can be non-monotonic (Hlasny, draft), so finishing early was not by itself a reliable predictor of score.
- Extra exam time bought small average gains, mainly for stronger students (SAT), and no gain in one online-quiz comparison (Portolese 2016); this bears on how much of a score is "pace-limited", but these studies manipulate exam time, not practice pace.
- No evidence was found on pacing behavior change from pace-target practice; the only practice-with-time-score example (Miller 2011) did not measure exam pacing.

### Gaps
- No study of pace-target practice -> exam time allocation (skipping, ordering, per-question time) was found.
- Most pacing data are conference papers/drafts or abstract-only records; in Hlasny's draft the regressions model the share of time forsaken rather than exam score, and the time-score relation is shown descriptively (Figure 2).
- No data on pacing in physics, calculus, or AP exams; Gernsbacher et al.'s underlying data were not retrieved.
- Rapid-guessing/response-time-effort literature (e.g., Wise & Kong) was not retrieved.

## 6. Effect sizes, sample sizes, ages (prioritizing high school and college): quick-reference table

### Takeaway
Direct timed-vs-untimed contrasts come from grade 1-6 samples (n up to 591 analysed in one RCT; 9 classes in one quasi-experiment) and small samples of adults, undergraduates, graduate students or participants of unstated age (30 to 211 participants); the retrieved high-school-age evidence is two small studies of adolescents with disabilities inside the Peltier preprint (n = 20 and n = 3) plus SAT test-taker data (age not stated in the abstract), and the only college STEM practice evidence is a small engineering historical-control pilot. Effect sizes: speeded vs non-speeded ES .51 (grade 1 arithmetic, confounded with practice format and strategy emphasis); g = 0.09 to 0.42 in the WWC's three timed-vs-untimed contrasts; and g = .71 (95% CI .35 to 1.07) for 7 group studies isolating timing parameters in the Peltier preprint (K-12, mostly grades 1-3).

### Inferences
- Age coverage is thin where it matters most for a practice app aimed at HS/college STEM: no timed-vs-untimed practice trial was found at those levels, and the only STEM-course timed-practice evidence is one pilot.
- Where ES exist for timed or speeded practice they come from K-6 samples (Fuchs 2013, grade 1; WWC, K-6) or from the Peltier preprint (mostly grades 1-3, with two high-school studies of students with disabilities); transfer of those ES to college algebra/calculus procedures is untested.
- Sample sizes in the timing-and-anxiety experiments are small (30 to 311), and effect-size reporting is inconsistent (η² = 0.13 in Maki 2024; β = 2.07 with a CI in Bentley 2026; ηp² values in Jazbutis 2023; none in several abstracts).

### Gaps
- Effect sizes for Jazbutis et al. beyond reported ηp² values, and effect sizes for Kellogg 1999, Tsui & Mazzocco, Grays 2017, Woodward 2006 and Tournaki 2003, were not in the retrieved records.
- WWC study-level identities, grade distribution, and per-study ES for the 27 studies were not extracted (only domain-level pooled values and the three timed-vs-untimed contrast values).
- Rows whose source is an ERIC, PubMed, Crossref or Europe PMC record rest on abstracts only and were not checked against full texts; the Peltier row rests on an unreviewed preprint whose OSF data files and forest plots were not examined.
