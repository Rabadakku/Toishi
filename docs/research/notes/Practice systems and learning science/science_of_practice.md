## Q1. Successive relearning (Rawson & Dunlosky): criterion levels, number of sessions, comparison with plain spacing, and what it means for mastery criteria

### Takeaway
The best-supported recipe is: retrieve each item to a criterion of about 3 correct recalls in the first session, then relearn it to 1 correct recall in about 3 further sessions spaced widely apart (533 students across three experiments, retention tested 1-4 months later). The two factors are subadditive: a high initial criterion matters a lot if you stop there and matters little once relearning sessions are added. Gains come from the number of spaced sessions, not from extra correct answers in one sitting. All of this evidence is for declarative or conceptual material; no study tested it on multi-step math problems, and math-specific meta-analytic evidence is weaker than the general literature (spacing g = 0.28; testing vs restudy g = 0.18, not significant).

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG for declarative material] The criterion x relearning grid.** Rawson & Dunlosky (2011, *JEP: General*): three experiments, 533 students, conceptual material, more than 100,000 hand-scored short-answer responses. Items were practiced to 1-4 correct recalls in the initial session, then to 1 correct recall in 1-5 later relearning sessions; durability was cued recall and rate of relearning 1-4 months later; efficiency was total practice trials. "The effects of initial learning criterion and relearning were subadditive, such that the effects of initial learning criterion were strong prior to relearning but then diminished as relearning increased." "Relearning had pronounced effects on long-term retention with a relatively minimal cost in terms of additional practice trials." Prescription: practice to an initial criterion of 3 correct recalls, then relearn 3 times at widely spaced intervals. — [Rawson & Dunlosky 2011 (abstract via Europe PMC)](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023956)
- **[MODERATE] Classroom results and the procedure as taught.** Teaching summary of Rawson & Dunlosky (2022, *Current Directions*): initial learning = 3 correct recalls per item; relearning = 1 correct recall per item per session, at least 3 relearning sessions ("may be sufficient for maximal benefits" but the required number is not settled and may depend on the retention interval). In a psychology course (Rawson et al. 2013), successive-relearning material scored about 10% higher at the unit test (about a letter grade) and the advantage grew to about 40% nearly a month later versus self-studied material. Recalling each item once in each of three spaced sessions produced more than twice the recall of three correct recalls in a single session (Rawson et al. 2018). More than a dozen studies (vocabulary, psychology and statistics terms, probability concepts), mostly undergraduates; the procedure is time-consuming but recall time falls across sessions. (secondary) — [APS Observer, May & Scullin](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/teaching-current-directions-studying-psychopathology)
- **[MODERATE] A light criterion works in the field.** Higham, Zengel, Bartlett & Hadwin (in press, *J Educ Psychol*; preprint 2021): 212 UK intro-psychology students (of 264; completed at least 24 of 30 sessions), within-subject. Each weekly set of 20 items was practiced in 3 sessions two days apart; "relearn" used criterion = 1 correct (correct items dropped, incorrect reappeared for up to 3 passes) versus restudy, matched for exposure. On criterial tests at day 42 and 68, repeated items: relearn .55 vs restudy .41 (d = 0.705). Transfer and new items showed only marginal differences. Students preferred relearning, and by session 3 relearning was associated with lower reported anxiety (d = -0.23). Authors caution the summative exam was not analyzed and that benefits depend on repeated retrieval across sessions. — [Higham et al.](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/449677/1/Higham_et_al._in_press_JEdPsych.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Spaced retrieval vs spaced restudy, and a reactivity warning.** A preregistered series on Swahili-English pairs (three practice sessions two days apart, test two days later; Exp 3, N = 77): retrieval .65 vs restudy .55 (dz = 0.44) at every practice frequency. In Exp 2 (N = 63), requiring memory ratings during practice reversed the pattern: with ratings restudy beat retrieval (dz = 0.36); without ratings retrieval was nominally better (p = .062). The authors read this as reactivity: ratings helped restudy and impaired retrieval. Authors/title were not visible in the fetched text (DOI 10.1007/s10648-023-09809-2). — [Springer, Educational Psychology Review article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09809-2) [2023-26]
- **[STRONG for general spacing, thinner for math] Math-specific meta-analysis.** Murray, Horner & Goebel (2025, *Educational Psychology Review*): spaced vs massed practice in mathematics, 27 studies and 53 effect sizes, g = 0.28 ("robust small to medium"); isolated-learning studies g = 0.43 (10 studies, 27 effects, "larger, yet less robust"), course-embedded g = 0.24 (17 studies, 26 effects). Testing vs restudy in mathematics: 7 studies, 32 effects, g = 0.18, 95% CI crossing zero, so "inconclusive" because few dedicated studies exist. — [Murray et al. 2025 (White Rose record)](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/229807); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-025-10035-1); [teacher-facing summary notes possible publication bias in course-embedded studies](https://tipsforteachers.substack.com/p/research-bite-53-a-meta-analytic) [2023-26]
- **[MODERATE] Spaced vs massed retrieval specifically.** Latimier, Peyre & Ramus (2021) meta-analyzed 29 studies on spacing out retrieval practice episodes; effect sizes were not retrievable here. — [preprint abstract](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/kzy7u)
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED] Overlearning (practice beyond criterion in the same session).** Driskell, Willis & Copper (1992), 15 studies: overlearning improves later recall, more for cognitive than physical tasks, more with greater overlearning, but declining with retention interval; a 2025 paper quotes their discussion as "benefits ... disappear somewhere around 38 days." Rohrer & Taylor (2006): 3 vs 9 math problems in one sitting made no difference at 1 or 4 weeks. Rohrer et al.: geography/definitions overlearners were ahead at 1 week but the advantage faded. (secondary for the two Rohrer items) — [Wikipedia, Overlearning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overlearning); [Matayoshi et al. 2025](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/900/254). Earlier notes also have Arthur et al. 1998 (r = .08) and Hopkins et al. 2018 (amount of quizzing null, spacing significant).
- **[MODERATE, ALEKS A/B] Higher mastery threshold, small and shrinking retention gain.** Matayoshi et al. (2025, *JEDM*): randomized assignment of new topics (5% high, 5% low, 90% unchanged) from Apr 2023 to Nov 2024; high = score of 5, low = score of 3 (a correct answer adds 1, or 2 if it follows another correct answer; wrong subtracts 1); about 33 million data points for learning outcomes and 1,404,725 retention data points from 806,279 students. High threshold cost about 1.5 minutes more per topic (mean 6.6 vs 5.1 min) and about two more actions (7.4 vs 5.4). Retention differences mostly under 0.02 and "the gap narrows over time"; for topics where the student struggled first (wrong or explanation first) the gap reached about 0.03-0.04 at its largest. Topic-level mean gain 0.021 (5th-95th percentile 0.011-0.032), strongly related to low retention under the low threshold (r = -0.86): harder-to-retain topics gain more from a higher threshold. — [Matayoshi et al. 2025](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/900/254) [2023-26]

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: FSRS is already an adaptive form of successive relearning; add the missing criterion rule.** Define "first mastery" as 3 clean corrects (first attempt, no hint beyond level 1, fresh problem) in the acquisition phase, then require at least 3 spaced reviews each with one clean correct before a skill is treated as consolidated and allowed long intervals (the Rawson prescription mapped to problems). Within a session, a failed item is re-queued later in that session (not immediately), mirroring the "incorrect items reappear" step in the Higham procedure.
- **DESIGN: Count criterion across days, not within one sitting.** One clean correct on each of three different days should outrank three corrects in a row today (Rawson 2018: more than 2x recall). Log "distinct days with a clean correct" as a first-class field next to FSRS stability.
- **DESIGN: Make the number of relearning sessions a tunable default (3), and use exam proximity to override it.** The literature says the right number may depend on the retention interval; for a skill whose exam is 3 weeks away, three sessions inside that window are worth more than a high stability target.
- **DESIGN: Adaptive threshold instead of a fixed one.** Matayoshi's data say a higher bar pays off mainly for topics with low retention and for students who struggled early. Implement a per-skill rule: pass after 2 consecutive clean corrects if the first attempt was clean, 3 if the first attempt failed, and raise to 4-5 only for skills whose observed lapse rate is high. Matayoshi's authors themselves floated this as a speculative option; it is not yet tested.
- **DESIGN: Do not ask for per-item "how sure are you" ratings on every review without testing it.** The reactivity result (vocabulary, N = 63) suggests rating prompts can shift outcomes; use confidence capture sparingly (see Q5) and compare delayed accuracy with and without.
- **MISTAKE: treating 3 correct in a row today as mastery, letting the user retire cards, or adding extra same-day reps once criterion is met.** Overlearning gains are short-lived (about 38 days or less; null in math at 1 and 4 weeks), and Rohrer-type nulls say that time is better spent on spacing.
- **MISTAKE: quoting the 40% or letter-grade figures as expected gains for math problem solving.** Those are definition/concept recall in psychology courses. The math-specific numbers are smaller (g = 0.28 spacing; testing-vs-restudy not significant in 7 studies).
- **MISTAKE: assuming fresh-problem reviews inherit the identical-item benefit.** Higham found benefits largely restricted to repeated items; Yang (earlier notes) had identical 0.51, rephrased 0.56, untested 0.32. Fresh problems should be same-structure siblings early on (see Q8).

### Gaps
- No study of successive relearning on multi-step math or physics problems was found; a Kent State NSF-funded test in intro chemistry/biology/physics was noted in the earlier notes but no results were found.
- Latimier et al. (2021) effect sizes, the Rawson, Dunlosky & Janes (2020) "boundary condition" finding, and Janes et al. (2020) high-stakes-exam results could not be retrieved (full text blocked or abstract truncated).
- No direct experimental comparison of successive relearning against plain expanding-interval spaced retrieval without a criterion was found.
- The title and authors of the 2023 Springer vocabulary study were not visible in the fetched text.

## Q2. Interleaving problem TYPES in math: how much, when blocking is better, and discrimination training

### Takeaway
The headline classroom evidence (Rohrer et al. 2020, 54 classes, 787 seventh graders, d = 0.83) remains the strongest single result, and Brunmair & Richter's math estimate is g = 0.34 (see earlier notes). New since then, and important: a large pair of classroom experiments on a well-defined math concept (angle relations) repeatedly failed to find any interleaving/blocking effect and argues the benefit is often conflated with spacing; the IES-funded 189-classroom replication is still running (2022-2027); Donoghue & Hattie's pooled interleaving estimate (d = 0.47, from 104 effects but only 972 participants) sits in the lower half of the ten techniques, far below distributed practice (0.85) and practice testing (0.74). The defensible position: interleaving helps most when problem types look alike but need different strategies, the benefit is partly inseparable from spacing, and nobody has tested the exact queue we are building at college level.

### Cited Findings
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED, 2024] Null interleaving effect for well-defined math concepts.** Rowlandson & Simpson (*Journal for Research in Mathematics Education*, accepted 21 Oct 2024, in press): two large classroom experiments on learning angle relations compared interleaved, blocked and a "more common teaching approach"; the authors "repeatedly failed to detect an interleaving/blocking effect, despite large sample sizes," argue that advice to teachers conflates interleaving with spacing, and note that prior laboratory work did not test mathematical concepts with school students. Sample sizes and effect sizes are not in the abstract. — [Durham repository record](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/2979661) [2023-26]
- **[In progress] Preregistered replication of the Rohrer RCT.** NCER/WestEd project "A Systematic Replication Study of Interleaved Mathematics Practice" (award R305R220012, 2022-2027): Grade 7, about 70 teachers, 189 classrooms, 3,780 students, 13 states, classroom-within-teacher randomization on the ASSISTments platform, outcomes at year-end and start of grade 8, with a cost analysis. No results reported yet. — [IES award page](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/systematic-replication-study-interleaved-mathematics-practice) [2023-26]
- **[MODERATE] Hattie-style meta-analysis gives interleaving a modest, thinly supported estimate.** Donoghue & Hattie (2021, 242 studies, 1,619 effects, 169,179 participants): interleaved practice d = 0.47 from 104 effects and only 972 participants, versus distributed practice d = 0.85 (150 effects, 152,952 participants) and practice testing d = 0.74 (374 effects, 6,033 participants); re-reading also 0.47, underlining and summarization 0.44. The fetched table labels interleaving "Low" in a Dunlosky-rating column, whereas the earlier notes record Dunlosky et al. 2013 as rating it moderate (discrepancy not resolved); the authors say interleaving falls close to the moderate category. 93% of all outcomes were surface-level. — [Donoghue & Hattie 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Spacing and interleaving are entangled in the math literature.** The Murray et al. (2025) summary notes that interleaving "often comes with spacing within a course" and may add a benefit, and that textbooks' massed layout should be counteracted by revisiting topics over time. (secondary) — [Barton summary of Murray et al.](https://tipsforteachers.substack.com/p/research-bite-53-a-meta-analytic) [2023-26]
- **[MODERATE, not math] Learners block when left alone, but can be talked out of it.** Onan et al. (2024, *npj Science of Learning*; pre-registered; 91 undergraduates learning visual categories): students who organize their own study order rely mainly on blocked practice; a refutation plus metacognitive-prompt intervention significantly increased self-organized interleaving on immediate and delayed transfer tasks, and more interleaving was associated with better classification. Effect sizes not in the abstract. — [Onan et al. 2024](https://doaj.org/article/c835e588b7d24a2bb70eb40af0a640d1) [2023-26]
- Anchors from the earlier notes (not re-verified here): Rohrer et al. 2020 d = 0.83 (787 students, about 33 days later); Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic 2015 (first four problems of each new skill blocked, then interleaved; d = 0.79 at 30 days); Brunmair & Richter 2019 g = 0.42 overall, math g = 0.34 [0.11, 0.57], publication-bias-corrected 0.29, strongest when categories are similar; Samani & Pan 2021 physics 50-125% median gains; Yan, Bjork & Bjork 2016, 72% of learners prefer blocking.

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: Keep the unlabeled mixed queue, but rank its components by evidence.** The robust part is spacing (every review is already spaced by FSRS). The discrimination part (placing confusable skills near each other, hiding the skill name until after the attempt) has plausible support from Brunmair (similarity moderates the effect) but is contested for well-defined concepts. Spend engineering effort on a "confusable pair" table for skills where students really misclassify the method (u-sub vs by parts, chain vs product, permutation vs combination), not on interleaving everything.
- **DESIGN: Offer an explicit "method choice" item type** (a short prompt "which method applies and why" before solving) for confusable pairs. This tests discrimination directly and gives a measurable error type ("chose wrong method") that distinguishes it from algebra slips.
- **DESIGN: Do not let the user sort the daily queue by topic by default.** Self-organized study drifts to blocking (Onan 2024); if a "topic-only" mode exists for cramming, label it as lower-evidence and show the explanation that changed beliefs in the Onan intervention (refutation of "blocked feels like learning").
- **DESIGN: Log whether each review was preceded by a confusable neighbor** (adjacent item shares a pair tag). This produces the within-user data needed to estimate the app's own discrimination effect, since the literature cannot settle it.
- **MISTAKE: treating "d = 0.83" as the expected effect of the app's queue.** It comes from seventh graders, a particular curriculum and a specific interleaving design; the college-level support is one physics study and small lab studies, and one large null exists.
- **MISTAKE: showing skill labels or chapter names above each problem in the queue** (gives away the discrimination step).

### Gaps
- Sample sizes and effect sizes for Rowlandson & Simpson are not in the retrieved abstract; the finding could reflect lack of confusability between the angle-relation categories.
- No results yet from the 3,780-student replication.
- No college-level math RCT of interleaving was found beyond the earlier notes (Samani & Pan physics, Rohrer & Taylor small lab studies).

## Q3. Fluency and automaticity: is speed a separate goal, does timed practice help, and when does time pressure hurt

### Takeaway
Direct evidence for timed fluency practice is almost entirely from elementary-age basic-fact interventions (single-case and small group studies). It shows short daily timed trials build fact speed, gains can fail to generalize to related facts, and small doses (about 2 minutes a day) already work. There is no controlled college-level evidence that timed drills raise exam scores on multi-step problems. Time pressure is not uniformly harmful: it pushes people toward faster, retrieval-based strategies and acts mostly as a general stressor; its interaction with math anxiety is inconsistent in the 19-study review. Math Academy's strong automaticity claims have no cited evidence.

### Cited Findings
- **[MODERATE, elementary, (secondary)] Dose of timed fact practice.** A practitioner-researcher's summary of timed-practice dosage studies: Duhon (2022) RCT with eight conditions found 120 seconds of daily timed practice was the minimum dose with a significant effect, while 4x and 8x daily gave higher post-test scores but smaller growth per instructional minute; Knowles (2010): daily 180-second practice beat weekly practice and none; Poncy (2010): 240 seconds/day gave faster addition-fact gains than 60 seconds, but gains did not generalize to related subtraction facts; Fontenelle (2022, class-wide, grade 2): explicit timing and taped problems produced similar learning rates; Hernandez-Nuhfer (2020): benefit of longer sessions depended on set size (9 facts: no difference across 120/240/480 s; 36 facts: 480 beat 120); Powell (2022): a 3-hour break between sessions beat massed practice and a 10-minute break. The author notes most moderator tallies are correlational and sample/effect sizes are not reported in the post. — [Peltier, "Timed Math Practice"](https://coreypeltier.substack.com/p/timed-math-practice)
- **[WEAK, abstract only] Skill-by-treatment framing.** A meta-analysis of acquisition vs fluency math interventions (authors not shown in the fetched text) proposes matching intervention type to the student's skill level (instructional vs frustration level) and calls for more research on fluency interventions for students with instructional-level skills. The retrieved abstract gives no effect sizes. — [UMN research record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/meta-analysis-of-acquisition-and-fluency-math-interventions-with-/)
- **[MODERATE] What time pressure does.** Caviola, Carey, Mammarella & Szucs (2017, *Frontiers in Psychology*; 19 studies on time pressure and strategy use in mathematics): time pressure "generally acts as a stressor, causing suboptimal strategy selection," yet it also pushes solvers toward faster retrieval or heuristic strategies; Campbell & Austin found a 750 ms deadline raised retrieval use on large additions, where retrieval was faster and more accurate than a multistep procedure; adults under limits used simpler strategies like those children use on equivalence problems. For math anxiety the picture is mixed: Plass & Hill (anxious children worse under time pressure, effect removed mainly for boys), Tsui & Mazzocco (timing lowered accuracy, high-anxiety children changed little, low-anxiety children dropped), Kellogg et al. (additive effects, no interaction). The review concludes evidence that time pressure affects anxious and non-anxious students differently is weak and does not support a causal link from time pressure to math anxiety. The working-memory account comes mostly from social-pressure studies (Beilock; higher-WM adults were more disrupted in modular arithmetic). — [Caviola et al. 2017](https://research.unipd.it/bitstream/11577/3255605/1/Caviola_etal2017Frontiers.pdf)
- **[STRONG] Size of the anxiety-performance link.** Barroso et al. (2021, *Psychological Bulletin*): 747 effect sizes (1992-2018), r = -.28 between math anxiety and math achievement; smaller for Grades 3-5 and postsecondary samples and smaller for math exam grades than for other tests. — [Barroso et al. 2021 (abstract via Europe PMC)](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000307). von der Embse et al. (2018): 238 studies; test anxiety negatively related to standardized tests, university entrance exams and GPA, effects "small to moderate," most pronounced in middle grades; higher for tests perceived as difficult or high-stakes. — [von der Embse et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2017.11.048)
- **[MODERATE, correlational] Prerequisite mastery matters for calculus.** Sadler & Sonnert (2018, *JRME*): in more than 6,000 college freshmen at 133 institutions, "a firm grip on precalculus subjects had twice the impact of a high school [calculus] course" on college calculus grade (reported as a ratio only; the news summary does not say how mastery was measured). — [Harvard Gazette summary](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2018/07/masters-of-calculus-come-prepared-harvard-study-shows)
- **[PRACTITIONER] Math Academy's position.** Skycak argues automaticity is needed at every level (arithmetic through trig identities and calculus rules), recommends timed closed-book quizzes and rapid-fire flashcard training, and says rapid-fire training is on the to-do list. The article cites no studies. — [Skycak, "Yes, You Need to be Automatic on Math Facts"](https://justinmath.com/yes-you-need-to-be-automatic-on-math-facts/)
- **[MODERATE] Fluency as a by-product of spaced retrieval.** Recall time falls across successive-relearning sessions (see Q1), i.e., speed rises without explicit timing. (secondary) — [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/teaching-current-directions-studying-psychopathology)
- Overlearning/overpractice as an automaticity route is weak for long-term retention in math (Q1). Matayoshi (2025) shows a higher mastery threshold buys under 0.02-0.04 retention at a cost of about 1.5 minutes per topic.

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: Treat speed as a measured secondary variable, not a separate mastery gate.** Record time-to-solve per attempt, keep a per-skill median by tier, and use it in two places: (a) FSRS grading ("Easy" only for correct, independent, fast-for-this-skill) and (b) practice-test pacing (predicted time to finish a mock exam vs the real time limit). No evidence supports making speed a hard gate for ordinary skills.
- **DESIGN: Create a separate, optional "fact fluency" item class for truly retrieval-like content** (trig values, derivative/integral tables, algebra identities, unit conversions) with short rapid-fire sets of about 2-4 minutes, scheduled by FSRS like any other card and stopped once response time plateaus. Small set sizes (up to about 9-18 facts) are what the dose studies used; larger sets need more time.
- **DESIGN: Never show a countdown during ordinary reviews or hide the solution behind a timer.** Show elapsed time only after completion. Offer timed mode in practice tests and "exam boosters," ideally after the user has seen the same format untimed (so that time pressure is not the first exposure).
- **DESIGN: Use a lighter timing treatment for users with high self-reported anxiety** (a one-time optional questionnaire or a toggle), since the anxiety-by-timing interaction is inconsistent and the correlation of anxiety with college exam performance is modest (r around -.28 overall, smaller for postsecondary and classroom exams).
- **DESIGN: Prerequisite fluency checks belong at course boundaries.** The Sadler & Sonnert pattern supports the placement test and a "foundation skills before Calc 2" review (correlational).
- **MISTAKE: global timers, speed leaderboards, or "streak breaks if too slow."** Not supported, and time pressure is a documented stressor.
- **MISTAKE: importing Math Academy's fluency rhetoric as evidence.** The source cites nothing.
- **MISTAKE: assuming fact-fluency gains transfer to nearby skills** (Poncy 2010: no generalization from addition to subtraction facts).

### Gaps
- No college-level controlled study of timed practice vs untimed practice on exam performance in math was found.
- No evidence quantifying "solving speed" as a predictor of exam score beyond accuracy in college STEM was found.
- The Duhon (2022) and related dose results are read from a blog summary; effect sizes and N are not available. Grade levels are not stated for all studies.
- Tsui & Mazzocco, Ashcraft & Moore (2009) and Beilock-style "choking" studies were only read through the Caviola review.

## Q4. Mock exams, the testing effect at exam level, practice tests predicting scores, test anxiety and stress inoculation

### Takeaway
Practice testing is robust in general (classroom g about 0.5-0.67 across meta-analyses), but for mathematics alone the newest meta-analysis finds a non-significant testing-versus-restudy effect (g = 0.18, 7 studies), so "testing effect for math" is less secure than the general literature suggests. Short-answer practice helps mainly items that are already retrievable. Practice-test scores predict real exam scores reasonably well in one large self-report survey (R-squared about 0.62-0.68 for the best last-few-weeks tests) but with big individual errors. For anxiety, the best classroom-style evidence is cheap, brief interventions (expressive writing before the exam; reappraisal messaging), not exam-simulation per se; the claim that retrieval practice "inoculates" against stress rests on one lab study with a published critique.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG for general, contested for math] Testing effect anchors.** Donoghue & Hattie (2021): practice testing d = 0.74 (374 effects, 6,033 participants), effects similar at all retention intervals (under 1 day 0.58; 1 day-1 week 0.59; 1 week-1 month 0.56; 1-6 months 0.51), surface outcomes d = 0.60 (N = 1,473 as listed) vs deep d = 0.26 (N = 109), near transfer 0.61 vs far 0.39 (the fetched summary lists N = 197 near and 1,385 far yet also says far-transfer effects were too few to analyze by technique, so the near/far split should be treated cautiously); the authors say "it is not the frequency of testing that matters, but the skill in using practice testing." — [Donoghue & Hattie 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/pdf). Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017, 272 effect sizes): practice tests beat restudying and all other comparison conditions; a later paper attributes to it g = 0.67 for classroom settings, a general range of about 0.50-0.61 across Phelps, Rowland and Adesope, higher effects when practice and final formats match, and says Adesope found multiple-choice stronger than short-answer while Rowland found the opposite. Bangert-Drowns et al. (1991) d = 0.54 for classroom testing vs none; Schwieren et al. (2017) d = 0.56. (secondary) — [Adesope abstract](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306); [Greving & Richter 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Short-answer testing helps only retrievable items.** Greving & Richter (2018): university lecture, 92 analyzed participants; short-answer testing showed a one-tailed positive effect (beta = 0.44, p = .033) limited to high-retrievability items; multiple-choice testing showed no significant effect (beta = -0.42, p = .078); the authors note the MC items were easy and caution against generalizing. — [Greving & Richter 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/pdf)
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED, 2025] Math-only testing effect not established.** Murray et al. (2025): testing vs restudy in mathematics g = 0.18, CI crosses zero, 7 studies and 32 effects, versus Yang et al. (2021, earlier notes) classroom math/statistics g = 0.433 [0.193, 0.674] from 27 effects. Plausible reasons for the gap are comparison condition (restudy vs no quiz) and study count, not necessarily a contradiction. — [Murray et al. 2025](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/229807) [2023-26]
- **[WEAK, self-report] Practice tests predict exam scores, imperfectly.** Seal, Koek & Sharma (2020, *Cureus*): anonymous Reddit survey, 399 usable USMLE Step 1 respondents; single-practice-test R-squared for the best predictors 0.68 (UWorld self-assessment 2), 0.66 (NBME 16), 0.656 (UWorld question bank); average gap between best test and real score 6.9 points; students taking six or more practice tests scored higher, with 6-9 optimal; prediction-model errors on 19 held-out students ranged from -8.0 to +13.2 points. Volunteer self-report, unknown other study resources. — [Seal et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7198101)
- **[MODERATE, lab, critiqued] Retrieval practice and acute stress.** Smith, Floerke & Thomas (2016, *Science*): participants learned by restudying or retrieval practice; 24 hours later stress was induced in half; restudy learners showed the usual stress-related retrieval impairment while retrieval-practice learners did not. A commentary (Wolf & Kluge 2017) notes the study-practice group had worse overall performance (so the protection may reflect stronger traces, not the strategy), cortisol was not measured, and the finding needs validation with complex material. — [Smith et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aah5067); [Wolf & Kluge 2017](https://www.cog.psy.ruhr-uni-bochum.de/cog/mam/paper/2017/wolf_kluge_commentary-stress_retrievalpractice_frontbehavneurosci_2017_.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Brief expressive writing before a high-stakes exam.** Ramirez & Beilock (2011, *Science*): two laboratory and two randomized field experiments; writing about worries immediately before an important test significantly improved exam scores, "especially for students habitually anxious about test taking." Sample sizes and effect sizes are not in the abstract. — [Ramirez & Beilock 2011](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199427)
- **[MODERATE] Anxiety reappraisal message.** Brady, Hard & Gross (2018, *J Educ Psychol*): a brief message from course instructors the night before the first exam, encouraging students to view exam anxiety as beneficial or neutral, lowered exam worry and raised next-day exam and overall course performance for first-year students; it did not affect upper-year students. Sample size, randomization detail and effect sizes are not in the abstract. — [Brady, Hard & Gross 2018](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1314419)
- **[WEAK] Retrieval practice and test anxiety, abstract not read.** Agarwal, D'Antonio, Roediger, McDermott & McDaniel (2014) is titled "Classroom-based programs of retrieval practice reduce middle school and high school students' test anxiety"; numbers were not retrieved. — [WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/classroom-based-programs-of-retrieval-practice-reduce-middle-scho/)
- **[MODERATE] Format matching and retrieval-versus-example timing** (Yang 2021; Cao & Carvalho 2026) are in the earlier notes.

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: Practice tests should be exam-format simulations scheduled before each real exam, not generic quizzes.** Use the course's real format (free-response, show-your-work, calculator rules, time limit). Quizzing in the matching format gave larger effects (earlier notes: 0.53 matched vs 0.40 mismatched), and Adesope also reports a format-match benefit. Use short-answer/production formats, not multiple choice.
- **DESIGN: Schedule roughly six to nine full practice tests across a course's exam cycle rather than many unproctored ones** (borrowing the only available count; self-reported, medical-exam context, so treat as a starting default). Run at least one untimed or lightly timed diagnostic before the first timed simulation.
- **DESIGN: Practice-test score is a prediction with uncertainty, not a promise.** Use the user's own history (practice-test score vs real exam score) to calibrate a per-course offset, show an interval (individual errors of several points are normal), and combine with FSRS predicted retrievability on exam day.
- **DESIGN: Practice test errors flow into the mistakes bank** with the first wrong step and an error type; each missed skill gets a fresh-sibling cold re-attempt in the next 1-2 days and returns to the FSRS schedule.
- **DESIGN: Offer, as opt-in, a pre-exam "worries" writing prompt (Ramirez & Beilock) and a short reappraisal message (Brady).** Both are cheap, text-only, and were tested in real courses. Present as small-to-moderate and uncertain benefit, not as validated.
- **MISTAKE: marketing mock exams as stress inoculation.** The only direct evidence is a single lab memory study with a published critique; no college-math study of exam-simulation practice reducing anxiety or improving performance was found.
- **MISTAKE: using practice-test accuracy for ordinary review scheduling.** FSRS rates single-skill recall; a mock exam mixes skills, speed and endurance. Use the mock for diagnosis (which skills/time sinks), not as the memory signal.
- **MISTAKE: assuming the math testing effect is as large as the general 0.5 figure.** The math-only meta-analysis is small and inconclusive.

### Gaps
- Overall Adesope g and its moderator table were not retrieved; the 0.67 classroom figure and 0.61 range come from a citing paper.
- Agarwal et al. (2014), Jamieson et al. (2010, GRE reappraisal) and Huntley/Young et al. test-anxiety intervention reviews: titles found, effect sizes not retrieved.
- No study of full-length mock exams in college math courses (versus equal-time practice) was found.
- No peer-reviewed evidence that practice-test scores predict real exam scores in college math was found; the one dataset is a self-selected medical-exam survey.

## Q5. Errors and feedback: hypercorrection, errorful generation, feedback timing and content, erroneous examples, and metacognitive calibration (is marking "unsure" useful?)

### Takeaway
Elaborated feedback is what separates strong from weak feedback (d = 0.49 elaborated vs 0.05 right/wrong-only in a computer-based meta-analysis, with larger effects in mathematics). High-confidence errors get corrected well, including in authentic school math and a college classroom, though confidence adds modestly beyond prior knowledge. Feedback after a correct but low-confidence answer doubles retention of that answer, so "unsure" is a legitimate signal to trigger feedback. Two boundary conditions matter: even minimal feedback hurt children who already had a correct strategy (Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson), and rating prompts can change what practice does (reactivity). Erroneous worked examples help algebra learners modestly and do not increase errors; presenting the erroneous example first raised mental load in novices.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG] Content of feedback.** Van der Kleij, Feskens & Eggen (2015, *Rev Educ Res*): 40 studies, 70 effect sizes (range -0.78 to 2.29), computer-based item-level feedback. Elaborated feedback d = 0.49, knowledge of correct response 0.32, knowledge of result (right/wrong) 0.05; elaborated feedback especially better for higher-order outcomes; larger effects for mathematics than social sciences, science and languages; negatively affected by delayed feedback and by primary/high school samples; the interaction between feedback timing and lower- vs higher-order outcomes was not significant. — [Van der Kleij et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314564881). Earlier notes: Wisniewski et al. 2020 (435 studies, high-information feedback d = 0.99 vs corrective 0.46) and Mullet et al. 2014 (one-week-delayed feedback gave better transfer in engineering).
- **[MODERATE] Feedback can hurt when some knowledge is present.** Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson (2016, *J Educ Psychol*): 2nd-3rd graders, math equivalence problems. Exp 1 (N = 108): children with no strategy knowledge did better with right/wrong feedback (about 50% vs 18% procedural post-test; partial eta-squared .12); children taught a correct strategy did worse with feedback (about 62% vs 81%; partial eta-squared .05). Exp 2 (N = 95 analyzed): both immediate and summative correct-answer feedback lowered procedural and conceptual knowledge relative to no feedback in children who had been taught the strategy. Limited to one-on-one tutoring, one domain, immediate post-test, minimal feedback; elaborated feedback may behave differently. — [Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson](https://news.vanderbilt.edu/files/FyfeRittleJohnson2015_JEP.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Feedback on correct low-confidence answers.** Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2008): feedback "doubled the retention of correct low-confidence responses" relative to no feedback, so feedback corrects metacognitive as well as memory errors. — [WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/correcting-a-metacognitive-error-feedback-increases-retention-of-/)
- **[MODERATE] Hypercorrection in authentic math.** Foster, Woodhead, Barton & Clark-Wilson (2022, *Educ Stud Math*): observational Eedi data, 7,302 UK students aged 6-16 from 152 schools, 219,826 multiple-choice diagnostic responses with 5-point emoji confidence; confidence correlated with facility (r_s = .504), median confidence 82.8 on correct vs 68.8 on incorrect answers; for the follow-up (about three weeks later, 119,211 responses from 4,123 students) the abstract reports first evidence in authentic school mathematics that errors made with higher confidence are more likely to be corrected. The statistics for the hypercorrection result were not retrievable. — [Foster et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10649-021-10084-7)
- **[MODERATE] Hypercorrection in a college course.** Intro horticulture class (college students): significant hypercorrection of high-confidence errors on course content, and students with more prior knowledge expressed higher confidence and corrected errors more effectively. — [Memory 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2018.1477164)
- **[MODERATE] Erroneous worked examples in algebra.** Secondary analysis of Barbieri & Booth (2020): N = 206 Algebra I students in 11 classrooms randomized within class to problem-solving control, correct examples with explanation prompts, correct examples with "anticipate an error" prompts, or displayed incorrect examples with "why is this wrong" prompts. All three example conditions beat control on conceptual feature knowledge (standardized beta about .21-.22); only the displayed-error condition beat control on equation solving (beta = .200). Targeted conceptual errors on practice problems: control 12.29% vs 7.15-8.93% in example conditions; studying errors did not increase errors. — [Barbieri & Silla (UDel)](https://udspace.udel.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/775c29a4-17fa-418d-b7af-a1595b0f8b33/content)
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED] Order of correct vs erroneous examples for novices.** Wesenberg et al. (2022): N = 83 university students learning Vedic mathematics; correct-then-erroneous vs erroneous-first, same vs different problems; no effect on learning performance (interaction p = .117, underpowered); erroneous-first raised mental load (34.00 vs 31.07, p = .037); erroneous-first students judged their delayed performance more accurately (underestimation -12.44 vs -23.26). Errors were not signaled. — [Wesenberg et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1032003/pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Pretesting (errorful attempt before study), prose.** Kliegl & Baeuml (2025, *Psychological Research*): two experiments, 72 university students each, 24-hour delay; pretest condition recalled 34.9% vs 21.3% (related passages; partial eta-squared .18) and 63.4% vs 49.4% (unrelated passages; .15), even though pretest success was only 1-8%. Reading passages, not math; study time was shorter in the pretest condition. — [Kliegl & Baeuml 2025](https://baeuml.app.uni-regensburg.de/paper/papers_open/KB24_PR.pdf) [2023-26]
- **[WEAK] Metacognitive calibration and reactivity.** In the 2023 vocabulary series (Q1), requiring memory ratings reversed the retrieval-vs-restudy advantage (N = 63). Earlier notes: Nederhand et al. 2018 (detailed standards reduce overconfidence), Hacker 2000 (low performers stay overconfident), Sitzman et al. 2014 (confidence adds little beyond prior knowledge; delayed vs immediate feedback correction 51% vs 42%). — [Springer 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09809-2)
- **[2025 pointer] Learning from errors special issue.** A British Journal of Educational Psychology commentary synthesizes 11 papers on contextual, individual, process and instructional-strategy factors (feedback and prompts) in learning from errors; abstract only, no effect sizes. — [BJEP 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12716) [2023-26]

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: Always give elaborated feedback after an attempt, never right/wrong only.** Full worked solution with the reason for each step (and the specific misconception for the user's wrong step when known). Right/wrong-only feedback (d = 0.05) is the thing to avoid.
- **DESIGN: Do not skip feedback when the answer is right but the user is unsure.** Offer a compact "why this works" view for low-confidence corrects (Butler: doubles retention); for confident, fast corrects, show a one-line confirmation and let the user open the full solution.
- **DESIGN: Mistakes bank prioritization.** Schedule fresh-sibling re-attempts for errors first, ordered by (high-confidence errors, then method-choice errors, then concept errors, then slips). High-confidence errors are the best-corrected category in school and college data, which supports making them the most visible items in the bank rather than the most punished. Do not treat confidence as a strong predictor in the memory model (adds little beyond prior knowledge).
- **DESIGN: Capture confidence cheaply and test it.** Make it a one-tap 3-level choice, shown only on a random subset or only on mixed-queue reviews, and compare delayed accuracy of rated vs unrated reviews inside the app, since rating prompts changed outcomes in one study.
- **DESIGN: A two-step reveal for stable skills.** For skills with high stability, show the final answer or a step checkpoint first and let the user locate their own mistake before the full solution is revealed. This keeps the generation/diagnosis step that Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson suggest feedback can crowd out for people who already know the strategy. It is an inference from a child study and should be A/B tested, not assumed.
- **DESIGN: Use erroneous examples as an occasional item type for skills with known misconceptions.** Present the flawed solution with the error unsignaled or signaled, ask "find and fix the mistake," then link it to the skill. Algebra evidence is favorable (equation-solving beta .20) and errors did not increase; do not present the flawed example before the correct method for brand-new skills (higher load in novices, no learning gain).
- **MISTAKE: immediately repeating the same failed problem.** Evidence on delayed vs immediate re-testing (earlier notes) supports a delayed fresh sibling instead.
- **MISTAKE: auto-inferring "confidence" from time or hints and treating it as ground truth.** No evidence for that proxy.
- **MISTAKE: treating the 2022-2025 hypercorrection studies as proof that wrong-answer streaks are productive.** The effect is for confident errors followed by feedback.

### Gaps
- Foster et al.'s hypercorrection statistics (odds ratios, percentages) were not retrievable.
- No study of marking "unsure" on multi-step math problems (versus multiple-choice items) was found.
- No math-specific evidence on pretesting problems before instruction was found (the 2025 result is prose).
- The Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson finding concerns children and minimal feedback; adult elaborated feedback for novices vs near-experts has not been directly tested here.

## Q6. Worked-example fading, expertise reversal, hints, productive failure, and what step-level tutoring evidence implies

### Takeaway
Worked examples help math learners (g about 0.43 in the poster, reported as 0.48 elsewhere), and the effect weakens as learners become competent (expertise reversal). Step-level feedback is the feature with the clearest ITS evidence: step-based tutors perform near human-tutor levels (about 0.75 SD) and answer-based systems about 0.35 (VanLehn, secondary). Newer, more cautious data: a 2025 K-12 US meta-analysis finds a smaller pooled ITS effect (g = 0.271), with worked examples and on-demand hints in the favorable range but differences small and mostly non-significant. Productive failure helps concepts, not procedures (earlier notes).

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG with discrepancy] Worked examples in math.** The earlier notes cite the Barbieri, Clerjuste & Chawla poster: g = 0.43 [0.31, 0.55] from 49 studies/185 effects with no significant moderators. A University of Delaware follow-up paper's discussion cites the published meta-analysis as "moderate positive effect (g = .48)" and reports that example conditions that included self-explanation prompts had smaller effects (beta = -0.24). The published version is Barbieri, Miller-Cotto, Clerjuste & Chawla (2023, *Educational Psychology Review*). — [UDel follow-up](https://udspace.udel.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/775c29a4-17fa-418d-b7af-a1595b0f8b33/content); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1) [2023-26]
- **[STRONG] Intelligent tutoring, older anchors.** Kulik & Fletcher (2016, *Rev Educ Res*): 50 controlled evaluations; median effect 0.66 SD (50th to 75th percentile), larger on locally developed than standardized tests; evaluations with nonconventional controls or flawed implementations showed small effects. — [Kulik & Fletcher 2016](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315581420). VanLehn (2011), as summarized: step-based and substep-based tutors and human tutoring cluster near 0.75 SD, answer-based systems average about 0.35 SD; human tutoring closer to 0.79 than Bloom's 2 SD. (secondary) — [Education Next](https://www.educationnext.org/how-machine-based-tutoring-could-disrupt-human-tutors/); [VanLehn 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369)
- **[MODERATE, 2025 preprint] Newer, smaller ITS estimate.** "Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?" (arXiv, Nov 2025): 18 experimental studies, 77 effect sizes, 11 ITS (one study contributes 50.65% of effects); pooled g = 0.271 (SE 0.011); math 0.253 vs reading/writing 0.323; duration 2 weeks-3 months 0.449, 3-6 months 0.225, over 6 months 0.110; usage as main instruction 0.294, homework 0.317, after school 0.014; on-demand hints 0.316, just-in-time hints 0.337 (ns), worked-out examples 0.324, motivation support 0.265; immediate measurement 0.460 vs end of year 0.155; standardized tests 0.200 vs researcher tests 0.483 (ns). Most moderator estimates rest on few studies; authors speculate on-demand help may beat auto-triggered hints but warn less self-regulated learners may game it. Not yet peer reviewed. — [arXiv 2511.04997](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.04997) [2023-26]
- **[MODERATE] Prior knowledge decides example-first or problem-first.** Reisslein et al. 2006 and Kalyuga expertise-reversal work (earlier notes). Cao & Carvalho 2026: without prior instruction worked examples beat retrieval practice with repeated items; after instruction retrieval wins (earlier notes).
- **[MODERATE] Productive failure.** Sinha & Kapur 2021 (earlier notes): g = .36 for conceptual knowledge and transfer, not significantly better for procedural learning.
- **[MODERATE, short-term] Correct vs erroneous example order for novices:** see Q5 (Wesenberg 2022).
- **[Not accessible]** A 2026 LAK paper on hint buttons ("Revisiting the Hint Button") and Aleven et al.'s help-seeking review were found by title but could not be fetched (robots restrictions), so their findings are not reported.

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: Step-level diagnosis is the right place to invest.** The grain-size result (answer-based about 0.35 vs step-based about 0.75, secondary, from tutoring systems that check each step) supports the feature set. But it holds when the system itself checks steps. For user-marked first-wrong-step diagnosis, build assisted checking where possible: let the user enter intermediate expressions and check equivalence with a CAS, and otherwise align the user's work to the stored solution's step list.
- **DESIGN: Hint ladder with cost accounting.** Level 1 = a goal/cue ("what are you trying to find?"), level 2 = the next step, level 3 = full solution. Record hints; a solve with hints beyond level 1 counts as "Hard" or "Again" and does not advance the mastery counter. On-demand hints (user-triggered) fit the better-supported side of the weak evidence, with a cap and a cooldown to deter gaming (the authors of the 2025 meta-analysis warn of gaming).
- **DESIGN: Fade support with stability, not with a fixed schedule.** Acquisition: example then example-problem pair, then completion problem, then independent. Reviews: cold attempt first; a worked example reappears only after a long-lapse failure, consistent with the prior-knowledge crossover.
- **DESIGN: Do not require self-explanation prompts on every example.** One meta-analytic moderator (as cited second-hand) found smaller effects with prompts; use prompts for conceptual or misconception-prone examples only.
- **DESIGN: Productive failure is optional and conceptual.** Reserve for a few concept-introduction skills.
- **MISTAKE: assuming the ITS 0.66 median transfers.** Newer, broader K-12 data give 0.27 and larger effects on researcher-made tests than standardized tests; our target outcome is a real course exam, closer to a standardized or instructor-made test.
- **MISTAKE: unlimited hints with full-credit outcomes.** Makes mastery counters unreliable.

### Gaps
- Barbieri et al. (2023) published moderator results (fading, incorrect examples, prompts) and the 0.43 vs 0.48 discrepancy could not be resolved from the paper itself.
- VanLehn's numbers are second-hand; N per category not retrieved.
- Alfieri et al. (2011, discovery learning) and Alfieri et al. (2013, case comparisons) were located but their numbers were not retrieved.

## Q7. Mastery learning: thresholds, criterion-referenced progress, and the "wheel-spinning" problem

### Takeaway
Mastery learning helps on criterion-referenced tests and barely at all on standardized tests held to equal time and coverage (Slavin 1987: median ES +0.04 on standardized measures; Kulik et al. 1990: 0.52 overall, 0.10 to 0.33 on standardized tests, per earlier notes). Within an adaptive practice system, raising the pass threshold buys small, shrinking retention for a real time cost, with larger gains on hard-to-retain topics. Students who cannot reach a simple "3 in a row" within about 10 attempts (wheel spinning) are 0.6-10% of student-skill pairs under that rule, but different detection rules disagree more than half the time.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG] Slavin's skeptical best-evidence synthesis.** Slavin (1987, *Rev Educ Res*): studies of at least 4 weeks only; 17 studies emphasized (7 equal-time with standardized measures, 6 equal-time experimenter-made only, 4 unequal-time). Median ES +.04 on standardized measures; +.255 on experimenter-made measures (immediate posttests, 8 studies); retention effects "essentially zero"; "mastery learning trades coverage for mastery"; the evidence supports a "curricular focus" claim but not the strong claim of superiority at equal time and coverage-sensitive measures. — [Slavin 1987](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/1987-slavin.pdf)
- **[STRONG] Kulik et al. (1990)** (earlier notes): 108 evaluations, ES = 0.52; higher mastery criteria gave larger effects (91-100% criterion: 0.80); standardized tests 0.33 (PSI) and 0.10 (Learning for Mastery); mastery classes about 4% more instructional time; self-paced PSI had lower completion.
- **[MODERATE] N-consecutive-correct rules.** Kelly et al. (2015), cited in Matayoshi et al.: 3 vs 5 consecutive correct produced large performance differences, but with only 56 students and no long-term retention test. Earlier notes: EDM 2015 (3 in a row predicted the next problems; 5 in a row tracked transfer better). Doroudi (2020), cited there: simple mastery heuristics are equivalent to a simplified Bayesian knowledge tracing model. Pelanek & Rihak (2017): "the choice of data sources for the mastery decision and the setting of thresholds matter more than the choice of learner modeling technique"; an exponential moving average is a suitable mastery criterion; simulated and real data, sample sizes not in the abstract. — [Matayoshi et al. 2025](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/900/254); [Pelanek & Rihak 2017](https://www.ics.muni.cz/en/research/publications/1392193)
- **[MODERATE, 2025] Threshold trade-offs** (Matayoshi et al.; details in Q1): high-vs-low threshold retention difference mostly under 0.02, up to about 0.04 for struggling students, shrinking over weeks; about 1.5 extra minutes per topic; gain correlates with low retention (r = -0.86 across topics). Authors suggest using the high threshold selectively or letting students who start correctly pass with two consecutive corrects. — [Matayoshi et al. 2025](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/900/254) [2023-26]
- **[MODERATE] Wheel spinning.** Zhang et al. (2019, EDM; CMU tutor data): wheel spinning defined as failing to master a knowledge component despite significant practice. Under "fail to get 3 correct in a row within the first 10 opportunities," prevalence was 6.6% (CL1), 0.56% (CL2) and 10.2% (Geometry) of student-skill pairs; under a Bayesian-knowledge-tracing-based rule (PS++) 24.2%, 2.17% and 13.2%. Agreement between the two definitions was under 50% (22.2%, 14.1%, 41.6%). Detection from early data is poor (under 20% precision/recall in the first three steps for the 3-in-a-row definition); a Random Forest on PS++ reached 90.8% precision and 81.4% recall at step 4; correct-response percentage alone was a competitive baseline. The paper tests no intervention. — [Zhang et al. 2019 (ERIC)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED594575.pdf)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** Math Academy-style "two correct in a row unlocks the next step" gating (earlier notes) and its critique.

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: Report mastery as criterion-referenced progress per skill, not as a percentage of course content covered.** Show state per skill (new, acquiring, consolidating, consolidated) with the evidence behind it (days with clean corrects, current stability), because "completion" metrics reward coverage and Slavin's finding is that coverage and mastery trade off.
- **DESIGN: Wheel-spinning rule.** If a skill has had about 8-10 attempts without meeting the acquisition criterion (or lapses repeatedly), stop drilling, show the worked example again, test the prerequisite skills in the graph, and move the skill to a "needs help" list. The 3-in-a-row-in-10 definition is the only sourced operational rule; the cutoff and what to do next are design choices.
- **DESIGN: Per-skill adaptive criterion** (see Q1) rather than one global threshold; start at 2-3 consecutive clean corrects, escalate for skills with high observed lapse rates, and recompute with the user's data.
- **DESIGN: Preserve breadth near exams.** If an exam is close and mastery gating is stalling progress, switch gating from "mastery before moving on" to "coverage first with spaced return" (Slavin's coverage trade-off), and say so in the interface.
- **MISTAKE: raising the threshold globally for safety.** It costs time in every topic for under 0.02-0.04 retention that shrinks, and the gain concentrates in low-retention topics.
- **MISTAKE: equating a 90%+ "mastery score" with exam readiness.** The effect on standardized or instructor-made exams is much smaller than on the system's own criterion tests.

### Gaps
- Kelly et al. (2015) was not read directly; the two ALEKS thresholds are a score system (5 vs 3), not "N correct in a row".
- Beck & Gong (2013) original wheel-spinning paper was not retrieved; Zhang et al. (2019) was used instead.
- No evidence was found on mastery thresholds for college-level multi-step problems specifically.

## Q8. Transfer and variation of practice: near vs far, how much variation, and avoiding memorized answers

### Takeaway
Retrieval benefits carry to rephrased and different-format items but shrink for truly untested material (earlier notes: 0.51 identical, 0.56 rephrased, 0.32 untested; matched exam format 0.53 vs mismatched 0.40). The better evidence on variation is nuanced: surface variation helped abstract concept learning only after the learner had verbal pretraining on the concept (17.4% vs 7.0% gain), not in the control or graphical-pretraining groups, so variation is not a free win during initial learning. Deep and far-transfer outcomes are scarce in the big meta-analyses (93% of Donoghue & Hattie's outcomes were surface-level). The risk that learners memorize answers of regenerated problem templates has no direct empirical test in the sources found.

### Cited Findings
- **[MODERATE, lab] Variation x preparation.** Braithwaite & Goldstone (N = 215 Indiana University undergraduates; sampling-with-replacement concept; verbal pretraining N = 73, graphical N = 71, none N = 71; varied vs similar surface stories among 6 training examples; 6-item posttest across five story types): overall pretest 58.5% to posttest 67.4%; varied vs similar examples gained 17.4% vs 7.0% after verbal pretraining (p = .042), 8.8% vs 10.9% after graphical pretraining, and 1.9% vs 7.4% with no pretraining (neither significant). Transfer to a cross-mapped story type dropped by 2%, attributed to learners whose first example was the cross-mapped type. The variation manipulated was surface story, not structure. Title and venue inferred from the lab's PDF listing ("Effects of variation and prior knowledge on abstract concept learning"). — [Braithwaite & Goldstone PDF](https://pc.cogs.indiana.edu/papers/variationpreparation.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Far transfer is rarely measured.** Donoghue & Hattie (2021): near transfer d = 0.61 vs far d = 0.39 (the fetched summary lists N = 197 and N = 1,385 respectively but also says far-transfer effects were too few to analyze by technique, so the labels may be mixed up); outcomes 93% surface-level; deep d = 0.26 vs surface 0.60. — [Donoghue & Hattie 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Short-answer retrieval helps retrievable items.** Greving & Richter 2018 (Q4): effect limited to high-retrievability items.
- **[MODERATE] Varied examples help under low load** (Paas & van Merrienboer 1994) and interleaving improved performance on combined novel physics problems (Samani & Pan) (earlier notes).
- **[MODERATE, correlational] Mastery of prerequisites predicts later performance** (Sadler & Sonnert 2018; Q3).
- **[Located, not read]** Alfieri et al. (2013) meta-analysis of case comparisons; Likourezos & Kalyuga work on the variability effect in worked examples.

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: Two kinds of variation, introduced in order.** (1) Surface variation within a fixed structure (context, numbers that change the work, representation) after the first clean corrects; (2) structural variation (new combinations, inverse direction, multi-skill problems) for consolidated skills. Do not maximize variation during acquisition: the lab evidence shows variation helping only when learners already had a verbal handle on the concept.
- **DESIGN: Track template and variation-axis IDs and enforce a minimum gap before the same template recurs.** Rejecting numeric-only variants as "different" avoids the template-memorization risk, which is an inference with no direct test; set a rule such as "no repeat of the same template within the same skill's next N reviews" and monitor within-user performance on recurring vs new templates.
- **DESIGN: Match the exam's format and wording style** for a share of items (the format-match advantage), and include integration problems as their own skills (earlier notes).
- **DESIGN: Use a prerequisite check at course entry** (placement test) and surface foundation skills before a new course (Sadler & Sonnert pattern, correlational).
- **MISTAKE: "new problem each time" meaning new numbers only.** If the surface and solution path are identical, the learner may memorize the procedure's pattern for that template or its answer shape; the benefit of fresh problems then becomes illusory.
- **MISTAKE: starting novices with maximally varied sets.** Evidence supports varied examples after a conceptual handle exists, not before.

### Gaps
- No study was found on memorization of regenerated templates or answer-shape cues.
- Braithwaite & Goldstone is a single-concept lab study with no standardized effect sizes; its bibliographic details were inferred.
- Alfieri 2013 and variable-practice meta-analyses were not retrieved.

## Q9. Motivation and adherence: what keeps people practicing for months, and what gamification actually does for learning outcomes

### Takeaway
Autonomy and personal value predict persistence better than external reward; brief planning interventions can raise practice volume on a self-directed exam-prep task (small, single-school RCT: about 140 vs 84 practice questions over a summer). Gamification effects on cognition are moderate, but motivation and behavior effects vanish in rigorous designs (earlier notes), and the newest broad meta-analysis is internally inconsistent and shows near-zero effects in higher education. Learners prefer less effective strategies and need explanations; one-on-one in-app evidence will matter more than the literature for tuning.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG] Types of motivation and persistence.** Howard et al. (2021, *Perspect Psychol Sci*): 344 samples, 223,209 participants, 26 outcomes; intrinsic motivation related to success and well-being; personal value (identified regulation) was "particularly highly related to persistence"; external regulation (reward/punishment) was unrelated to performance or persistence and associated with decreased well-being; introjected (ego-involved) motives related to persistence and performance goals but also to ill-being; amotivation related to poor outcomes. — [Howard et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691620966789)
- **[MODERATE, small RCT] Planning raises practice volume.** Duckworth, Grant, Loew, Oettingen & Gollwitzer (2011): 66 second-year high school students (35 MCII, 31 control; random, blind) from one selective urban school; a 30-minute written exercise in May (two positive outcomes, two obstacles, two if-then plans plus a plan for where and when to do the workbook) versus an unrelated essay; the outcome was practice questions completed in a PSAT workbook handed out in July and collected in October: about 140 vs about 84 ("more than 60% more"), beta = 0.27, p = 0.04, R-squared 0.35. Limitations: single school, goal commitment unmeasured, summer-course participation unequal (controlled statistically). — [Duckworth et al. 2011](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/duckworth_self-regulation_11.pdf)
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED, 2023] Broad gamification meta-analysis.** A 2023 Frontiers in Psychology meta-analysis: 41 studies, 49 samples, more than 5,071 participants; overall g = 0.822 [0.567, 1.078] with I-squared 94.1%; academic performance g = 1.015 (k = 30), engagement 0.383 (k = 16), motivation 2.206 (k = 3, CI spans zero); duration over one semester g = 3.304 (k = 5); online g = 0.340 vs hybrid 0.863; the paper's text and tables disagree on higher education vs secondary (one reading gives higher education about 0.014); other internal inconsistencies noted. Treat as low reliability. — [PMC10591086](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10591086) [2023-26]
- **[MODERATE] Better anchor for gamification.** Sailer & Homner 2020 (earlier notes): cognitive g = .49 (.42 in high-rigor designs), motivational g = .36 (.22, non-significant in rigorous designs), behavioral g = .25 (.27 and non-significant in rigorous designs).
- **[MODERATE] The doer effect at scale.** Van Campenhout et al. (2023, LAK23): 15.2 million data events from 18,546 students across seven online higher-education courses; students who practice while reading outperform students who only read; analyzed correlationally and causally in five ways. Effect sizes and the causal method were not in the abstract. — [Van Campenhout et al. 2023](https://research.vitalsource.com/publications/the-doer-effect-at-scale-investigating-correlation-and-causation-across-seven-courses) [2023-26]
- **[MODERATE] Where and how it is used matters.** In the 2025 K-12 ITS meta-analysis, use as the main instructional method (g = 0.294) or for homework (0.317) outperformed after-school use (0.014). — [arXiv 2511.04997](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.04997) [2023-26]
- **[WEAK, not math] Beliefs can be changed.** Onan et al. 2024 (Q2): refutation plus metacognitive prompts increased self-organized interleaving. Earlier notes: streaks (Silverman & Barasch 2023), self-regulated spacing completion 35% vs 71%, Settles & Meeder 2016 (better scheduler raised daily retention by 12% in a 3.3-million-user test), Kulik (self-paced mastery lowers completion).

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: Onboard with an if-then plan.** A single setup step: "When and where will you do your daily set? What usually gets in the way? If that happens, I will ..." (mental contrasting plus implementation intentions). Cost is minutes; the one RCT showed a large practice-volume effect on a self-directed test-prep task. Expect a smaller effect in real use.
- **DESIGN: Build around autonomy-supportive choices and personal value, not points.** Let the user choose course, exam date, daily minutes and goal framing; show how each day's work maps to the user's own goals (the exam, a later course). Keep scheduling out of the user's hands (Q1, Q7).
- **DESIGN: Keep gamification minimal and tied to learning behavior.** Points that track minutes of clean practice and delayed-review gains are acceptable; avoid rewards for completion counts or speed. The only rigorous-design motivation and behavior effects were non-significant, and the largest "overall" number comes from a flawed meta-analysis.
- **DESIGN: Use the in-app logs as the evaluation instrument.** Because the evidence base is thin on adherence for single-user STEM SRS apps, record days practiced, session length, backlog size and dropout points, and test changes (plan prompt, forgiving streaks, catch-up queue) with within-user comparisons.
- **MISTAKE: relying on external rewards or competition for a one-person offline app.** The evidence for persistence points the other way.
- **MISTAKE: showing the user mostly performance-during-practice stats.** They are misleading (Q5/earlier notes); show delayed accuracy and predicted exam readiness.

### Gaps
- No study was found on dropout or backlog effects in single-user spaced-repetition apps for STEM.
- The doer-effect "times more effective than reading" multiplier was not verified here; effect sizes for the 2023 paper were not retrieved.
- Duckworth et al. 2011 has a small, selective sample; no replication was retrieved.

## Q10. Anchor meta-analyses: what each number means for a college math practice tool and how they reconcile

### Takeaway
Hattie-style numbers (d 0.74 practice testing, 0.85 distributed practice) are largest because they pool lab and classroom studies, surface outcomes and short delays; math-specific and classroom-specific meta-analyses are smaller. For a tool aimed at college math exams, plan on a stack of small-to-moderate effects (spacing g about 0.28 in math, interleaving about 0.34 in math, worked examples about 0.43-0.48, elaborated feedback about 0.49, ITS about 0.27-0.66 depending on the sample) that will not simply add.

### Cited Findings
| Source | What it pools | Key numbers | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dunlosky et al. 2013 (*PSPI*) | Review of 10 techniques | Practice testing, distributed practice rated high; interleaving, self-explanation, elaborative interrogation moderate | Ratings, not effect sizes; earlier notes |
| Donoghue & Hattie 2021 | 242 studies, 1,619 effects, 169,179 participants | Overall 0.56; distributed 0.85 (150 effects, 152,952); practice testing 0.74 (374, 6,033); interleaved 0.47 (104, 972); surface 0.60 vs deep 0.26; near 0.61 vs far 0.39; 93% surface outcomes | Surface, short-delay heavy; I-squared 85% — [source](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/pdf) |
| Adesope et al. 2017 | 272 effect sizes, lab and classroom | Practice tests beat restudy and all comparison conditions; classroom g = 0.67 and general range about 0.50-0.61 as cited second-hand | Overall g not verified — [source](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306) |
| Yang et al. 2021 | 222 studies, 573 effects, 48,478 students (classroom) | g = 0.499; math/statistics 0.433 (27 effects); untested material 0.321 | Earlier notes |
| Murray et al. 2025 | Math-only | Spacing g = 0.28 (27 studies, 53 effects); isolated 0.43, course-embedded 0.24; testing vs restudy 0.18 (7 studies, 32 effects, CI includes 0) | Few studies — [source](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/229807) |
| Brunmair & Richter 2019 | 59 papers, 238 effects, 8,466 participants | Interleaving overall 0.42; math 0.34 [0.11, 0.57]; bias-corrected 0.29 | Earlier notes; plus a 2024 null classroom study |
| Kulik et al. 1990; Slavin 1987 | Mastery learning | 0.52 local tests, 0.10-0.33 standardized (Kulik); +0.04 standardized, +0.255 experimenter-made (Slavin) | See Q7 |
| Van der Kleij 2015 | 40 studies, 70 effects (computer feedback) | Elaborated 0.49, correct answer 0.32, right/wrong 0.05 | See Q5 |
| Barroso 2021 | 747 effect sizes | Math anxiety vs achievement r = -.28 | See Q3 |

### Inferences
- **For planning, use the math-specific and classroom-specific numbers**, and treat the Hattie numbers as an upper bound. Expected gains from any single feature are a few percentage points on a delayed exam; total gain depends on adherence and whether the app causes more correctly spaced practice than the student would do alone.
- **Do not claim additive effects** (no source tested the whole package against a conventional course).

### Gaps
- Overall Adesope g and Latimier g are not verified; Dunlosky 2013 has no numeric effect sizes here.
- No direct evidence that the whole package works at college level (carried over from earlier notes).

## Q11. Cross-cutting: what changes relative to the earlier notes, and the ranked list of design decisions

### Takeaway
Three earlier-note conclusions need softening or extension: (1) interleaving rests on a strong seventh-grade RCT but now has a large classroom null and a replication underway; (2) math-only retrieval-vs-restudy evidence is weaker than the general testing literature; (3) mastery thresholds are better evidenced than previously noted, with a 2025 randomized A/B test suggesting an adaptive, per-skill criterion. Open gaps from the earlier notes that this pass resolved: Matayoshi thresholds, wheel-spinning operationalization (via Zhang 2019), the Braithwaite & Goldstone result, a classroom Adesope figure (second-hand).

### Cited Findings
- All cited findings are in Q1 to Q10 above. Notably for conflicts: Yang 2021 math/statistics g = 0.433 vs Murray 2025 testing-vs-restudy g = 0.18 (CI includes 0); Rohrer et al. 2020 d = 0.83 vs Rowlandson & Simpson (2024, accepted) null for angle relations; Barbieri poster g = 0.43 vs g = 0.48 cited in a follow-up paper; Driskell 1992 (overlearning helps) vs Rohrer & Taylor 2006 (null in math); Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson 2016 (feedback hurt strategy-knowing children) vs the broad feedback meta-analyses (feedback helps, elaboration helps most).

### Inferences
Ranked by (strength of evidence) x (cost to build) x (risk if wrong):
1. **Criterion across days plus FSRS (Q1, Q7).** Three clean corrects to enter, at least three spaced successful reviews to consolidate, algorithm-controlled retirement, wheel-spinning escape after about 8-10 attempts. Highest confidence on direction; thresholds are tunable and should be estimated from the user's own logs.
2. **Elaborated post-attempt feedback with a hint ladder (Q5, Q6).** Strong evidence on content of feedback; hint design is lower-evidence but low-risk.
3. **Cold review, fresh sibling problems, format-matched, with delayed re-test of errors (Q4, Q5, Q8).** Moderate evidence; the key risk is template memorization, which needs monitoring.
4. **Mixed queue with confusable pairs near each other and no labels (Q2).** Moderate and now contested; cheap to build, so keep it, and log neighbor effects.
5. **Mock exams in exam format; practice-test scores treated as predictions with intervals; optional pre-exam writing and reappraisal prompts (Q4).** Moderate and low-cost; avoid stress-inoculation claims.
6. **Speed as a measured variable, small rapid-fire fact sets for retrieval-like content, no global timers (Q3).** Evidence is elementary-school and thin; keep dosage small.
7. **If-then planning at onboarding, forgiving streaks, minimal gamification (Q9).** Cheap, small-sample support; test in-app.
8. **Confidence capture and erroneous-example items (Q5).** Interesting but contested; make them sparse and A/B tested.

A short list of tests the app can run on itself, since the literature cannot settle them: neighbor-adjacency (confusable-pair) effect on delayed accuracy; adaptive vs fixed pass threshold; rated vs unrated confidence prompts; two-step reveal vs full reveal on stable skills; recurring templates vs new templates; plan-prompt vs no plan prompt on first-month adherence.

### Gaps
- Several 2024-2026 items could not be accessed (the LAK 2026 hint-button paper, Janes et al. 2020, the Rawson-Dunlosky-Janes boundary-condition paper, Aleven et al. 2016), and the first-hand numbers for Latimier 2021, Adesope 2017, Foster 2022's hypercorrection result and the published Barbieri 2023 moderators remain unretrieved.
- This pass used far more than the 15-call guideline because of the breadth of the questions; the remaining gaps above are where a further pass would add the most.
