# Learning Science for a Spaced-Repetition Problem-Solving App (college math, then physics, chemistry, C++)

Evidence-strength tags used below:
- **[STRONG]**: meta-analysis or several replications, including classroom or college samples
- **[MODERATE]**: a few good studies, a single large randomized controlled trial (RCT), or a meta-analysis with real caveats
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED]**: small, correlational, lab-only, or actively disputed
- **[PRACTITIONER]**: a claim by Math Academy, the Anki/FSRS community or similar sources, with no peer-reviewed test
- **(secondary)**: the finding comes from a blog or reading-note summary of the primary paper, not the paper itself

Research note: the shared web-search budget ran out near the end of this research. A few items I meant to verify are listed as gaps instead (Adesope 2017 effect sizes, Beck & Gong wheel-spinning, Kapler 2015, Braithwaite & Goldstone, the FSRS-vs-SM-2 benchmark numbers, and the Pomodoro/break study).

## Q1. Retrieval practice / testing effect for problem solving: does it transfer to new problems?

### Takeaway
Retrieval practice is among the best-supported learning techniques. Classroom meta-analyses find about g = 0.5 overall and g ≈ 0.43 for math/statistics. The benefit carries over to rephrased problems and different formats with almost no loss, but only about two-thirds of it reaches truly untested material. Two conditions make it work: the learner has to succeed fairly often on the first attempts, and the retrieval should come with feedback. Without both, the benefit for problem solving shrinks or disappears.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG] Overall classroom effect, by subject and by college level.** Yang et al. (2021, *Psychological Bulletin*) pooled 222 studies, 573 effects and 48,478 students and found g = 0.499 [0.442, 0.557] for classroom quizzing. Mathematics/statistics (k = 27) had g = 0.433 [0.193, 0.674], engineering (k = 11) g = 0.584, chemistry (k = 10) g = 0.340, and university/college samples (k = 335) g = 0.486. The subject moderator was not significant (p = .692). — [Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- **[STRONG] Transfer to rephrased and untested items (Yang 2021).** Identical items gave g = 0.512, rephrased questions 0.558 and partial overlap 0.496. Untested material from the same lessons gave g = 0.321 [0.210, 0.432]. Quiz format matching the exam format gave g = 0.531, mismatched formats 0.399. — [Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Problem-solving exams benefit less (Yang 2021).** By final-exam content: fact g = 0.524, concept g = 0.644, problem-solving g = 0.453 (k = 59). This moderator was not significant (p = .087), and the authors conclude that testing does benefit application. — [Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- **[STRONG] More retrieval events help (Yang 2021).** One test gave g = 0.444, two tests 0.601, three or more 0.642, and unlimited tests 0.762. Each added test raised g by β = 0.083. — [Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- **[STRONG] Rowland (2014) lab meta-analysis.** 61 studies and 159 effects gave g = 0.50 [0.42, 0.58]. Production tests beat recognition tests: cued recall g = 0.61 versus recognition g = 0.29 (full set). Retention intervals of at least 1 day gave larger effects (g = 0.69) than shorter ones (0.41). — [Rowland 2014](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf)
- **[STRONG] Initial success gates the benefit (Rowland 2014).** In studies without feedback, initial test performance at or below 50% produced g = 0.03, not reliably different from zero. Performance of 51–75% gave g = 0.29, and above 75% gave g = 0.56. — [Rowland 2014](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf)
- **[STRONG/MODERATE] Transfer of test-enhanced learning (Pan & Rickard 2018).** Grand mean transfer was d ≈ 0.40. The biggest moderator was "response congruency" (practice and final answers overlap): d = 0.58 with it versus 0.28 without. Elaborated retrieval practice (explain, answer higher-order questions, elaborative feedback) added about d = 0.23. Initial accuracy added about 0.0058 d per percentage point, and transfer vanished when initial performance was very poor. (secondary) — [Matuschak notes on Pan & Rickard 2018](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zC1oBp6yE72b7YHzaJZmjXf)
- **[MODERATE] Agarwal et al. (2021), classroom systematic review.** Of 50 classroom experiments, 28 of 49 Cohen's d values exceeded 0.50. Undergraduates appeared to gain the least, though the data were noisy. Effects were smaller when final questions were rephrased rather than verbatim. (secondary) — [Matuschak notes on Agarwal et al. 2021](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zV1xNteVY4otyHP5gCcanVN)
- **[MODERATE] Fact quizzes do not build higher-order skill (Agarwal 2019).** Fact quizzes did not improve higher-order test performance. Mixed fact plus higher-order quizzing did, consistent with transfer-appropriate processing. — [Learning Scientists on Agarwal 2019](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2019/6/27-1)
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED] The complexity debate.** Van Gog & Sweller (2015) argued that "the testing effect decreases or even disappears as the complexity of learning materials increases," citing worked-example studies. Karpicke & Aue (2015) rebutted this. They pointed out that element interactivity is not measurable, that no study manipulated it, and that the null studies used immediate or massed tests. Their reading of the worked-example data was a small positive effect. — [Karpicke & Aue 2015 rebuttal](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-015-9309-3)
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED] Leahy, Hanham & Sweller.** They found an inverted testing effect with complex problem material on immediate tests, and only a slightly positive, nonsignificant effect after a 1-week delay. Rawson argued the delayed effect would be significant with a one-sided test. (secondary) — [Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zR7pUgEqqBEPq2eZvMpRrHX)
- **[MODERATE] Retrieval versus worked examples depends on prior instruction (Cao & Carvalho 2026, *Educational Psychology Review*).** After rules were taught, retrieval practice beat worked examples. Without initial instruction, worked examples beat retrieval when the retrieval used repeated items. Varied retrieval items took the most time. The author suggests worked examples may be more efficient for relearning after long delays or for reviewing prerequisites. (secondary) — [Learning Scientists, June 2026](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2026/6/18-1)
- **[STRONG] Dunlosky et al. (2013, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*).** Practice testing and distributed practice were rated high utility. Interleaved practice, self-explanation and elaborative interrogation were rated moderate. Rereading and highlighting were rated low. — [Dunlosky et al. 2013 abstract](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/954654)
- Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017, *Review of Educational Research*): "practice tests are more beneficial for learning than restudying and all other comparison conditions." The effect sizes were not retrievable here; see Gaps. — [Adesope et al. 2017 abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654316689306)

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: The app's core mechanic is well supported.** Attempting a problem before seeing its solution is retrieval practice, and drawing a fresh problem per skill is validated by the rephrased-item result (0.558, not below identical items). The caveat is that transfer to "untested" material is weaker (0.32). The problem generator for each skill therefore needs to span the full range of problems the exam could ask for that skill, not only near-clones of one template.
- **DESIGN: Never put a skill into cold retrieval until it has been taught and passed at a high rate.** Below about 50% initial success, the benefit is near zero without feedback (Rowland), and transfer grows with initial accuracy (Pan & Rickard). New skills should enter the review rotation only after an acquisition phase (see Q4 and Q5).
- **DESIGN: Always show the full solution after an attempt.** Feedback roughly doubles the effect in the lab (see Q6). Elaborated retrieval also improves transfer, so the solution view should explain why each step works, not just list the steps.
- **DESIGN: Model conceptual and higher-order questions as skills of their own.** Examples: "why does this test apply," "which method and why," "interpret this result." Fact flashcards and procedural drills will not produce higher-order performance by themselves (Agarwal 2019).
- **DESIGN: Formula and definition flashcards should require production, not recognition.** Have the user write or type the formula. Recall formats beat recognition in Rowland's data.
- **Expectations:** The math/problem-solving effect is real but moderate (about 0.43–0.45). The complexity debate suggests pairing retrieval with worked examples during early acquisition of complex procedures, rather than relying on cold retrieval alone.

### Gaps
- Adesope et al. (2017) moderator figures (overall g, format, postsecondary, feedback) could not be retrieved. A commonly cited overall g ≈ 0.61 is unverified here.
- No meta-analysis isolated "far transfer to novel multi-step math problems" as its own outcome. Yang's "problem-solving" category is the closest.
- The Pan & Rickard (2018) numbers come from a reading note, not the paper itself.

## Q2. Spacing / distributed practice in math: what gaps, and how should intervals relate to the exam date?

### Takeaway
Spacing is a high-utility, well-replicated effect for facts. For math procedures the evidence is positive but more variable, including some clean nulls. Several college-math classroom studies (engineering precalculus to calculus) show spaced quizzing improving finals and next-semester retention, typically by about 5 percentage points (half a letter grade). Extra massed repetitions did not last. The optimal gap scales with how long you need to remember: roughly 10–20% of the retention interval for 1–2 month horizons, and a smaller fraction for a year. Gaps that are too short cost far more than gaps that are too long.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG for facts] Cepeda et al. (2008), the "temporal ridgeline."** 1,354 online participants learned 32 trivia facts under 26 combinations of gap and retention interval (RI). Optimal recall gaps were about 3, 8, 12 and 27 days for RIs of 7, 35, 70 and 350 days, which is 43%, 23%, 17% and 8% of the RI. Optimal spacing raised recall by 64% versus a zero gap (d = 1.1). Gaps longer than optimal cost performance, but "these costs are much smaller than the costs of using too short a gap." — [Cepeda et al. 2008](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Pashler, Rohrer & Cepeda (2006).** In a 161-subject study with a 6-month test, performance was best when the gap was 10–20% of the RI. For one-year retention, a one-month gap gave a three-fold or greater improvement over a day or a week. The authors note spacing benefits "appear weak for visuospatial skills." — [Pashler, Rohrer & Cepeda](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/PRC2006.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Rohrer & Taylor (2007), college permutation problems.** Students who spaced practice scored 74% at one week versus 49% for massed practice. Doubling massed practice (4 vs 2 problems) gave no benefit (49% vs 46%), "a null effect of overlearning." Practice-phase scores did not differ (83% vs 82%). — [Rohrer & Taylor 2007](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2007-rohrer.pdf)
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED] A null result for math procedures.** Ebersbach & Barzagar Nazari (2020) tested 235 university students on permutation tasks with gaps of 0, 1 or 11 days and RIs of 1 or 5 weeks. They found "No Robust Effect of Distributed Practice," only an effect of retention interval. Their review notes mixed earlier results: Rohrer & Taylor 2006 found no benefit at 1 week but a benefit at 4 weeks, and Smith & Rothkopf found no procedural benefit. — [Ebersbach & Barzagar Nazari 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00811/pdf)
- **[MODERATE] College classroom: Hopkins et al. (2016, *Educational Psychology Review*).** In engineering precalculus, spaced quiz questions (spread over about 4 weeks) beat massed ones on the cumulative final. Spaced operations scored about 3% higher within the experimental group and about 8% higher than the control group's massed operations. On the next semester's calculus final, the experimental group scored about 10% higher, and 68% versus 48% earned a C or better (not significant with n = 54). (secondary) — [Lyle, Learning Scientists 2016](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2016/10/11-1); [Learning Scientists 2022 summary](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2022/8/18-1)
- **[MODERATE] Spacing beats extra quizzing (Hopkins et al. 2018 ASEE, Lyle et al. 2020).** A 2 (3 vs 6 questions) × 2 (massed vs spaced) within-subjects design spaced questions at the week taught, +2 weeks and +4 weeks (n = 51). On a readiness test one month after the final, spaced objectives scored .59 versus .54 for massed (F(1,50) = 7.87, p = .007, η² = .136), "equivalent to half a letter grade." The amount of practice had no effect (F < 1). — [Hopkins et al. 2018](https://peer.asee.org/29900.pdf). Lyle et al. (2020) likewise found spacing's benefit persisted into the next semester while the benefit of extra quizzing vanished, and that spacing lowered initial quiz scores. — [Learning Scientists on Lyle et al. 2020](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2022/9/7-1)
- **[MODERATE] Spacing advantage shrinks for complex tasks.** Donovan & Radosevich (1999) meta-analyzed 63 studies (112 effects) and found d = 0.46 overall. Task complexity correlated negatively with effect size (r = −.25). The high-complexity clusters showed d = 0.07–0.11, versus 0.97 for simple tasks. The authors: the strong effect "appears to be limited to relatively simple tasks." Most of these tasks were motor or skill tasks, not math. — [Donovan & Radosevich 1999](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/1999-donovan.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Personalized, model-driven review beats generic spacing (Lindsey et al. 2014).** 179 eighth-grade Spanish students used about 30 minutes per week of review. On a cumulative exam a month after the semester, personalized review beat massed review by 16.5% and generic spaced review by 10%. — [ScienceDaily on Lindsey et al. 2014](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/01/140121093046.htm)
- **[MODERATE] Spacing and working memory (Chen et al. 2018; Chen, Paas & Sweller 2021).** Spaced math practice in grades 4–5 produced higher post-test and working-memory scores than massed practice. The authors argue massed practice depletes working memory. — [Learning Scientists on Chen et al. 2018](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2023/11/16). In a 2021 systematic review, the spacing effect was "robust for varied lengths of resting, from 30 s to a few days." — [Chen, Paas & Sweller 2021](https://nlumaster.nl.edu/media/nledu/content-assets/documents/ctle/on-demand-learning-resources/Chen2021_Article_SpacingAndInterleavingEffectsR.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Very-long-term retention of algebra (Bahrick & Hall 1991).** Among more than 1,000 adults, people who took "some courses beyond calculus" showed "*no* loss of their algebra knowledge, even 50 years after their last math course." This is attributed to years of continued use and review in later coursework. (secondary) — [Willingham, AFT](https://aft.org/node/10659)
- **[WEAK] Self-regulated spacing fails on adherence.** In a high-school math study with home practice, only 35% of the distributed-practice group completed versus 71% of the massed group (p = .007). — [Barzagar Nazari & Ebersbach 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02170/pdf)

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: Let FSRS do long-horizon scheduling, and add an exam-date constraint for each course.** Use FSRS's retrievability prediction. For every skill tagged to an upcoming exam, if the next scheduled review falls after the exam and predicted R(exam date) is below target, pull a review earlier. Following Cepeda's asymmetry, place that review about 5–10 days before a 1-month-away exam rather than the day before. After the exam, return the skill to its normal long-term schedule (cumulative finals and later courses).
- **DESIGN: Rule of thumb for an "exam mode" view.** Last-review gap ≈ 10–20% of the time remaining to the exam for 1–2 month horizons, and a few percent for year-long horizons (Calc 1 to Calc 2, Calc to physics). Err long, not short.
- **DESIGN: Do not reward extra same-day repetitions of a skill once it meets criterion.** Extra massed practice did not last (Rohrer & Taylor overlearning null; Hopkins 2018 and Lyle 2020 amount null). Spread repetitions across days instead.
- **DESIGN: The app has to hold the schedule, not the user.** Self-regulated spacing has poor completion.
- **Expectations:** In college math, the realistic per-skill gain from spacing is about 5 percentage points on delayed tests. The larger long-term payoff is the Bahrick pattern: years of continued use.
- The rest periods between items and between sessions may help through working-memory recovery (Chen). This supports splitting heavy days into two sittings, though that is not directly tested.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests Cepeda-style optimal-gap ratios specifically for multi-step math procedures. The ridgeline data are trivia facts.
- No study directly compares FSRS-style adaptive scheduling against fixed schedules for math problem solving. Lindsey et al. (2014) is language vocabulary.
- Kapler et al. (2015) on spacing for higher-level university learning was not retrieved because of the search budget.

## Q3. Interleaved practice in math: effect sizes, when it helps versus hurts, and how much blocking for new material

### Takeaway
Interleaving math problem types is one of the strongest classroom-validated design choices for this app. A preregistered cluster RCT with 787 students found d = 0.83 at about 1 month. A college physics replication found median improvements of 50–125%. Across domains the meta-analytic effect is smaller (math g = 0.34; overall 0.42, about 0.29 after a publication-bias correction). Interleaving helps most when problem types look alike but need different strategies, and it does not help for word lists or expository text. Researchers recommend a short block of the new skill immediately after instruction, then mostly interleaved practice ("at least two-thirds" of problems). No study has established an optimal ratio.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG] Brunmair & Richter (2019, *Psychological Bulletin*).** 59 papers, 238 effects and N = 8,466 gave g = 0.42 [0.34, 0.50]. By material: mathematical tasks g = 0.34 [0.11, 0.57]; paintings 0.67; words −0.39 (blocking better); expository texts no significant effect. Higher between-category similarity strengthened the effect, and higher within-category similarity weakened it. Interleaved items in immediate succession gave g = 0.73 versus 0.22 when spaced. Trim-and-fill suggested publication bias (corrected g = 0.29). Retention interval and retention-versus-transfer tests were not significant moderators. — [Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)
- **[STRONG] Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung (2020), preregistered cluster RCT.** 54 seventh-grade classes in 5 schools, 787 students analyzed. The mostly interleaved group scored 60.7% versus 37.6% for the mostly blocked group, d = 0.83 [0.68, 0.97], on a test about 33 days after a common interleaved review. Every teacher showed a positive effect (d = 0.23–1.48). Students saw solutions and corrected their errors. — [Rohrer et al. 2020](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2019-rohrer.pdf). Time on task was not measured, and teachers reported that interleaved assignments took longer, so the effect per minute is likely smaller. (secondary) — [Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zHvJf88XUgLPMhdTigkoiUR)
- **[MODERATE] Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic (2015, *Journal of Educational Psychology*).** 126 seventh graders, within-subjects. At 1 day: interleaved 80% versus blocked 64% (d = 0.42). At 30 days: 74% versus 42% (d = 0.79). The interleaved condition still placed **the first four problems of each new skill as a block right after the tutorial**. — [Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2014-rohrer-2.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] It helps even for dissimilar problem types (Rohrer, Dedrick & Burgess 2014).** With 140 students, interleaved scored .72 versus blocked .38 (d = 1.05) two weeks after the last assignment. Discrimination errors were rare (4.4% of errors). The authors suggest interleaving strengthens the link between problem features and strategy, and that spacing contributed substantially. — [Rohrer, Dedrick & Burgess 2014](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2014-rohrer.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] College physics (Samani & Pan 2021, *npj Science of Learning*).** In two sections of introductory physics over 8 weeks of homework, interleaving produced median improvements of 50% and 125% on two surprise tests with novel, harder problems. Students rated interleaved practice harder and wrongly believed they learned less. — [Samani & Pan 2021 abstract](https://doaj.org/article/da1dfb08ef7d4cd7a757a8dad0716c75). The test problems combined topics never combined in homework, such as Faraday's law with torque on a current loop. (secondary) — [Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zTBR5JQDn9BFfUaPn4G58wn)
- **[MODERATE] Early learning on mixed practice looks worse.** Rohrer & Taylor (2007) volumes-of-solids experiment: practice scores were blocked 89% versus mixed 60%, but test scores one week later were mixed 63% versus blocked 20% (d = 1.34, n = 18). — [Rohrer & Taylor 2007](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2007-rohrer.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Practitioner-researcher guidance (Rohrer & Hartwig).** "There is no magic proportion," but the best-performing students in the cited studies had "at least two-thirds of the problems" spaced or interleaved. "At least some blocked practice might be beneficial when students first encounter a new skill," and weaker students "might need more blocked practice." Solutions should be shown "soon after they attempt the problem." Blocked practice "can create an illusion of mastery." — [Rohrer & Hartwig chapter](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-spaced-and-interleaved-mathematics-practice-rohrer-hartwig.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Mechanism: discrimination (Chen, Paas & Sweller 2021).** Their systematic review argues interleaving works by discriminative contrast and helps most when categories are "more difficult to distinguish apart." Null results came from clearly different materials. — [Chen, Paas & Sweller 2021](https://nlumaster.nl.edu/media/nledu/content-assets/documents/ctle/on-demand-learning-resources/Chen2021_Article_SpacingAndInterleavingEffectsR.pdf). Carvalho & Goldstone (2014) found the interleaving advantage "considerably reduced for low similarity categories," and blocked study gave better performance *during* learning. — [Carvalho & Goldstone 2014](https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00936/full)
- **[MODERATE] Robust across learners (Yan & Sana 2021).** Interleaving beat blocking for low- and high-working-memory learners alike, and "blocking was never better than interleaving." There was no difference when tasks were too hard or too easy. About 80% of students still believed blocking was better. (secondary; category-learning materials) — [Learning Scientists on Yan & Sana 2021](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2023/11/2-1)
- **[STRONG] Learners misjudge interleaving.** Across Yan, Bjork & Bjork (2016) experiments, 72% judged blocking more effective. Only combining experience with an explanation moved a majority (75%, rising to 93% when interleaving was experienced first). — [Yan, Bjork & Bjork 2016](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/11/YanBjorkBjork2016.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Textbooks rarely interleave.** Across 13,505 problems in six math texts, "only 9.7% of the problems were interleaved." — [Rohrer, Dedrick & Hartwig 2020](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-020-09516-2)
- **[PRACTITIONER] Skycak / Math Academy.** "Some initial amount of blocking is useful when first learning a skill," with lessons using "minimal doses of blocked practice," but no ratio is given. His "doubled test scores" attribution to Taylor & Rohrer (2010) conflicts with the Scientific American summary he quotes (25% better at 1 day, 76% at 1 month). — [Skycak, "Interleaving"](https://justinmath.com/cognitive-science-of-learning-interleaving/)

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: The headerless mixed queue is well supported. Keep the skill name hidden until the problem is solved.** Interleaving works partly because the learner has to identify the problem type from the problem itself. Matuschak's critique of Math Academy, that reviews grouped and announced by topic may "significantly harm transfer," is a reason not to show labels before the attempt ([Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Math_Academy)).
- **DESIGN: New skills get a short block, then go to interleaving.** A defensible default is a worked example, then about 2–4 problems of the new skill in a row (Rohrer 2015 used 4) until the in-session criterion in Q4 is met. After that, the skill appears only interleaved, on later days. Across a typical day, at least two-thirds of problems should be interleaved reviews. Weaker or brand-new topics may need a slightly longer initial block.
- **DESIGN: Order the queue for discriminative contrast, not pure random shuffle.** Similar-looking skills with different strategies should land near each other: u-substitution versus integration by parts, law of sines versus law of cosines, permutation versus combination, product rule versus chain rule. Adjacent items gave the largest effect (g = 0.73 in immediate succession). Explicit "which method applies?" items would add more of this (inference).
- **DESIGN: Flashcards (formulas, definitions) behave like the "words" material in the meta-analysis**, where interleaving did not help. They can sit in their own short segment of the queue without loss.
- **DESIGN: Tell the user in the interface that mixed practice will feel harder and score lower during practice,** and show delayed-retention data as evidence. The illusion is strong and hard to dislodge.
- Interleaving costs more time per problem (teacher reports in Rohrer 2020; Cao & Carvalho on varied items). Workload estimates should use measured time per item, not item counts.

### Gaps
- No study has established an optimal blocked-to-interleaved ratio for brand-new material. "≥2/3 interleaved" and "first ~4 problems blocked" are the best available operationalizations. A 2024 conference paper ("To block, interleave or interleave increasingly?", Scheitz, JURE 2024) tests gradually increasing interleaving, but its results could not be retrieved.
- Samani & Pan's sample size and effect sizes were not retrievable (only median improvements).
- All of Rohrer's classroom math RCTs are seventh grade. College-math interleaving evidence comes mainly from Rohrer & Taylor's small lab studies and the Samani & Pan physics study.

## Q4. Mastery learning, "minimum effective dose," and how many correct answers indicate mastery

### Takeaway
Mastery learning has a moderate, consistent benefit (ES ≈ 0.52 on local exams, much smaller on standardized tests). Higher mastery standards produce larger effects, and a 2025 randomized study found a higher threshold reduces later forgetting, though the gap narrows over time. For the in-session criterion, simple "N correct in a row" rules hold up: 3 in a row predicts the next problems, and 5 predicts transfer better. More practice within one session yields little. Successive-relearning research recommends a modest initial criterion (about 3 correct) followed by about 3 spaced relearning sessions with 1 correct each, which beats piling up correct answers in one session.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG] Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990).** 108 controlled evaluations gave ES = 0.52 (50th to 70th percentile). Higher mastery criteria gave larger effects (regression p = .016). For Bloom's Learning for Mastery at 91–100% unit mastery, ES = 0.80. On standardized tests, effects were 0.33 (Keller's self-paced Personalized System of Instruction, PSI) and 0.10 (Learning for Mastery). Self-paced PSI reduced course completion: 23 of 32 studies favored conventional classes (h = −0.14). Mastery classes used about 4% more instructional time. Follow-up exam effects persisted (0.71 across 11 studies). — [Kulik et al. 1990](https://www.academia.edu/81783373/Effectiveness_of_Mastery_Learning_Programs_A_Meta_Analysis)
- **[MODERATE] Bloom's "2-sigma" has not replicated at that size.** The Math Academy Way itself notes that other researchers have not replicated the effect. (secondary) — [Hecker on *The Math Academy Way*](https://frankhecker.com/2025/02/10/math-academy-part-3/). Kulik's meta-analytic 0.52 is the more realistic figure. — [Kulik et al. 1990](https://www.academia.edu/81783373/Effectiveness_of_Mastery_Learning_Programs_A_Meta_Analysis)
- **[WEAK/MODERATE] "N consecutive correct" thresholds (EDM 2015, ASSISTments-type data).** 3 in a row was adequate for predicting the next problem: 80% of students with 3 correct got the 4th and 5th right. 5 in a row detected mastery for a transfer question more accurately (about 73% versus 57%, computed from the paper's tables). Both N-in-a-row rules were more accurate than Bayesian knowledge tracing in this study. "Over 90%" of students who reached 3 in a row went on to 5. — [EDM 2015 poster](https://www.educationaldatamining.org/EDM2015/proceedings/poster630-631.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Matayoshi et al. (2025, *Journal of Educational Data Mining*), randomized A/B test of two mastery thresholds (ALEKS).** "Students who learn with the higher mastery threshold are less likely to forget the learned knowledge," but "over time this difference decreases." The effect varied with how much students struggled and with subject. — [Matayoshi et al. 2025](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/view/900)
- **[MODERATE] Overlearning in one session is wasteful.** Doubling massed problems had no effect at one week (49% vs 46%). — [Rohrer & Taylor 2007](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2007-rohrer.pdf). Six versus three quiz questions per objective had no long-term effect, while spacing did. — [Hopkins et al. 2018](https://peer.asee.org/29900.pdf). Arthur et al.'s (1998) skill-decay meta-analysis found overlearning barely related to decay (r = .08, few studies). — [Arthur et al. 1998](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/1998-arthur.pdf)
- **[STRONG for declarative] Successive relearning (Rawson & Dunlosky).** The procedure: retrieve correctly 3 times in initial learning, then 1 correct per later relearning session, with at least 3 relearning sessions, which "may be sufficient for maximal benefits" (Rawson & Dunlosky 2011). One correct recall in each of 3 spaced sessions produced "more than twice as much" recall as 3 correct recalls in one session (Rawson et al. 2018). Rawson et al. (2013) found scores about 40% higher nearly a month later than for self-studied material. — [APS, successive relearning](https://psychologicalscience.org/?p=193451). In an Introductory Psychology course, the gain was about a letter grade (C to B). **When students controlled when to drop items, retrieval showed no exam benefit.** — [RetrievalPractice.org](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/archive/2018/successive-relearning). An NSF-funded test of this in introductory chemistry, biology and physics is in progress. — [Kent State](https://www.kent.edu/node/893711)
- **[PRACTITIONER] Math Academy mechanics.** Each knowledge point "starts with a fully worked out example." "Two correct practice answers in a row" unlocks the next example, with "up to 5 practice problems" per round. Quizzes come every 150 XP, and missed quiz topics become review assignments. — [Math Academy, How it works](https://mathacademy.com/how-it-works). Matuschak observed that he could miss several questions and still continue, and he questions whether this is true mastery gating. — [Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Math_Academy)
- **[MODERATE] Skills forget at different rates.** On three math-tutor datasets, a model with per-skill forgetting parameters (DAS3H) beat shared-parameter models by +0.03 to +0.04 AUC. Estimated decay per time-window step ranged from 1.15% to 2.74% correctness depending on the skill. — [Choffin et al. 2019, DAS3H](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.06873)

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: A two-stage mastery rule.**
  1. Acquisition (same session): after the worked example, require about 2–3 correct in a row on fresh variants without hints (Math Academy uses 2; the EDM data support 3 for next-problem prediction; Rawson uses 3). If the learner keeps missing, stop drilling. Re-show worked examples and check prerequisites.
  2. Consolidation (across days): the skill counts as "mastered" only after it survives about 3 spaced reviews (successive relearning) and reaches FSRS stability above a set level. For example, stability greater than the time to the next exam, or above about 21–30 days. That cutoff is a design choice, not something the research establishes.
- **DESIGN: Use the probabilistic model (FSRS stability and retrievability) for long-term state, and "N in a row" only to gate first exposure.** Use more varied problems in the "N in a row" sequence to approximate the transfer-oriented 5-in-a-row criterion without massing.
- **DESIGN: The algorithm decides mastery and when a skill is retired, not the user.** Learner-controlled dropout erased the successive-relearning benefit.
- **DESIGN: "Minimum effective dose" is better spent on spacing than on extra same-day reps.** That supports a small daily dose of each due skill (typically one fresh problem per due skill) rather than sets.
- **DESIGN: Give every skill its own forgetting parameters** (FSRS's per-card difficulty and stability provide this), and expect some skills (exponent and log rules, trig identities) to decay faster.

### Gaps
- Beck & Gong (2013) "wheel-spinning" (students who fail to master a skill within about 10 opportunities) was not verified because of the search budget. A "give up and remediate after about 8–10 failed attempts" rule is therefore unsourced here.
- The two thresholds compared by Matayoshi et al. (2025) and their effect sizes are not in the abstract.
- No study has tested successive relearning criteria for multi-step math procedures specifically. Rawson's work is mostly definitions and concepts.

## Q5. Worked examples, faded examples, expertise reversal, and productive failure: when to show a worked solution and when to attempt cold

### Takeaway
For novices, studying worked examples beats unguided problem solving: a math meta-analysis finds g ≈ 0.43. The benefit reverses as expertise grows (the expertise reversal effect). The right progression is worked example, then faded or completion problems, then independent problems. Once a skill is learned, reviews should be attempted cold, with the worked solution shown afterward. Problem-solving-first ("productive failure") helps conceptual understanding and transfer (g ≈ 0.36) but not procedural skill, and it depends heavily on careful problem design.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG] Worked-examples meta-analysis in mathematics (Barbieri, Clerjuste & Chawla).** 40 articles, 49 studies and 185 effects gave g = 0.43 [0.31, 0.55], with high heterogeneity (94%). No tested moderator (example type, format, grade, content) was significant, and the effect "appears to be robust to variations in design, age, and content area." (Poster version; published as *A Meta-analysis of the Worked Examples Effect on Mathematics Performance*, *Educational Psychology Review*, 2023.) — [Barbieri et al. poster](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Worked-Examples-Meta-Poster_022722_SC.pdf)
- **[STRONG] Expertise reversal (Kalyuga et al. 2001, 2003).** Worked examples lose their advantage, and can hurt, as learners gain expertise. This has been shown in mechanical engineering, programming logic and other domains. Faded examples, where solution steps are removed gradually (Renkl, Atkinson & Große 2004), ease the transition. Completion problems that are "halfway worked out" matched fully worked examples (Paas 1992). Adding a second, more complex example aids transfer (Reed & Bolstad 1991). (secondary, with primary references listed) — [Wikipedia, worked-example effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worked-example_effect)
- **[MODERATE] Prior knowledge decides the order of example and problem (Reisslein et al. 2006).** In electrical circuit analysis (N = 185), low-prior-knowledge learners did best with example→problem pairs on near transfer, and high-prior-knowledge learners did best with problem→example pairs. Backward fading showed no advantage in this study. — [Reisslein et al. 2006](https://faculty.engineering.asu.edu/mre/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2020/02/Exp_Rev_LI06.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Varied examples help only under low load (Paas & van Merriënboer 1994).** High variation produced better transfer than low variation when learners studied worked examples, but not when they problem-solved under high cognitive load. (secondary) — [Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zMbuRKNDzLXba2nEcCa6MKV)
- **[MODERATE] Productive failure meta-analysis (Sinha & Kapur 2021, *Review of Educational Research*).** 53 studies, about 36.7% of comparisons with undergraduates. Problem-solving-first beat instruction-first for conceptual knowledge and transfer (g = .36), but the advantage "did not prove to be significantly greater" for procedural learning. Benefits were strongest when the design followed productive-failure principles. (secondary) — [UT Austin summary](https://education.utexas.edu/about/deans-office-units/office-instructional-innovation/instruction-then-problem-solving-or-vice-versa). Instruction after the attempt should build on the learner's failed solutions. Young children (grades 2 and 5) did not benefit. (secondary) — [Tech & Learning](https://techlearning.com/news/study-productive-failure-a-success-in-education)
- **[WEAK] Learning from failure (Darabi et al. 2018).** Only 12 experimental studies qualified. The result was "moderately positive," and no moderator was significant. — [Darabi, Arrington & Sayilir 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-018-9579-9)
- **[MODERATE] Retrieval versus worked examples depends on whether the rule was taught (Cao & Carvalho 2026).** See Q1. Worked examples won when no instruction preceded practice. Worked examples may be more efficient for relearning after long delays. — [Learning Scientists 2026](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2026/6/18-1)
- **[PRACTITIONER critique] Math Academy usually shows one worked example before exercises,** while the research cited involves several. Long examples caused attention drift for Matuschak. — [Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Math_Academy)

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: New-skill flow (the acquisition stage the queue inserts when a skill unlocks):**
  1. A worked example with labeled steps (subgoal labels).
  2. A second, varied example, or an example-problem pair.
  3. One or two completion or faded problems (the user fills in the missing steps).
  4. Independent fresh problems until 2–3 are correct in a row (Q4).
  This brief phase is where blocking is appropriate (Q3).
- **DESIGN: Reviews of learned skills are always cold attempts.** Showing an example first would be redundant for someone who knows the skill and would remove the retrieval (expertise reversal). The full worked solution appears after the attempt.
- **DESIGN: Use a graded hint ladder instead of all-or-nothing reveal**, for example "first step," then "next step," then "full solution." Record hint use as a degraded outcome (FSRS "Hard" or "Again"). This also gives a cheap first-step diagnostic in the spirit of Kalyuga's rapid assessment (not directly verified here).
- **DESIGN: A skill that lapsed after a long interval** (well beyond its stability) gets a worked example before the next attempt, per Cao & Carvalho's relearning suggestion. A recent, narrow miss goes straight to a fresh cold re-attempt after the solution has been reviewed.
- **DESIGN: Productive failure is optional and low priority.** It suits a few conceptual skills (for example, an "invent a measure of rate of change" challenge before the derivative lesson). It is not suited to procedural drill, and it needs carefully designed problems plus an explanation that addresses the user's failed attempts.
- **DESIGN: Fade scaffolding with expertise.** Early on, use low-variability problems right after examples. Increase variability (Q8) as stability rises.

### Gaps
- The published Barbieri et al. (2023) moderator results (fading, incorrect examples, self-explanation) could not be retrieved; only the poster's "no significant moderators."
- No direct evidence was retrieved on adaptive (learner-tailored) fading versus fixed fading. Kalyuga & Sweller's rapid-assessment papers were cited but not read.
- Sinha & Kapur's separate effect sizes for transfer and for high-fidelity productive failure, and their confidence intervals, were not retrievable from the summaries.

## Q6. Feedback: timing, elaboration, error correction, hypercorrection, confidence ratings, and the reliability of self-grading

### Takeaway
Feedback roughly doubles the testing effect, and elaborated, information-rich feedback is much more effective than right/wrong. Delaying feedback by about a week improved transfer in a college engineering class, despite students preferring immediate feedback. In lab studies, delay modestly reduces how many errors get corrected. High-confidence errors are corrected unusually well (hypercorrection), but confidence adds little beyond prior knowledge. Students' self-assessments are biased toward overconfidence, especially among weaker performers. Giving a detailed standard to grade against (an idea-unit or step rubric) substantially reduces that bias.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG] Feedback boosts testing effects.** Rowland 2014: with feedback g = 0.73 versus 0.39 without. — [Rowland 2014](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf). Yang 2021: with corrective feedback g = 0.537 versus 0.374 without (p = .001). — [Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- **[STRONG] Information content matters (Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020).** 435 studies and N > 61,000 gave d = 0.48 overall. By type: reinforcement or punishment d = 0.24, corrective d = 0.46, high-information feedback d = 0.99. "Feedback is more effective the more information it contains." Timing was not coded. — [Wisniewski et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full)
- **[MODERATE] Delayed feedback in college engineering (Mullet, Butler, Verdin, von Borries & Marsh 2014).** In two experiments in an upper-level engineering course, feedback on weekly homework one week after the deadline (versus right after) "produced superior transfer" to new exam problems. Students believed immediate feedback helped them more, which the authors call a "metacognitive disconnect." — [Mullet et al. 2014](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/delaying-feedback-promotes-transfer-of-knowledge-despite-student-/)
- **[MODERATE] Lab counterpoint on timing.** In general-knowledge experiments, error correction was lower after delayed feedback (51% vs 42% in Exp 1b; 46% vs 40% in Exp 2), although hypercorrection appeared under both timings. — [Sitzman, Rhodes & Tauber 2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-013-0344-3). Rohrer & Hartwig recommend showing the solution to every practice problem "soon after they attempt the problem," ideally by the next school day. — [Rohrer & Hartwig](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-spaced-and-interleaved-mathematics-practice-rohrer-hartwig.pdf)
- **[STRONG] Errorful learning plus correction (Metcalfe 2017, *Annual Review of Psychology*).** Making errors followed by corrective feedback benefits learning. High-confidence errors are corrected more readily (hypercorrection). Feedback should include "analysis of the reasoning that led to the mistake." Making and correcting errors in low-stakes practice is worthwhile when the goal is high-stakes performance. — [Metcalfe 2017](https://annualreviews.org/doi/abs/10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022)
- **[MODERATE] Hypercorrection persists, but confidence is a weak signal.** Hypercorrection gammas were positive in every condition (for example G = .48 and .52) and still present at 48 hours (G = .38 and .35). However, "knew-it-all-along" ratings predicted correction better (G = .71) than confidence (G = .27). The authors conclude prior knowledge is the main driver, with confidence possibly acting as a proxy for it. (General-knowledge facts, not math.) — [Sitzman et al. 2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-013-0344-3)
- **[MODERATE] Self-assessment is biased, and weak students are least accurate.** In Hacker et al. (2000), undergraduates' postdictions beat their predictions but did not improve over the semester. Low performers remained overconfident even with instruction, self-scored practice tests and reflection. — [Learning Scientists on Hacker et al. 2000](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2017/2/16-1)
- **[MODERATE] Detailed standards improve self-grading (Nederhand et al. 2018).** Idea-unit standards made self-assessment more accurate (absolute error .20 vs .24) and cut overconfidence (bias .06 vs .14) compared with whole-answer standards. Low performers were the most overconfident (bias .20) and benefited most. This study used memorized definitions, not math problems. — [Nederhand et al. 2018](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/109964/REPUB_109964-OA.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Learner control undermines retrieval benefits.** When students decided for themselves when to drop flashcards, retrieval practice gave no exam benefit. — [RetrievalPractice.org](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/archive/2018/successive-relearning)

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: Feedback stays immediate within the session, but the solution must be elaborated.** Show the full worked solution with the reasoning for each step (high-information, about d = 0.99 versus about 0.46 for corrective only), not just the answer. The delayed re-test that Mullet's results reward already happens through the scheduler: the skill comes back days later with a fresh problem. An optional "revisit yesterday's misses" block at the start of the next session adds delayed re-exposure without withholding feedback.
- **DESIGN: Make error correction an active step.** After a wrong answer, ask the user to mark the first incorrect step in their work against the solution and pick an error type (concept, method choice, algebra slip, arithmetic, sign, misread). Store the error type. Slips and concept errors should feed back into scheduling differently (for example, an algebra slip should not reset a calculus skill's stability as hard as a method-choice error would). This follows Metcalfe's recommendation to analyze the reasoning behind a mistake; the slip-versus-concept weighting is inference.
- **DESIGN: Collect a one-tap confidence rating before the reveal.** Uses: (a) flag high-confidence errors for a re-test within 1–2 days, because they are well corrected but also show a misconception; (b) treat low-confidence correct answers as FSRS "Hard"; (c) chart calibration over time. Do not weight confidence heavily in the memory model; it adds little beyond correctness.
- **DESIGN: Self-grading needs structure.** (1) Auto-check final answers whenever possible (numeric or symbolic equivalence; covered by a separate research stream). (2) Otherwise grade against a step rubric (key steps or "idea units"), never a single "did I get it?" button. (3) Log disagreements between self-grade and auto-check to estimate the user's grading bias, and alert when self-grades run lenient. The research predicts self-grading will drift lenient, especially on weak skills.
- **DESIGN: Never let the user retire a skill or drop an item manually.** Allow "suspend" only with friction, outside the normal flow.

### Gaps
- No study was found that measures self-grading accuracy for multi-step college math solutions against a full worked solution. The calibration evidence above is from multiple-choice exams and memorized definitions.
- Only one classroom study (Mullet et al. 2014) directly compares feedback delays for STEM problem solving. Its effect sizes and N were not in the abstract.
- Hypercorrection evidence is mostly general-knowledge facts; no math-specific hypercorrection study was retrieved.

## Q7. Desirable difficulties and difficulty calibration: what success rate to target, and how to ramp difficulty

### Takeaway
The "85% rule" (Wilson et al. 2019) is a mathematical result for gradient-descent learners on binary classification tasks. It was never tested on human learners and should not be treated as an empirical law of education. The closest human evidence (Eglington & Pavlik 2020) found that retention per unit of practice time peaked when items were practiced at a predicted recall of about 0.86–0.94, with an inverted U. Very hard practice (0.40) was clearly worse. The testing-effect literature also says low initial success removes the benefit. A target of about 85–90% success on reviews is well supported in spirit, and higher during first acquisition. Practice-phase accuracy must not be read as learning, because mixed and spaced practice lower it while raising delayed performance.

### Cited Findings
- **[WEAK as applied to humans] The 85% rule (Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen 2019, *Nature Communications*).** The optimal training error rate is about 15.87% (about 85% accuracy) for **binary classification** learned by **gradient descent** with Gaussian noise. The authors say it "remains to be generalized to a broader class of circumstances, such as multi-choice tasks and different learning algorithms." A Bayesian learner shows no sweet spot. Validation used a perceptron and a model of monkey perceptual learning, not human experiments. — [Wilson et al. preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/255182v1)
- **[MODERATE] The human optimum when time costs are counted (Eglington & Pavlik 2020, *npj Science of Learning*).** 291 adults practiced Japanese–English word pairs for 22 minutes under adaptive "optimal efficiency thresholds" (OETs) of .40, .70, .86, .94 and .98, plus a fixed-spacing control. Three days later, OET .86 (M = .48) and .94 (M = .47) beat .40, .98 and the control. The relationship was an inverted U, with OET .94 about 40% higher retention than the control and OET .40. The benefit over conventional spacing was d = 0.64. The authors caution that the optimum depends on materials and feedback: "When failures provide more learning or take less time, lower thresholds may work better." — [Eglington & Pavlik 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7567101/)
- **[STRONG] Low initial success removes the benefit.** Without feedback, g = 0.03 at or below 50% initial correct, versus 0.56 above 75%. — [Rowland 2014](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf). Transfer rises with initial accuracy (Pan & Rickard). — [Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zC1oBp6yE72b7YHzaJZmjXf)
- **[MODERATE] Interleaving does nothing when tasks are too hard or too easy.** — [Learning Scientists on Yan & Sana 2021](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2023/11/2-1)
- **[STRONG] Practice performance does not equal learning.** Blocked practice scored 89% during practice versus 60% for mixed, but 20% versus 63% at test (d = 1.34). — [Rohrer & Taylor 2007](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2007-rohrer.pdf). Spacing lowered initial quiz performance while improving long-term retention. — [Learning Scientists on Lyle et al. 2020](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2022/9/7-1)
- **[PRACTITIONER] FSRS desired retention.** Higher desired retention means shorter intervals and more reviews. Lower retention means more forgetting and relearning, so "the graph has a point of minimum workload." Anki (since version 24.04) can compute the retention that minimizes workload per unit of knowledge (knowledge = Σ R_i), using a simulator that samples from the user's own review history and time costs. — [FSRS wiki, "The optimal retention"](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/wiki/The-optimal-retention). The default desired retention is 90%. In one user's simulation of a vocabulary deck, 90% retention cost about 24 minutes per day, while 70% started at about 13 minutes per day and rose to about 23. — [Domenic Denicola, "Spaced repetition recap"](https://domenic.me/fsrs/)
- **[PRACTITIONER] Math Academy publishes no success-rate target.** Its pedagogy page states none. — [Math Academy pedagogy](https://mathacademy.com/pedagogy)

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: Target success rates by stage.**
  - Acquisition: aim for about 90%+ with scaffolding, using examples and hints until the criterion is met. Low early success wastes retrieval (Rowland).
  - Reviews: set FSRS desired retention at about 0.85–0.90 to start. This sits inside the Eglington & Pavlik optimum and matches the FSRS default.
  - Make it adjustable per course. Once a few hundred reviews are logged, compute a time-cost-aware optimum with the FSRS simulator approach, using real solve times (5–15 minutes per problem versus about 10 seconds per flashcard).
  - Two forces pull in opposite directions for problems. A failed problem takes a long time, which argues for higher retention. But a failed problem followed by a worked solution is highly informative, which argues for lower retention. The optimum has to be estimated from data rather than copied from flashcard practice.
- **DESIGN: Monitor session-level success rate as a health metric, not a goal.** If a day's success on reviews falls well below target (for example under about 70%), the likely causes are too many new skills, prerequisite gaps, or problem tiers that are too hard. Automatically reduce new-skill introductions and serve lower tiers. This threshold is an inference; no study set it.
- **DESIGN: Difficulty ramp per skill.** Tag every problem template with a tier: (1) basic or direct, (2) standard textbook, (3) exam-level or multi-step, (4) challenge or synthesis.
  - Choose the tier for a due skill from its state: early reviews (low stability) draw tiers 1–2, and later reviews draw tiers 3–4. Each successful review then meets a harder problem while the expected success stays near target.
  - Treat a tier jump that fails as information about the tier, not a full lapse of the skill (for example, rate it "Hard" and repeat at the same tier).
  - Cross-skill synthesis problems (as in the Samani & Pan test items) should be their own "skills," unlocked when their component skills are stable.
  - All of this is inference built on the findings above; no study tested this specific ramp.
- **DESIGN: Do not show practice accuracy as "how well you're learning."** Show delayed-review accuracy (first attempt after at least 7 days) as the learning metric.

### Gaps
- No human study tests the 85% rule as stated, and no study finds the optimal practice success rate for multi-step math problems.
- No study validates a tiered within-skill difficulty ramp tied to memory stability. This is a design hypothesis the app's own logs could test.
- Math Academy's internal success-rate data, if any, are not published.

## Q8. Variation of practice and varied problem formats for transfer

### Takeaway
Varying the problems for a skill (surface features, representations, formats) supports transfer, but mainly once cognitive load is manageable: after worked examples, not during unguided novice problem solving. Retrieval benefits carry over to rephrased items and different formats, with somewhat larger effects when practice matches the exam format. A mix of question types (facts plus higher-order) beats one type. Varied practice costs more time.

### Cited Findings
- **[MODERATE] Variation works under low load (Paas & van Merriënboer 1994).** High-variability worked examples produced better transfer than low-variability ones, but this did not hold for high-load problem solving. (secondary) — [Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zMbuRKNDzLXba2nEcCa6MKV)
- **[STRONG] Format and rephrasing (Yang 2021).** Rephrased items g = 0.558, identical 0.512, untested 0.321. Matched quiz/exam format g = 0.531 versus mismatched 0.399, so the benefit carries across formats but is larger when matched. — [Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf). In Pan & Rickard, response congruency was the largest transfer moderator (d = 0.58 vs 0.28). — [Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zC1oBp6yE72b7YHzaJZmjXf)
- **[MODERATE] Mixing question types (Agarwal 2019).** Mixed fact and higher-order quizzes produced the largest gain on higher-order final questions in a classroom. — [Learning Scientists on Agarwal 2019](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2019/6/27-1)
- **[MODERATE] Varied retrieval items take the most time** but help learners abstract rules for novel problems when instruction is absent. — [Learning Scientists on Cao & Carvalho 2026](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2026/6/18-1)
- **[MODERATE] Transfer to combined problems after interleaving.** Physics test items combined topics never paired in homework, and interleaved practice still produced large gains. — [Matuschak notes on Samani & Pan](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zTBR5JQDn9BFfUaPn4G58wn); [Samani & Pan 2021](https://doaj.org/article/da1dfb08ef7d4cd7a757a8dad0716c75)
- **[MODERATE] Within-category variability.** Lower within-category similarity strengthened interleaving effects in the meta-analysis. — [Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)
- **[PRACTITIONER critique] Template sameness.** Matuschak reports Math Academy proof exercises are template-based, so he "never has to choose a proof strategy," and its tasks lean toward "apply." — [Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Math_Academy)

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: Variation axes for each skill's problem generator:**
  - surface context: pure symbolic versus word problem versus applied units (physics or chemistry contexts later);
  - representation: equation, graph, table, verbal;
  - direction: forward ("find the derivative") versus inverse or reasoning ("find a function whose derivative…", "which graph is f′?");
  - parameter and structure variation (signs, nested forms, special cases);
  - answer format: final value, expression, or explanation.
  The selection should be spread so that consecutive reviews of a skill rarely share an axis value.
- **DESIGN: Introduce variability gradually.** Use low variability during acquisition (right after examples) and full variability during reviews. This follows the Paas & van Merriënboer load interaction.
- **DESIGN: Match the formats the course's exams will use** (show-your-work free response, the professor's style), and still include other formats for robustness.
- **DESIGN: Add "integration" problems** that need two or more skills from different sections as their own skills. They train the Samani & Pan-style transfer that single-skill review can't.
- Plan for more time per varied item when estimating daily load.

### Gaps
- Braithwaite & Goldstone (2015) and other math-specific variability studies were not retrieved because of the search budget.
- No study was found on how much surface variation is optimal for retrieval in math (too little invites memorizing the template; too much may defeat recognizing the skill).

## Q9. Session length, daily workload, fatigue, and sleep

### Takeaway
There is little direct evidence on the optimal length of a problem-solving session. The available pieces: spacing works over rests from 30 seconds to days, and massed practice depletes working memory (Chen et al.); within-session breaks help in the motor and skill literature; and practitioner data from Math Academy put sustainable serious practice at about 30–60 minutes per day. Sleep after learning helps consolidation and insight on a hidden-rule task. Workload should be budgeted in minutes, not items.

### Cited Findings
- **[MODERATE] Working-memory depletion.** Massed math practice lowered working-memory test scores and post-test learning relative to spaced practice, in elementary students. — [Learning Scientists on Chen et al. 2018](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2023/11/16). The spacing effect was "robust for varied lengths of resting, from 30 s to a few days," but "the concept of mental rest-from-deliberate-learning requires further empirical evidence." — [Chen, Paas & Sweller 2021](https://nlumaster.nl.edu/media/nledu/content-assets/documents/ctle/on-demand-learning-resources/Chen2021_Article_SpacingAndInterleavingEffectsR.pdf)
- **[MODERATE, mostly motor tasks] Short intertrial rests gave the largest spacing effects:** under 1 minute d = 0.71, 1–10 minutes d = 0.48, 10 minutes to 1 hour d = 0.26, over 1 day d = 0.16. — [Donovan & Radosevich 1999](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/1999-donovan.pdf)
- **[PRACTITIONER] Math Academy workload.** 1 XP ≈ 1 minute of focused work (a calibration choice). The maximum sustainable rate is "around 50-60 XP/day, that is, 50-60 minutes of serious practice per day," based on eyeballing leaderboards. About 30 XP/day produces "seriously fast" progress. Their model student does 40 XP per weekday. These are opinions or internal assumptions, not studies. — [Skycak, sustainable daily XP](https://justinmath.com/what-is-the-highest-sustainable-daily-xp-on-math-academy/); [*The Math Academy Way*](https://justinmath.com/files/the-math-academy-way.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Small doses work.** Personalized review took about 30 minutes per week and produced large retention gains. — [ScienceDaily on Lindsey et al. 2014](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/01/140121093046.htm)
- **[PRACTITIONER] FSRS workload simulation.** One user's simulation: about 24 minutes per day at 90% retention for a vocabulary deck. Problem-based decks will cost far more per item. — [Domenic Denicola](https://domenic.me/fsrs/)
- **[MODERATE] Sleep and insight (Wagner et al. 2004, *Nature*).** After initial training, "over twice as many subjects gained insight into the hidden rule after sleep as after wakefulness," regardless of time of day. "Sleep, by restructuring new memory representations, facilitates extraction of explicit knowledge." — [Wagner et al. 2004 abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v427y2004i6972d10.1038_nature02223.html)
- **[MODERATE] Interleaved assignments take longer** (teacher reports in the 2020 RCT). — [Matuschak notes on Rohrer et al. 2020](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zHvJf88XUgLPMhdTigkoiUR)

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: Daily budget in minutes, with time estimates per item.** Track actual solve time per skill and tier, project tomorrow's load from FSRS due dates multiplied by expected time, and cap new-skill introductions so projected load stays inside the budget. A default of about 30–60 minutes of problems per day is the practitioner range; let the user set it.
- **DESIGN: Built-in micro-breaks and an optional split session.** Suggest a short break after about 25–30 minutes of continuous problem solving, and offer a second sitting later in the day for the rest of the queue. This is inferred from the working-memory depletion and spacing-of-rest evidence; no study validates these specific numbers.
- **DESIGN: New material first in the session, while fresh; reviews and flashcards after.** This is inference from the depletion account, not tested. Studying new material on a day with normal sleep afterward is supported by consolidation evidence. A learner who gets stuck on a hard new skill can be told to sleep on it (Wagner), with a scheduled re-attempt the next day.
- **DESIGN: Exam-week mode** should keep daily minutes roughly constant and shift the mix toward exam-tagged skills. It should not spike workload, because cramming conflicts with spacing.

### Gaps
- No study was found on the optimal length of a daily problem-solving session or on fatigue within sessions for college math. The Pomodoro versus self-regulated breaks study (Biwer et al. 2023) could not be retrieved.
- Sleep evidence here is a single lab task (number reduction). No study was retrieved on sleep and retention of procedural math skills in college students.

## Q10. Motivation and adherence in spaced-repetition and adaptive systems: backlogs, streaks, XP, dropout

### Takeaway
Adherence is the main practical risk. When students control their own spacing they often don't follow through, and self-paced mastery courses lower completion. Gamification reliably shows small-to-medium cognitive benefits, but its motivational and behavioral benefits do not hold up in rigorous designs. Streaks increase engagement while they last, and engagement drops when they break. Reminders about the broken streak don't help; offering a fresh goal does. Learners consistently prefer less effective strategies (blocking, immediate feedback, massing), so the app should enforce good scheduling and explain why. No peer-reviewed study of the SRS review-backlog "avalanche" was found.

### Cited Findings
- **[MODERATE] Self-regulated spacing has poor completion.** 35% (distributed) versus 71% (massed), p = .007. — [Barzagar Nazari & Ebersbach 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02170/pdf)
- **[STRONG] Self-pacing lowers completion in mastery courses.** In 23 of 32 Keller PSI studies, conventional classes had higher completion. — [Kulik et al. 1990](https://www.academia.edu/81783373/Effectiveness_of_Mastery_Learning_Programs_A_Meta_Analysis)
- **[MODERATE] Gamification meta-analysis (Sailer & Homner 2020).** Cognitive outcomes g = .49 (still .42 in high-rigor designs). Motivational g = .36, but only .22 and non-significant in rigorous designs. Behavioral g = .25, .27 and non-significant in rigorous designs. Designs combining competition with collaboration beat competition alone for behavioral outcomes. — [Sailer & Homner 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)
- **[MODERATE] Streaks (Silverman & Barasch 2023, *Journal of Consumer Research*).** Streaks become goals in themselves. When they break, through the user's lapse or a glitch, people often disengage. Messaging that highlights the failure "typically doesn't work." Offering a new goal, or adjusting goal periods, works better. They also caution about paid streak "repairs." Data included a language-learning app. (secondary) — [CU Boulder on Silverman & Barasch 2023](https://www.colorado.edu/business/news/2023/04/20/research-streaks-marketing-tech-barasch)
- **[MODERATE] Scheduler quality affects engagement (Settles & Meeder 2016, Duolingo).** Half-life regression cut recall-prediction error by more than 45% versus baselines (MAE 0.128 vs Leitner 0.235). In a live experiment with 3.3 million users, the better model raised daily "any activity" retention by 12.0%. An earlier version had *reduced* practice-session retention by 7.3%, which shows that scheduler changes can move engagement either way. — [Settles & Meeder 2016](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/P16-1174.pdf)
- **[STRONG] Learners prefer less effective strategies.** 72% judged blocking better (Yan, Bjork & Bjork 2016). — [Yan et al. 2016](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/11/YanBjorkBjork2016.pdf). Physics students believed they learned less from interleaving. — [Samani & Pan 2021](https://doaj.org/article/da1dfb08ef7d4cd7a757a8dad0716c75). Engineering students preferred immediate feedback despite worse transfer. — [Mullet et al. 2014](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/delaying-feedback-promotes-transfer-of-knowledge-despite-student-/)
- **[PRACTITIONER] Math Academy engagement design.** XP calibrated to minutes, a daily XP goal, bonus XP for perfect scores, weekly leagues, and "accumulated XP is never lost." — [Math Academy, How it works](https://mathacademy.com/how-it-works). Game elements "need to be resistant to 'hacking' behaviors." — [*The Math Academy Way*](https://justinmath.com/files/the-math-academy-way.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Mastery classes produced more positive attitudes** toward instruction (0.63) and toward the subject (0.40). — [Kulik et al. 1990](https://www.academia.edu/81783373/Effectiveness_of_Mastery_Learning_Programs_A_Meta_Analysis)

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: Backlog ("avalanche") handling.** No peer-reviewed study was found, so these are inferences:
  1. Load-balance due dates (small random fuzz on intervals, spreading reviews to lighter days).
  2. Gate new-skill introduction on projected 7-day load, not just today's.
  3. After missed days, show a capped, prioritized "catch-up" queue: lowest predicted retrievability first, weighted by exam proximity and by how many later skills depend on the skill. Hide the full overdue count.
  4. Spread the backlog over several days at the normal daily budget instead of demanding it all at once.
  Because FSRS models retrievability at the actual review time, late reviews are handled correctly by the model. The danger is psychological, not algorithmic.
- **DESIGN: Streaks that forgive.** Base streaks on weekly targets (for example, 5 of 7 days) or include freeze days, so one miss does not reset a long record. Display cumulative, never-lost totals (minutes practiced, skills mastered) next to streaks. After a break, offer a fresh short goal ("clear today's 10-minute catch-up") instead of a message about the broken streak.
- **DESIGN: Calibrate XP or progress points to minutes of focused effort, and make them hard to game.** For example, no XP for revealing the solution without an attempt, and less XP for hint-heavy solves. For a single-user, offline app, the leaderboard and competition elements are irrelevant, and the motivational evidence for them is weak anyway.
- **DESIGN: An "evidence dashboard" to counter metacognitive illusions.** Show the user's own delayed-review accuracy, how interleaved-day performance compares with what they felt, retention curves per course, and exam-readiness (predicted retrievability on the exam date). Pair this with short explanations. Yan et al. found experience plus explanation was what changed beliefs.
- **DESIGN: The scheduler enforces spacing and interleaving by default.** User overrides such as "study this topic only" should exist for exam cramming but be clearly marked as less effective.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found on SRS review-backlog effects, dropout curves of Anki users, or how backlog size predicts abandonment.
- The magnitudes from Silverman & Barasch (2023) were not reported in the summary retrieved.
- No study was found specifically on adherence in self-directed, single-user STEM spaced-repetition apps.

## Q11. Spaced retrieval of procedural and problem-solving skills versus facts: forgetting curves for skills, and whether FSRS-style models fit

### Takeaway
Cognitive skills decay substantially without use, more than physical skills, and they decay at different rates per skill. In engineering-math classrooms, spaced retrieval of skills improves long-term retention, but lab results for math procedures are mixed, and spacing effects shrink for complex tasks. FSRS was developed and benchmarked on flashcard review logs. No published validation of FSRS for scheduling problem-solving skills with freshly generated problems was found. Skill-level knowledge-tracing models that include forgetting (DAS3H) predict math performance better than models without it. Math Academy's "implicit repetition" credit for prerequisites is plausible but proprietary and unvalidated.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG] Skill decay (Arthur et al. 1998).** 53 articles and 189 data points. Decay grows with retention interval, reaching d ≈ −1.4 after more than 365 days of nonuse. Cognitive tasks decayed more (d ≈ −1.15) than physical tasks (−0.75). Overlearning was barely related to decay (r = .08), with sparse data. — [Arthur et al. 1998](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/1998-arthur.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Spacing effects shrink for complex tasks** (d about 0.07–0.11 in the high-complexity clusters). — [Donovan & Radosevich 1999](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/1999-donovan.pdf). Spacing benefits "appear weak for visuospatial skills." — [Pashler, Rohrer & Cepeda](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/PRC2006.pdf). There was no spacing effect for permutation procedures in 235 university students. — [Ebersbach & Barzagar Nazari 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00811/pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Classroom math skills do benefit.** Spaced quizzing of precalculus skills improved retention a month after the final (.59 vs .54). — [Hopkins et al. 2018](https://peer.asee.org/29900.pdf). Benefits carried into the next semester's calculus course. — [Learning Scientists on Lyle et al. 2020](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2022/9/7-1)
- **[MODERATE] Skill-level forgetting models (DAS3H, Choffin et al. 2019).** Item difficulty plus per-skill learning and forgetting over time windows (1/24, 1, 7, 30, +∞ days), built on Knowledge Tracing Machines. Mean AUC: 0.826 versus 0.775 for DASH on Algebra 2005, 0.791 versus 0.749 on Bridge to Algebra, and 0.744 versus 0.703 on ASSISTments 2012. Per-skill parameters were worth +0.03–0.04 AUC. Items tagged with multiple skills are handled. All three datasets are math. — [DAS3H paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.06873)
- **[PRACTITIONER] FSRS model and data.** FSRS tracks per-card difficulty (1–10), stability (days for recall to fall from 100% to 90%) and retrievability. It has used a power-law forgetting curve since v4, has 21 parameters, and is fit by maximum likelihood on the user's own review history. — [Domenic Denicola](https://domenic.me/fsrs/); [History of FSRS](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/G7fpGCi8r7nCKXsQk). Training and benchmark data are Anki flashcard logs: 20,000 collections, about 1.4 billion reviews. FSRS-5 began to account for same-day reviews. Modeling short-term memory as a separate component "was not successful." — [History of FSRS](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/G7fpGCi8r7nCKXsQk). The open benchmark's calibration metric, RMSE(bins), groups reviews by interval, review number and lapse count. SM-2-style schedulers produce no probabilities and cannot be scored on it. — [srsbench README](https://ftp.fau.de/cran/web/packages/srsbench/readme/README.html)
- **[PRACTITIONER] Math Academy's Fractional Implicit Repetition (FIRe).** Topics, not cards, are scheduled, and each review is a random problem from the topic's pool, with later reviews 3–5 questions. Success on an advanced topic passes fractional credit down an expert-built "encompassing" graph, and failures pass penalties upward. A per-student, per-topic "learning speed" scales intervals, and implicit credit is discarded for slow learners. Skycak: "don't have any official academic studies out at the moment." The evidence is anecdotal. — [Skycak, hierarchical spaced repetition](https://justinmath.com/individualized-spaced-repetition-in-hierarchical-knowledge-structures/). Matuschak found its hierarchical SRS "too conservative," since he forgot earlier material faster than it assumed. — [Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Math_Academy)
- **[MODERATE] Long-term retention of math comes from continued use in later courses** (Bahrick: no algebra loss after 50 years for those who took courses beyond calculus). — [Willingham, AFT](https://aft.org/node/10659)

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: FSRS is a reasonable starting point, not a validated one.** Run it per skill with the default parameters at first. Log every attempt with: skill ID, problem template ID, tier, time to solve, hints used, self-grade, auto-check result, confidence and error type. After a few hundred reviews (enough for the optimizer), refit the parameters for each course. Check calibration with log loss and RMSE(bins) on a time-based holdout. If predictions are poorly calibrated, add template and tier difficulty as covariates. Fresh problems inject item-difficulty noise that FSRS's per-card model does not represent; a DAS3H-style item term is one fix.
- **DESIGN: Map problem outcomes onto FSRS's 4 buttons consistently.**
  - Again: wrong answer, or needed the full solution.
  - Hard: correct but needed hints, very slow, low confidence, or a slip-only error. (The slip treatment is a judgment call.)
  - Good: correct, independent, normal time.
  - Easy: fast and confident on a high tier.
  Derive the button automatically where possible rather than asking the user to choose, to limit self-grading bias (Q6).
- **DESIGN: Implicit credit only, conservatively.** When a problem exercises prerequisite skills (a chain-rule problem uses power-rule differentiation), consider small partial stability credit for the prerequisites. Make the weights conservative and tunable, given Matuschak's report and the absence of validation. Using failures to diagnose prerequisites (fail an encompassing skill → schedule an early review of its prerequisites) is lower-risk and more useful.
- **DESIGN: Lean into Bahrick's pattern.** Tag each skill with the later courses that use it (Calc 1 → Calc 2, Calc 3, Physics). Long-term review intervals can then rely partly on real use in those courses, and the app can surface "foundation" skills before a new course starts.
- **Expect skills to need shorter early intervals than flashcards** (cognitive tasks decay more, procedures are multi-step), but learn this from the user's own data rather than assuming it.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or benchmark evaluation of FSRS (or SM-2) for scheduling *skills* with *generated* problems was found. Whether the power-law forgetting curve fits multi-step procedure retention is unknown.
- FSRS-versus-SM-2 numeric benchmark results were not retrieved (search budget).
- No independent evaluation of Math Academy's FIRe or hierarchical scheduling exists in the sources found.

## Q12. Programming practice (later phase): evidence on spaced repetition and retrieval practice for coding skills

### Takeaway
The evidence base for programming is thin. One quasi-experimental study (n = 200 freshmen) found more frequent spaced quizzing improved final exams. One small correlational CS1 study found students who spaced and interleaved retrieval quizzes scored much higher. Retrieval effects in engineering courses generally are about g ≈ 0.58. Expertise reversal has been shown for programming logic. No RCT of spaced repetition for code *writing* was found. The math-derived design (skills, fresh problems, interleaving, worked examples, then cold attempts) is a reasonable extrapolation, not a validated one.

### Cited Findings
- **[WEAK/MODERATE] Spaced quizzing for novice programmers (Li et al. 2021, *Journal of Pacific Rim Psychology*).** 200 freshmen on a "Daily Quiz" mobile platform with the same number of multiple-choice questions, prompted every 3 days versus every 7 days. The 3-day group did "significantly" better on final exams and on first-check correctness. Effect sizes were not in the abstract. — [Li et al. 2021](https://pure.bit.edu.cn/en/publications/evaluation-of-a-practice-system-supporting-distributed-practice-f/)
- **[WEAK] CS1 retrieval-practice quizzes (Moraes, Lionelle, Ghosh & Folkestad 2023).** A Java CS1 course with unlimited-retake formative quizzes, practice-pattern visualizations and reflection (N = 47 analyzed). Students with spaced or interleaved practice patterns beat those with massed patterns on the final coding exam (8.00 vs 6.84, g = .68) and course grade (94.92 vs 87.37, g = .92). There was no randomization, and the authors acknowledge selection effects. — [Moraes et al. 2023](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10544324)
- **[MODERATE] Engineering retrieval effects in classrooms:** g = 0.584 (k = 11). — [Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Expertise reversal includes programming-logic tasks** (Kalyuga et al. 2000/2001). (secondary) — [Wikipedia, worked-example effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worked-example_effect)
- **[WEAK] A 2026 Brown CS course lecture on spaced repetition cites no studies of SRS for programming,** only vocabulary, medical-school Anki (about one letter grade) and textbook-integrated SRS. — [Brown CSCI 1377 lecture](https://cel.cs.brown.edu/csci-1377-s26/03_Mnemonics_II.pdf)

### Inferences
- **DESIGN (C++ phase): Reuse the same skill model.** Item types per skill could include short code-writing tasks checked by unit tests (auto-graded, which avoids self-grading bias), code-tracing or predict-the-output items, find-the-bug items, and "which construct/STL container fits?" discrimination items (interleave similar constructs: references versus pointers, vector versus deque). Syntax and standard-library facts go to flashcards that require production. This is extrapolation from the math evidence; the programming-specific evidence is limited to the two weak studies above.
- **DESIGN: Expect expertise reversal.** Worked examples (annotated code) first for new constructs, cold code-writing in reviews.
- An offline desktop app can compile and run tests locally, which makes C++ the domain where automatic grading is most feasible. This partly offsets the weak evidence base, since feedback quality will be high.

### Gaps
- No RCT of spaced retrieval of code-writing skills was found, and no study measured forgetting curves for programming skills.
- Programming-education studies on worked examples or subgoal labels (for example Margulieux and colleagues) were not retrieved because of the search budget.

## Q13 (cross-cutting). How the findings fit together in one daily-queue design, and where Math Academy's claims outrun the evidence

### Takeaway
The app's core concept is strongly supported by the most robust findings in the field: one mixed daily queue, skills scheduled by a memory model, a fresh problem for each review, and attempts before solutions. These are retrieval practice, spacing and interleaving, all rated high or moderate utility, with math classroom RCTs behind them. The biggest evidence-based risks are:
1. Putting skills into cold retrieval before they are learned (low initial success removes the benefit).
2. Weak, unstructured self-grading.
3. Adherence and backlog collapse.
4. Treating FSRS, the 85% rule, and Math Academy's mechanisms as validated for problem-skill scheduling when they are not.

### Cited Findings
- Practice testing and distributed practice are high utility; interleaving is moderate. — [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/954654)
- Interleaved math RCT: d = 0.83 at about 1 month. — [Rohrer et al. 2020](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2019-rohrer.pdf). Classroom testing effect in math/statistics: g = 0.43. — [Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- Low initial success removes the testing benefit. — [Rowland 2014](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf)
- **Math Academy claims that go beyond the evidence:**
  - The "4x" learning-speed claim rests on the company's own XP-to-minutes comparison with AP Calculus BC (about 6,000 XP), not an independent study. — [*The Math Academy Way*](https://justinmath.com/files/the-math-academy-way.pdf)
  - "We don't have any official academic studies out." FIRe, "theoretical maximum learning efficiency," and "world's first" are unsupported. — [Skycak](https://justinmath.com/individualized-spaced-repetition-in-hierarchical-knowledge-structures/)
  - The "doubled test scores" interleaving claim conflicts with the source it quotes. — [Skycak, interleaving](https://justinmath.com/cognitive-science-of-learning-interleaving/)
  - The "10x more finely scaffolded" and roughly 1,000-step calculus figures are self-descriptions. — [Math Academy pedagogy](https://mathacademy.com/pedagogy)
  - The 50–60 XP/day ceiling comes from leaderboard eyeballing. — [Skycak, daily XP](https://justinmath.com/what-is-the-highest-sustainable-daily-xp-on-math-academy/)
  - On the other side, most of Math Academy's *principles* do line up with the research reviewed here: mastery, spacing, interleaving, worked examples, retrieval and non-interference.

### Inferences
- **DESIGN: Daily queue algorithm (synthesis; each element is traced to a question above):**
  1. **Budget.** Take today's minute budget (Q9). Compute due skills from FSRS, plus any skills pulled forward by an exam-date constraint (Q2).
  2. **Triage.** If the due load exceeds the budget, rank by (low retrievability) × (exam proximity) × (number of later skills that depend on it), and defer the rest without shaming (Q10).
  3. **New skills.** If there is spare budget and the projected 7-day load allows, unlock new skills whose prerequisites are stable. Each gets a mini-lesson (worked examples, then faded problems, then 2–3 correct in a row on fresh variants), placed early in the session (Q4, Q5, Q9).
  4. **Ordering.** Interleave the reviews with no headers and the skill name hidden. Put confusable skills near each other and keep same-skill items apart. Keep flashcards as a short segment (Q3).
  5. **Item selection.** For each due skill, generate a fresh problem: a tier chosen by stability, varied along the variation axes, and matched to the exam format (Q7, Q8).
  6. **Attempt.** Cold, with a hint ladder available. Ask for confidence, then check the answer automatically where possible, or with a rubric (Q6).
  7. **Feedback.** Show the full elaborated solution. On an error, the user marks the first wrong step and picks an error type. Derive the FSRS rating automatically (Q6, Q11).
  8. **After a failure.** Re-attempt a fresh variant later in the same session (after other items) or the next day. Flag high-confidence errors. If the skill lapsed after a long interval, re-show a worked example first. If failures repeat, check prerequisites (Q5, Q11).
  9. **Mastery.** A skill counts as mastered after about 3 successful spaced reviews and a stability threshold. The user cannot retire skills manually (Q4).
  10. **Metrics shown.** Delayed-review accuracy, calibration, predicted exam-day retrievability and minutes practiced. Practice-phase accuracy is not shown as a learning measure (Q7, Q10).
- **DESIGN: Settings worth making tunable per course:** desired retention (default 0.9), daily minutes, new-skill cap, blocked-intro length (default 2–4 problems), mastery stability threshold, and implicit-credit weight (default low or off).
- **DESIGN: Built-in self-evaluation.** The app's logs can measure what the literature cannot: within-person comparisons of delayed accuracy by tier, by interleaving neighbor, or by retention setting. These can tune the parameters above over time.

### Gaps
- Several 2024–2026 studies probably exist that were not found because the search budget ran out: newer interleaving and spacing meta-analyses in math, studies of FSRS on non-flashcard material, and published outcome evaluations of Math Academy.
- No source tested the whole package (adaptive SRS, interleaving, mastery gating and worked-example introductions) against a conventional course with college students. The component effects cannot be assumed to add up.
