## Q1. What do the AI tutors and AI study tools actually do as of late 2026? (ChatGPT Study Mode, Gemini Guided Learning/LearnLM, Claude learning mode, Khanmigo, photo-solve apps, Chinese AI tutors, Duolingo Max, Math Academy)

### Takeaway
Every frontier lab now ships a "learning mode" that is a prompt/style layer asking for Socratic behavior (ChatGPT study mode, Gemini Guided Learning, Claude "Learning" style); none launched with outcome data, the user can switch each off, and OpenAI itself admits the behavior is inconsistent. The photo-solve apps (Photomath, Mathway, Gauth, Question.AI, Brainly) remain answer-giving tools, with Photomath and Mathway getting correctness from solvers rather than from an LLM. Math Academy is the one product here whose "AI" is a non-generative expert system for scheduling and diagnosis, which is the closest match to Toishi's own approach.

### Cited Findings

#### General-purpose "learning modes" (prompt-level Socratic behavior)
- **ChatGPT study mode.** [Doc] Launched 2025-07-29 for logged-in Free, Plus, Pro and Team users (Edu "in the next few weeks"); started from "Study and learn" in the tools menu. Features: Socratic questions, hints and self-reflection prompts; scaffolded responses that limit context to avoid overload; personalization from skill-assessing questions and memory of earlier chats; quizzes, open-ended questions and feedback as knowledge checks; the user can toggle study mode on and off mid-conversation. It runs on custom system instructions written with teachers, scientists and pedagogy experts, and OpenAI states this "results in some inconsistent behavior and mistakes across conversations." No quantitative outcome data at launch; plans to train the behavior into the main models. — [OpenAI, Introducing study mode](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-study-mode/)
- **OpenAI's first learning-outcome disclosure (2026-03-04).** [Vendor, RCT, unpublished] A randomized study with "over 300" college students preparing for neuroscience and microeconomics exams; control used traditional online resources with AI overviews disabled; two groups used study-mode variants; sessions nominally 40 minutes and participation was not tied to exam performance. Neuroscience: directionally positive but not distinguishable from control (onboarding and technical issues cut study time for some users). Microeconomics: study-mode students scored roughly 15% higher (relative). Analysis "still underway." OpenAI also announced a Learning Outcomes Measurement Suite (system instructions, learning-interaction classifiers, learning-quality graders, longitudinal graders, standardized cognitive and metacognitive measures; tracked capabilities are autonomous motivation, productive engagement, task persistence, metacognition and recall), a study with nearly 20,000 Estonian students aged 16-18, partners University of Tartu and Stanford SCALE, and a pledge to release the suite as a public resource; it says "there will be no silver bullet in terms of what to optimize for." — [OpenAI, Understanding AI and learning outcomes](https://openai.com/index/understanding-ai-and-learning-outcomes/)
- **Gemini Guided Learning and LearnLM.** [Doc] Announced 2025-08-06: a "personal learning companion" in the Gemini app that uses probing open-ended questions, breaks problems into steps, and gives multimodal responses (images, diagrams, videos, interactive quizzes) that "test knowledge by focusing on the process rather than only the final answer." It is built on LearnLM, "a family of models fine-tuned for learning and grounded in educational research," whose capabilities were folded into Gemini 2.5; Google says its learning-expert partners found "simply improving prompting wasn't enough." The post gives no outcome data, no controlled studies and no limitations. — [Google, Guided Learning](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/guided-learning/)
- **Google's own comparative claims (2025-05-20).** [Vendor] Gemini 2.5 is "the world's leading model for learning"; "Gemini 2.5 Pro outperformed competitors on every category of learning science principles" (chart lists Claude 3.7, GPT-4o, o3); "educators and pedagogy experts preferred Gemini 2.5 Pro." The page gives no rater counts or percentages. Practice quizzes with hints and explanations rolled out for ages 18 and up; a conversational tutor (Project Astra prototype) was "later this year." — [Google, Learn in newer, deeper ways with Gemini](https://blog.google/outreach-initiatives/education/google-gemini-learnlm-update/)
- **Google "Learn Your Way".** [Vendor-authored, Preprint, RCT-lab] Gemini 2.5 Pro turns a textbook chapter into personalized text, slides, audio and mind maps with embedded quizzes. Randomized lab study: 60 students aged 15-18 (30 per arm), one adolescent-brain-development chapter, 20-40 minutes; higher scores than a plain PDF reader on the immediate test (p = 0.03) and on a retention test sent three days later (p = 0.03; 58 of 60 completed); effect sizes appear only in a figure; educator rubric ratings above 0.90. Authors list one chapter, no isolation of components, retention only at three days. — [arXiv 2509.13348](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.13348)
- **Claude learning mode.** [Doc] Introduced 2025-04-02 inside Claude for Education (higher-ed institutions, used within Projects): "guides students' reasoning process rather than providing answers," asking e.g. "How would you approach this problem?" and "What evidence supports your conclusion?"; no studies or evaluation on the page. — [Anthropic, Claude for Education](https://www.anthropic.com/news/introducing-claude-for-education). On 2025-08-14 it was extended to all users as a "Learning" option in the Claude.ai style menu (Socratic; can be turned off at any time), plus Claude Code "Explanatory" and "Learning" output styles (Learning pauses and leaves a "#TODO" asking the user to write 5-10 lines); Output Styles were opened to developers. Anthropic's education lead cited students' own sense that copy-paste "is not good for their long-term learning"; no data. — [Engadget](https://engadget.com/ai/anthropic-brings-claudes-learning-mode-to-regular-users-and-devs-170018471.html)
- **Anthropic Education Report (April 2025).** [Vendor, descriptive] About 1 million anonymized Claude.ai Free/Pro conversations from higher-education email addresses, 574,740 after filtering for academic relevance over 18 days. Nearly half (~47%) were "Direct" (seeking answers with minimal engagement). By Bloom's taxonomy, Claude's responses were creating 39.8%, analyzing 30.2%, applying 10.9%, understanding 10.0%, remembering 1.8%. Computer science was 38.6% of conversations versus 5.4% of US degrees; natural sciences and mathematics 15.2% versus 9.2%. The authors warn AI "may provide a crutch... stifling the development of foundational skills." — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-education-report-how-university-students-use-claude)

#### Khanmigo
- Configured to withhold answers and respond with hints, questions and step-by-step prompts via a chat window and suggested prompts; teachers see a dashboard; license cost about $15 per student per year in the Hamilton County trial. — [Oreopoulos & Low, EdWorkingPaper 26-1551](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1551.pdf)
- Math accuracy engineering (post dated 2024-05-17): "Khanmigo now uses a calculator to solve numerical problems instead of using AI's predictive capabilities"; the AI is instructed to "write out all the ways in which the student may have arrived at their answer"; the post admits "Sometimes Khanmigo makes mistakes when evaluating whether a student is right or wrong," gives no before/after error rates and says it "occasionally makes mistakes." — [Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/why-were-deeply-invested-in-making-ai-better-at-math-tutoring-and-what-weve-been-up-to-lately/)
- Trial results are under Q2 (S1); Khan's May 2026 A/B results are in the earlier notes [Carried] (summary of recent attempts +3.4% next-item correctness; surfacing unmastered prerequisites +2.7%; example problems no effect) — [Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/?p=21314).

#### Photo-solve and homework-help apps
- **Photomath (Google).** [Doc] A scanned image goes to cloud servers where "a highly advanced neural network" determines the formula, then "a problem-solving algorithm is applied to the formula to provide the solution and solving steps"; Google calls it an "expert system that emulates the problem-solving ability of a human math expert." Machine-generated steps are free; Photomath Plus adds "AI-powered animated Tutorials," hints and "third-level steps." The page does not say an LLM is used, does not say humans verify solutions, and makes no accuracy statement. — [Google Photomath help](https://support.google.com/photomath/answer/14328660?hl=en)
- **Mathway (Chegg).** [Doc] "AI-powered math solver and calculator" with a photo solver, free to use, an optional subscription that "unlocks the full work shown for every step", coverage from basic math through calculus, statistics, linear algebra, chemistry and physics; no price and no accuracy disclaimer on the page. — [Mathway](https://www.mathway.com/). Chegg cut roughly 45% of staff in October 2025, attributed to AI eroding traffic (search-result headlines only; neither page was opened) — [AllWork](https://allwork.space/2025/10/chegg-cuts-45-of-employees-after-ai-slashes-traffic); [Tech.co](https://tech.co/news/chegg-fires-half-workforce-new-realities-ai).
- **Gauth (ByteDance) and Question.AI (Zuoyebang's international app).** [Journalistic, informal tests, article uses Sept 2025 data] Gauth shows the final answer first with steps underneath; Question.AI and ByteDance's Chinese Doubao 爱学 show steps before the solution. Gauth reached nearly 700,000 downloads per day globally by March 2024 (TikTok creator advertising) and over 2 million peak daily active users in 2024; Question.AI's largest user bases are the USA and Indonesia. Informal accuracy test: all solved basic integration by parts; Gauth solved trig integrals that Doubao could not (Doubao produced a 31-step attempt "none of [which] related to each other"); Question.AI and Zuoyebang gave wrong answers on the trig integral; the author concludes the apps handle most high-school math but college calculus "can stump them." No formal research cited. — [ChinaTalk](https://www.chinatalk.media/p/cheating-apps-chinas-latest-tech)
- **Brainly.** [Vendor, App Store] Photo or typed question to step-by-step explanation; "Real human tutors. Smart AI."; "Every interaction is monitored and 100% kid-safe"; Brainly Plus $9.99/month or $39.99/year, Brainly Tutor (live human tutors) $29.99/month or $95.99/year; 4.7 stars from 284K ratings; "See real grade improvement in four weeks — or we'll make it right"; no accuracy disclaimer, though a user review on the page says some tutors gave wrong answers. — [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/app/745089947)

#### Chinese AI tutors (new items only; mechanics are in `asian_practice_tools.md` sections 3-4 [Carried])

#### Duolingo Max
- [Vendor] GPT-4-based tier above Super. #### Math Academy
- "Math Academy's AI is an expert system that emulates the decisions of an expert tutor regarding what tasks a student should work on at any given point in time": a knowledge graph of topics, prerequisites and problem difficulty; a student model using spaced repetition (its own "Fractional Implicit Repetition", FIRe); an adaptive diagnostic claimed to cut questions "by an order of magnitude"; task selection using mastery learning, layering and interleaving; quizzes tuned so students score "about 80% on average." The page never mentions large language models, generative AI or chatbots, does not say who writes explanations or problems, and cites no outcome data. — [Math Academy, How our AI works](https://mathacademy.com/how-our-ai-works)
- Non-staff comments in a March 2025 Hacker News thread (low reliability): LLMs are "amazing at complementing Math Academy" for conceptual questions; one user found Math Academy's assessment exposed gaps an LLM would not have prompted. — [Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=43281460)

### Inferences
- **Soft guardrails.** The three frontier learning modes are prompt-level behavior that users can switch off, and OpenAI concedes inconsistency. The implementations that carry positive evidence (B1, B5, H1 "GPT Tutor") add something the modes lack: verified solutions placed in the prompt and a platform that controls the dialogue.
- **Answer-giving UX.** Photomath and Mathway are hallucination-resistant for supported problem types because a solver, not a language model, produces the steps, but their design is answer delivery; LLM-first apps (Gauth, Question.AI) show answers first and fail on harder calculus in the one informal test found.
- **"AI" means two different things.** Math Academy uses the word for scheduling and diagnosis (what Toishi does with FSRS, a skill graph and an Elo difficulty model, with no generative model); the frontier labs use it for dialogue. The evidence in Q2 is almost entirely about the second meaning.
- **Evidence ranking among named products:** Khanmigo (a two-year cluster RCT with a small effect) > ChatGPT study mode (one unpublished vendor RCT, mixed) > Gemini, Claude, Photomath, Mathway, Gauth, Question.AI, Brainly, Duolingo Max (no outcome evidence found).

### Gaps
- No feature changes after March 2026 were found for ChatGPT study mode, Gemini Guided Learning or Claude learning mode; the latest official sources are those dated above.
- Duolingo Max: no price, no independent evaluation, and the "Explain My Answer" description is unconfirmed on the official page.
- Math Academy: no explicit Math Academy or Skycak statement about LLM tutors was found in reachable sources; the only AI statement is the page above.
- Photomath: whether Plus explanations use an LLM is not stated; no accuracy data for any photo-solve app beyond one informal test.
- 豆包 learning features, 九章, 讯飞星火 devices and 作业帮 AI were not re-researched beyond the earlier notes and the items above; no independent accuracy test of the Chinese tutors was found.
- Chegg figures are headline-level only.

## Q2. What do rigorous studies say about learning outcomes of AI tutors and general AI use, including harms? (design type, sample size and effect size for each)

### Takeaway
The 2024-2026 evidence is two-sided and depends on design. Unguarded or answer-giving AI produced practice gains and learning losses in the best-identified studies (Turkey RCT: exam -17% for the plain GPT-4 arm; Maryland RCT of 2,379 students: -0.37 SD final grades; Chinese panel of 26,811 students: closed-book exams -20% after six months). Tightly grounded, hint-only, often supervised tutors gave large short-run gains in lab-like or supervised settings (Harvard physics 0.73-1.3 SD; Nigeria 0.31 SD; Eedi/DeepMind transfer +5.5 points over human tutors, exploratory), while the optional-chat deployment at school scale gave about 0.04 SD (Khanmigo two-year trial). Delayed-retention evidence is nearly absent, the most-cited positive meta-analysis was retracted in 2026, and no rigorous study of photo-solve apps was found.

### Cited Findings

#### M. Calibrators to read first
- **M1 Tutoring at scale shrinks (human tutoring).** [Meta-analysis, Peer-reviewed, abstract only] Kraft, Schueler and Falken (2026) pool 282 RCTs (the page also says 265): 0.42 SD across math and reading (reading 0.436, math 0.385). In studies resembling large-scale US programs aimed at standardized tests, pooled effects are "only a third to a half as large," driven by "stark declines... as tutoring program scale increases." — [Stanford NSSA summary](https://nssa.stanford.edu/studies/what-impacts-should-we-expect-tutoring-scale-exploring-meta-analytic-generalizability)
- **M2 The most-cited positive ChatGPT meta-analysis was retracted.** [Retracted] Wang and Fan (Humanities & Social Sciences Communications, May 2025; 51 studies; g = 0.867 on learning performance, about 0.456 on perception and higher-order thinking) was retracted in May 2026 over "discrepancies in the meta-analysis"; critics said the forest plot supports roughly 0.5, a retracted study was among those included, studies were mislabeled or too small, and weighting was wrong; the paper had more than 500 citations. — [Plagiarism Today (Jonathan Bailey), 2026-07-15](https://www.plagiarismtoday.com/2026/07/15/study-claiming-ai-helps-students-learn-retracted/); critique of the same paper: [Shadowrunners Substack](https://shadowrunners.substack.com/p/a-criticism-of-the-recent-chatgpt) (pooled incommensurable outcomes such as test results and reading time; control groups usually undescribed; none of the studies blinded)
- **M3 A larger LLM meta-analysis is positive but heterogeneous.** [Preprint] Huang et al. (arXiv, Sept 2025): 133 experimental and quasi-experimental studies, 188 effect sizes; Hedges g = 0.751 for "qualification" (learning achievement; k = 123; I² = 88%), 0.745 socialisation (k = 11), 0.654 subjectification (k = 54). Mathematics g = 0.342 with a confidence interval spanning zero [-0.236, 0.919]; science 1.058; 78.2% of studies from Asia, 81.2% in higher education, 87.2% ChatGPT only; mathematics only 3.76% of studies; publication-bias section not read. — [arXiv 2509.22725](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.22725v1)
- **M4 A separate 2026 meta-analysis exists (Nature family, 35 experimental studies) but its numbers could not be retrieved.** — [title seen in search, page failed](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-07019-z)

#### H. Harm or no benefit when AI substitutes for the student's own work
- **H1 Bastani et al., PNAS 2025.** [RCT, Peer-reviewed; numbers from the working-paper PDF] Nearly 1,000 students (grades 9-11, one large Turkish high school, about 50 classes, fall 2023-24), classroom-level randomization, pre-registered; four 90-minute sessions, each an AI-assisted practice phase then a closed-book, closed-laptop exam. Arms: control (textbook and notes), GPT Base (ChatGPT-like GPT-4 interface), GPT Tutor (GPT-4 with teacher-written solutions and common student mistakes in the prompt, incremental hints, "show your work first"). Practice scores (control mean 0.284): GPT Base +0.137 (+48%), GPT Tutor +0.361 (+127%), both p < 0.01. Exam without AI (control mean 0.321): GPT Base -0.054 (SE 0.022, p < 0.05; -17%; about -0.19 SD, derived), GPT Tutor -0.004 (not significant); Tutor vs Base +0.028, p = 0.019. Students in the Base arm did not think they had learned less, and Tutor-arm students believed they did significantly better on the exam ("overly optimistic"). When GPT-4 was asked each of 57 practice problems 10 times it was correct on average 51% of the time (logic errors 42%, arithmetic errors 8%). Caveats: five class sessions missed their assigned treatment (intention-to-treat used); honors classes excluded; single school and country; mechanism analysis "suggestive." — [PNAS record and DOI 10.1073/pnas.2422633122, published 2025-06-25](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/generative-ai-without-guardrails-can-harm-learning-evidence-from-high-school-mathematics); [full-text PDF](https://nmdprojects.net/teaching_resources/bastani_generative_ai_can_harm_learning.pdf)
- **H2 University of Maryland course-integrated tutor, Fall 2025.** [RCT, Working paper] 2,379 undergraduates and 30 instructors, randomized by instructor within 13 course blocks (exact-match sample: 22 instructors, 1,353 students; 34 section clusters). A GPT-4o "Virtual Study Assistant" using retrieval over instructor-selected course materials; instructors could choose direct-instruction or tutor mode and only two ended the term in tutor mode. Final grades -4.12 points (SE 1.80; -0.37 SD) in the exact-match sample (-2.88, marginal, in the full sample); LMS participation -0.90 SD; active days -0.51 SD; first-generation students -0.50 SD (full sample) versus -0.22 SD for others. Only about 15% of offered students used it; of 3,460 coded requests 73.8% were direct-answer, 11.3% practice or test prep, 0.7% feedback on the student's own attempt. Authors' caveats: course grades not independent assessments, low uptake, other AI tools freely available, 18% survey response, tutor mode rarely used, so results concern the tool "as configured, not scaffolded tutoring." — [IZA DP 18989, Oct 2026](https://docs.iza.org/dp18989.pdf); [EdTech Innovation Hub](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/students-offered-an-ai-tutor-got-lower-grades-and-disengaged-from-course-materials-university-of-maryland-trial-finds)
- **H3 The Generative AI Learning Penalty (China).** [Quasi-exp, Working paper] Strömberg, Lei and Wu (CEPR DP 21577, June 2026): 26,811 students in grades 7-12 in one county in central China, 30 months of administrative records across nine subjects, staggered difference-in-differences (Callaway-Sant'Anna), adopters and non-adopters on similar pre-trends; about 80% reported using generative AI by June 2025. After adoption homework time fell from 64 to about 45 minutes and homework scores rose 18%, while monthly closed-book exam scores fell 20% after six months, and entrance exams fell 24% (high-school) and 18% (college) when adoption was two or more years earlier. By subject: social science -27%, STEM -22% (math not reported separately), English -17%, Chinese -9%. Losses were largest for junior, male and initially high-performing students; students who finished homework in under 50 minutes did markedly worse, while those who spent about as long as non-users had similar exam scores; the penalty grew over time. Adoption was self-selected, and the most-used tools were general chatbots (Doubao, DeepSeek). — [VoxEU column by the authors](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/generative-ai-learning-penalty-secondary-school). Education Next (secondary) adds that about half of students reached "full homework outsourcing" after roughly five months and reports 1.4 SD (monthly tests), 1.5 SD (high-school entrance) and 1.3 SD (college entrance), and says the penalty fell from about 25% in early 2023 to about 16% by June 2025; it also describes adoption timing as read from device registrations, whereas the authors' column says teacher-distributed surveys asked students for registration dates, and it rounds the sample to "about 27,000" — [Education Next](https://www.educationnext.org/the-penalty-students-pay-for-using-ai/)
- **H4 Lehmann, Cornelius and Sting, "AI Meets the Classroom."** [Observational field + Lab RCTs, Preprint] Field study of two graduate Python courses at a Dutch university (113 students, 6,594 student-question observations; LLM use proxied by similarity of code to ChatGPT output; ChatGPT outages as instruments) plus pre-registered lab experiments (107 and 69 subjects, 45-minute Python lessons, ChatGPT gpt-3.5-turbo vs none). No effect of LLM access on overall lab learning. Asking for solutions raised volume but lowered post-test scores (-0.195, and -0.312 holding learning-phase progress constant); asking for explanations did not change post-test but raised understanding (+0.249, p about 0.05); each unit of cumulative ChatGPT similarity was associated with lower later grades (-0.06 instrumented); low-prior-knowledge subjects understood less with the LLM. Small samples, one lesson, one model. — [arXiv 2409.09047](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.09047)
- **H5 "Beware of metacognitive laziness" (Fan et al.).** [Lab RCT, Preprint; published version in a journal not confirmed here] 117 university students (all English-as-second-language) in four groups (no support 30, ChatGPT 35, human expert 25, checklist tool 27), a two-stage reading and writing task. Essay-score improvement favored ChatGPT (F = 4.549, p = 0.005, η² = 0.108); knowledge gain (post-test p = 0.438) and knowledge transfer (p = 0.996, η² = 0.000) showed no differences; motivation did not differ. Authors flag short duration, small sample, a single task and no direct measure of metacognitive laziness. — [arXiv 2412.09315](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.09315)
- **H6 ChatGPT in an introductory programming task.** [Lab, Preprint, pseudo-random assignment] 55 analyzed undergraduates (29 ChatGPT-4.5, 26 search-only), three C tasks in 30 minutes. Performance 0.89 vs 0.69 (Hedges g = -1.24, favoring AI); retention immediately after 0.41 vs 0.53 (g = 0.71 favoring no-AI) and at 48 hours 0.39 vs 0.52 (g = 0.77); self-attributed ownership 44.6 vs 80.8 (g = 1.05). 15 of 29 AI students pasted the task and accepted the answer. Only 12 of 55 were women; physiological measures were inconclusive. — [arXiv 2609.21194](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.21194)
- **H7 "Fast and Forgettable."** [Quasi-exp, Preprint, n = 22] Novices wrote Python with GitHub Copilot or with a human partner (20 minutes each) and were retested a week later: performance better with Copilot, a larger retest decrement in the AI condition (retest difference not significant), reduced workload; no effect sizes on the page. — [Stanford SCALE repository entry](https://scale.stanford.edu/ai/repository/fast-and-forgettable-controlled-study-novices-performance-learning-workload-and)
- **H8 LLM summaries vs web search (Melumad and Yun, PNAS Nexus, January 2026).** [Lab, Peer-reviewed] Experiment 1: 1,104 participants; Experiment 2: 1,979 (the authors report seven experiments; the summary page describes four). Learning a topic through ChatGPT-style summaries rather than web links produced shallower knowledge, less time and effort, lower felt ownership of the advice written, and sparser, less original advice. Paid participants, hypothetical advising tasks. — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/learning-from-ai-summaries-leads-to-shallower-knowledge-than-web-search/)
- **H9 "Your Brain on ChatGPT" (Kosmyna et al.).** [Lab, Preprint, not shown to be peer-reviewed] 54 participants in three groups (LLM, search engine, brain-only) over three sessions, 18 in a fourth crossover session; EEG, essay analysis, teacher and AI scoring. LLM users showed the weakest brain connectivity, lowest self-reported essay ownership and struggled to quote their own work; the LLM-to-brain group showed under-engagement. Small and single-task. — [MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/your-brain-on-chatgpt/)
- **H10 Grades up, traditional skill possibly down (Israel).** [Quasi-exp, Working paper] Hausman, Weisburd and Rigbi (CEPR DP 20206): student-course data from a top Israeli university, courses more vs less AI-compatible before and after ChatGPT; AI availability raised grades, especially for lower-performing students, compressed the grade distribution, and "evidence suggests gains in AI-specific human capital but possible losses in traditional human capital." The page gives no sample size or magnitudes. — [CEPR DP 20206](https://cepr.org/publications/dp20206)
- **H11 Generative-AI use and exam scores.** [Observational, abstract only] Wecks et al.: students identified by AI-detection tools as GenAI users scored on average 6.71 (out of 100) points lower than non-users, with the effect "particularly harmful for students with high learning potential"; sample size not on the page. — [arXiv 2404.19699](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19699)

#### B. Benefit when the tutor is grounded, structured or supervised
- **B1 Harvard physics (Kestin et al.).** [RCT crossover, Preprint version read; published in Scientific Reports 2025, not opened] 194 eligible students (233 enrolled) in Physical Sciences 2, fall 2023, two weekly lessons (surface tension and fluid flow) with the order switched; randomized by peer-instruction group. The AI tutor ("PS2 Pal", GPT-4) was prompted to be brief, reveal one step at a time and prompt students to try first, and each question was supplied with a step-by-step solution paralleling the in-class explanation; the platform walked students through multi-part problems because a system prompt alone could not reliably scaffold them. Post-test median 4.5 (AI) vs 3.5 (active learning) vs 2.75 pre-test (Mann-Whitney z = -5.6); standardized regression coefficient 0.63 and quantile-regression effect sizes 0.73 to 1.3 SD (authors say ceiling effects understate it); median time 49 minutes vs 60 in class; higher engagement (4.1 vs 3.6 of 5) and motivation (3.4 vs 3.1). Critiques: Harvard students, proctored sessions at home on Zoom, two short lessons, tutor given the correct solutions, no delayed test mentioned. — [Research Square preprint PDF](https://biblioteca.unisced.edu.mz/bitstream/123456789/3656/1/AI_Tutoring_Outperforms_Active_Learning.pdf); [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-ai-tutor-harvard-physics/); journal link seen at [GIGAZINE](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20250730-ai-tutor-physics-course-engagement/)
- **B2 World Bank Nigeria.** [RCT, Working paper (Policy Research WP 11125)] 657 first-year senior-secondary students (about age 15) in nine Benin City public schools assigned to treatment and 671 to control by individual randomization; only 422 treated and 337 control students took the final test (differential attrition). Six weeks (June-July 2024), twelve 90-minute after-school sessions of Microsoft Copilot (GPT-4) for English with curated teacher-provided prompts and lesson guides; teachers (one three-day training) kept students on task, helped them spot hallucinations and discouraged over-reliance. Intention-to-treat effects: overall weighted score 0.31 SD (SE 0.068), English 0.238 SD, AI knowledge 0.309, digital skills 0.139 (10% level), third-term regular English exam 0.206; dropping one school reduced the overall effect to 0.156 (10% level); girls gained more but may reflect one girls-only school. Cost about $48 per pupil for the pilot ($9 marginal); the authors equate the effect to 1.5 to 2 years of business-as-usual schooling and claim it beats 80% of rigorously evaluated interventions. Not isolated from the whole package; persistence untested; student-level randomization allowed spillovers. — [World Bank working paper PDF](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/cd9cca5b-2ffd-4be1-8313-0f4d2ed6ef4f/download); [publication record](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/15e1ff08-15ae-4f7a-b2a8-d146e6c113ee); two World Bank blogs by the team add that the evaluation results were still "soon to be published" when the first was written, that attendance was disrupted by flooding, teacher strikes and students' after-school work, that the authors think the design may have underestimated the impact, that the tool is "not a silver bullet", and that scaling would need significant investment — [From chalkboards to chatbots](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/From-chalkboards-to-chatbots-Transforming-learning-in-Nigeria); [Let's Talk Development](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/addressing-the-learning-crisis-with-generative-ai--lessons-from-)
- **B3 Rori (Ghana).** [School-level comparison, randomization not stated in abstract, Preprint] About 1,000 students in grades 3-9 across 11 schools, a WhatsApp math tutor used for two 30-minute sessions per week over 8 months on top of regular instruction; effect size 0.37 (p < 0.001), year 1 only, "judicious" interpretation advised. — [arXiv 2402.09809](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09809)
- **B4 Tutor CoPilot (Stanford).** [RCT, Working paper] Human-AI system that suggests expert moves to tutors during live chat tutoring. Students whose tutors had access were 4 percentage points more likely to master topics (p < 0.01), and 9 points more for students of lower-rated tutors; tutors used more guiding questions and gave away answers less often; cost about $20 per tutor per year; interviews flagged suggestions that were not grade-level appropriate. Sample conflict: the arXiv v3 abstract says 900 tutors and 1,800 students (550,000+ messages), while the Stanford NSSA page says more than 700 tutors and more than 1,000 students (350,000+ messages). — [Stanford NSSA](https://nssa.stanford.edu/studies/tutor-copilot-human-ai-approach-scaling-real-time-expertise); [arXiv 2410.03017](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03017)
- **B5 Eedi and Google DeepMind (LearnLM), UK.** [RCT, exploratory, Preprint, Dec 2025] 165 students aged 13-15 in five secondary schools plus 17 expert tutors, mathematics on the Eedi platform, May-June 2025; students randomized to static pre-written hints (control, N = 91) or tutoring (N = 74); tutoring sessions randomized to a human tutor or LearnLM (a Gemini 2.0 Flash fine-tune) drafting messages that a human tutor approved, edited or rewrote. Correct on second attempt: static hints 65.4%, human tutor 91.2%, supervised LearnLM 93.0%. Misconception resolved: 86.8%, 94.9%, 95.4%. Next-unit transfer: 56.2%, 60.7%, 66.2% (LearnLM minus human +5.5 points, 95% credible interval -1.4 to +12.4; 93.6% credibility of advantage). Safety audit of 3,617 drafted messages: zero harmful or risky content, five factual errors (0.1%), 25.6% edited or rewritten (44.3% of edits were about pacing). Grounding: each call carried a fixed system prompt requesting a concise Socratic reply that did not reveal the answer, populated with the question, the student's wrong answer, the platform-diagnosed misconception, and pre-written explanations. Limits: five schools, math only, session-level randomization, N = 27 survey responses, LearnLM sometimes kept asking Socratic questions past students' patience. — [arXiv 2512.23633](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23633v1)
- **B6 StudentBench (GRE).** [RCT, Preprint, Sept 2026] 2,383 adults in a paid study, one hour of assigned tutoring, 27-question pre- and post-tests; 13 AI tutors per section built on 12 models with minimal scaffolding (two prompts) and given the student's pre-test record with answer keys, versus nine human tutors per section (ex-ETS or Kaplan or 5+ years experience), versus a control watching unrelated videos. Pooled AI-minus-human learning gain -0.58 points (90% CI -2.18 to +1.03), equivalent within ±0.25 SD (p = .015); quantitative equivalent (p = .028), verbal not established (p = .085). Versus control: AI +6.15 points (95% CI 4.08 to 8.21), human +7.04 (3.88 to 10.20). Human sessions were few (61 quantitative, 79 verbal vs 1,139 and 1,000 AI sessions). Cost $0.0052 vs $4.81 per point gained (918x), $0.067 mean per session for Gemma 4 31B. Only immediate gains were measured; no practice-alone control. — [arXiv 2609.28470](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.28470)
- **B7 Fischer, Rau and Rilke (Berlin).** [RCT, Working paper] 334 analyzed students reading two economics textbook excerpts then a 25-item incentivized test; control textbook only (112), AI after initial reading (120), unrestricted AI (102), acemate GPT-4 retrieval tutor. Any AI +0.23 SD (p < 0.05); unrestricted vs control +0.34 SD (p < 0.01); restricted vs control +0.13 (not significant); unrestricted vs restricted +0.21 (p = 0.066). Benefits concentrated in below-median baseline scorers, strong book-readers and low procrastinators (heterogeneity suggestive, unadjusted). 25-minute lab session. — [IZA DP 18338](https://docs.iza.org/dp18338.pdf)
- **B8 Gallegos, "Guidance Over Adoption" (Chile).** [RCT, Working paper, March 2026] Econometrics course, 384 randomized students (analytic samples 303 and 289), a course-specific GPT assistant on the free ChatGPT interface. Emails encouraging adoption before the midterm raised awareness and use but not midterm scores (+0.053 SD, not significant). Emails giving guidance on learning-oriented use (tutor-style interaction, structured prompts, answer verification) before the final raised final-exam performance by 0.218 SD (p < 0.05; open-response items +4.99 points, multiple-choice not significant), shifted use toward tutor-style interaction and displaced time from readings and labs. Peer spillovers, self-reported usage, one course. — [IZA DP 18513](https://docs.iza.org/dp18513.pdf)
- **B9 ChatGPT-written hints vs human-written hints.** [Lab RCT, Peer-reviewed] Pardos and Bhandari: 274 Mechanical Turk adults, algebra and statistics problems, ChatGPT-3.5 hints vs tutor-written hints vs bottom-out-only control; both hint types beat control and did not differ from each other. ChatGPT hints initially had errors in about 32% of problems; generating ten hints and keeping the majority answer ("self-consistency") cut errors to near zero for algebra and about 13% for statistics. — [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/ai-math-tutor-chatgpt-can-be-as-effective-as-human-help-study-suggests/)

#### S. School-scale results with an optional AI tutor
- **S1 Khanmigo two-year cluster RCT (Hamilton County, Tennessee).** [RCT, Working paper, Aug 2026; EdWorkingPaper 26-1551, listed by SCALE as "One Click Away: AI Tutoring with Khanmigo in a Two-Year School Experiment" (NBER, 08/2026) — [SCALE repository](https://scale.stanford.edu/genai/repository/impact-randomized-controlled-trial); the earlier notes cite it as NBER w35620, which was not re-checked here] 18 middle schools, 53 grade-within-school clusters (28 treated, 25 control), 6,902 student-term observations; Khan Academy practice with Khanmigo during daily 25-40 minute remedial blocks. Control was business-as-usual remedial math, mostly other adaptive software (Waggle, IXL, Zearn, DeltaMath), not Khan without Khanmigo. Intent-to-treat on NWEA MAP math: +1.216 national percentile ranks (SE 0.568), 0.039 population SD (SE 0.018; 0.049 control SD); by school year (stacked) Year 1 0.020 (not significant), Year 2 0.084 (SE 0.041), stacked estimate 0.062 (10% level); implied 0.142 SD for participants in Year 2; only 53 clusters, borderline under wild-cluster bootstrap (p = 0.051). Usage in Year 2: 96% messaged Khanmigo at least once, but only 17% of mistake sessions contained a message (9,362 of 56,362) and messages were bare answers 39.4%, suggested-prompt clicks 24.2%, low-effort 12.6%, reasoning or math questions 14.5%, off-task 9.3%. Authors: gains come "mainly from structured, individually targeted practice rather than conversational tutoring"; "the binding constraint appears to be engagement." Correction to the earlier notes, which call the effect "similar to" and "equal to Khan practice without AI": the paper says its "resembles Khan practice without AI" claim relies on comparison with prior observational estimates (Eames et al., 2026), not a randomized arm, and that the design cannot isolate the tutor because assignment moved all program components together. — [EdWorkingPaper 26-1551](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1551.pdf)
- **S2 Making AI tutoring productive (mastery-based practice).** [RCT, Working paper, Aug 2026; NBER w35621] More than 6,000 Hamilton County middle-school students on the NUMI practice platform randomized on three factors: AI vs practice-only support, mastery vs non-mastery progression, and two math topics; delayed test one week later. AI students progressed more slowly and attempted fewer questions but were more accurate once they attempted, and after mistakes needed fewer attempts to return to a correct answer. Mastery raised three-in-a-row attainment but "did not by itself improve delayed learning"; the best delayed-test evidence was AI combined with mastery, only marginally significant and concentrated on practiced material in one exercise; no effect sizes on the page. Authors (Oreopoulos, Liut, Sungu, Low): LLM tutoring adds value "only with structure that turns mistakes into learning opportunities." — [NBER w35621](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35621); [EdWorkingPaper 26-1552](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai26-1552)
- **S3 OpenAI's own RCT** (over 300 college students; microeconomics about +15% relative; neuroscience not distinguishable; vendor, unpublished): see Q1.

#### P. Policy syntheses
- **Brookings (January 2026).** A report from the Brookings Institution concluded that the risks of AI in education currently outweigh the benefits for young people, relying on interviews with students, parents and teachers in 50 countries, with cognitive offloading as the mechanism; it admits "little rigorous, longitudinal evidence"; recommendations include AI "that teaches rather than simply tells answers, with vetted content." — [The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/four-takeaways-from-new-report-on-ais-risks-in-education/)

#### F. Photo-solve apps and homework copying
- No randomized or quasi-experimental study of Photomath, Gauth, Mathway, Question.AI or Brainly on learning outcomes was found; a search for Photomath studies returned only small studies in Indonesian and Thai journals (titles only, not read). The closest evidence concerns general chatbots used for homework: H3 (homework scores +18% while exams -20%), H2 (73.8% direct-answer requests) and H10.
- A Times Higher Education report that exam answers were found on Chegg for half of one team's modules was seen as a headline only. — [THE](https://timeshighereducation.com/news/researchers-find-exam-answers-half-their-modules-chegg) (headline from a search result; page not opened)

### Inferences
- **Pattern.** Across H1-H11 and B1-B9, harm tracks substitution (answer-giving, solution-seeking, homework outsourcing) and benefit tracks grounding plus structure plus supervision. The same model (GPT-4) produced -17% on the exam as a plain chatbot and no loss when given teacher solutions and told to hint (H1), and 0.73-1.3 SD when given solutions and a one-step-at-a-time instruction (B1).
- **Performance is not learning.** In H1 the practice arms rose 48% and 127%; in H3 homework scores rose 18% while closed-book scores fell 20%; in H5 the essays improved with no knowledge gain; in H6 task performance rose with worse retention. Any metric that counts AI-assisted success as skill would repeat this error.
- **Retention is the missing measurement.** The positive RCTs measured immediate or at most one-week outcomes (B1, B6, B7, S2: one week), while the one long-run panel shows a penalty that builds over about two years (H3). Positive effects should be read as short-run.
- **Effect-size calibration.** The school-scale AI trial gave 0.04 SD pooled intent-to-treat (0.06 stacked per school year, 0.08 in Year 2) (S1), consistent with human tutoring effects shrinking to a third to a half at scale (M1). Plan on 0.05 to 0.3 SD for any AI add-on, not 1 SD.
- **Engagement dominates.** Optional chat is mostly unused or used for answers (S1: 17% of mistake sessions; H2: 15% uptake and 0.7% feedback requests); defaults matter (H2: most instructors left direct-instruction mode on). In-flow structure after mistakes is the lever that moved outcomes (S2).
- **Heterogeneity conflicts.** Who benefits is unresolved: B7 found gains in below-median students; H4 found low-prior-knowledge students understood less; H3 found the largest losses among initially high performers.
- **No evidence that an LLM layer adds learning on top of well-built mastery practice.** S2 is the only test of that combination and found marginal delayed gains concentrated on one exercise; S1 cannot isolate the tutor.

- The 2026 Nature-family meta-analysis of 35 studies could not be retrieved; no independent meta-analysis of LLM tutoring that survives scrutiny was found (M2 retracted, M3 a preprint with I² around 85-88%).
- The Stanford SCALE repository lists many Aug-Oct 2026 AI-tutoring RCTs by title only (e.g., "When AI Tutors Speak", practitioner-led micro-randomised trials on GCSE science, "Learning To Prompt"); they were not read — [SCALE repository](https://scale.stanford.edu/genai/repository/impact-randomized-controlled-trial).
- No delayed retention beyond one week in any positive AI-tutor RCT; no LLM-tutor RCT with calculus-level outcomes; no study of photo-solve apps.

## Q3. Which design features separate helpful from harmful AI tutoring? (what the trials that helped share, and what the ones that hurt lack)

### Takeaway
Six design choices recur. The tutors that helped, or at least did no harm, were grounded in a verified solution or a diagnosed misconception, withheld the answer and revealed steps one at a time, sat inside a structure that triggers help after a mistake, and usually had a teacher, a curated prompt set or a human approving messages. The tutors that hurt were open chat with the answer a keystroke away, used by students who mostly asked for answers. Prompt wording is a weak guardrail, the default setting decides what most users get, and no trial isolates one feature, so the attribution below comes from bundles (see Gaps).

### Cited Findings

#### D1. The model is grounded in verified solutions or diagnosed misconceptions
- Same model, different prompt (H1). The GPT Tutor arm got teacher-designed solutions and common student mistakes in the prompt, was told to give incremental hints and not the full solution, and asked students to show their work first; the GPT Base arm had none of that. Exam without AI: Tutor -0.004 (not significant) vs Base -0.054 (p < 0.05); Tutor minus Base +0.028 (p = 0.019). Neither arm beat the control on the exam. — [Bastani PDF](https://nmdprojects.net/teaching_resources/bastani_generative_ai_can_harm_learning.pdf)
- Harvard (B1). Each question carried a step-by-step solution that paralleled the in-class explanation; the tutor was told to be brief and give away one step at a time; the authors say a system prompt alone could not reliably scaffold multi-part problems, so the platform guided students through each part in sequence. — [Kestin preprint](https://biblioteca.unisced.edu.mz/bitstream/123456789/3656/1/AI_Tutoring_Outperforms_Active_Learning.pdf)
- Eedi/DeepMind (B5). Every call carried the question, the student's wrong answer, the platform-diagnosed misconception and pre-written explanations, and a human approved or edited each message: 5 factual errors in 3,617 drafted messages (0.1%) and no harmful content. — [arXiv 2512.23633](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23633v1)
- StudentBench (B6). The AI tutors were handed each student's pre-test record with answer keys. — [arXiv 2609.28470](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.28470)
- Grounding in unverified model output is weaker: the Imperial College feedback assistant used no instructor solutions and relied on exemplary solutions precomputed by a reasoning model (o1-mini) and not edited by instructors; "All models had complete failure cases" in its stress tests. — [Gohr et al., arXiv 2601.03458](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.03458)
- Khanmigo does numeric work with a calculator instead of the model's predictions, and Khan says it "occasionally makes mistakes", including when judging whether student work is right. — [Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/why-were-deeply-invested-in-making-ai-better-at-math-tutoring-and-what-weve-been-up-to-lately/)
- Expertise is not pedagogy: Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct reached 0.88 problem-solving accuracy but only a 0.06 win rate on scaffolding generation; LearnLM-1.5-Pro was the most balanced (0.94 solving, 0.75 solution-correctness F1, 0.64 scaffolding win rate) and the only model to hold up on longer dialogues. — [MathTutorBench, arXiv 2502.18940](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.18940)
- A vendor case-study page is titled "AI Math Tutors: 99% Accuracy with Solution Injection"; it was seen only as a search-result title, so no method, baseline or sample size is known. — [AE Studio](https://ae.studio/projects/case-studies/ai-math-tutor-solution-injection-accuracy)

#### D2. The answer is withheld and steps are revealed incrementally; how students use the tool matters more than access
- Asking for solutions lowered post-test scores (-0.195; -0.312 holding learning-phase progress constant) while asking for explanations raised understanding (+0.249). — [Lehmann et al., arXiv 2409.09047](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.09047)
- At Maryland 73.8% of coded requests were direct-answer and 0.7% asked for feedback on the student's own attempt; instructors could choose direct instruction or a tutoring mode and most kept the direct-instruction default (only two ended the term in tutor mode). — [IZA DP 18989](https://docs.iza.org/dp18989.pdf); [EdTech Innovation Hub](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/students-offered-an-ai-tutor-got-lower-grades-and-disengaged-from-course-materials-university-of-maryland-trial-finds)
- Anthropic's own usage data: about 47% of university-student conversations were "Direct" (seeking answers with minimal engagement). — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-education-report-how-university-students-use-claude)
- In a programming lab, 15 of 29 AI-assisted students pasted the task into ChatGPT and accepted the answer. — [arXiv 2609.21194](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.21194)
- Answer leakage: in Gupta et al.'s dialogues the LLM tutors could be coaxed into revealing answers through yes/no questions (4 times). — [arXiv 2503.16460](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16460)
- Counter-evidence on restriction: in a 25-minute lab study a "restricted" arm (10 minutes of text only, then 15 with AI) did not beat unrestricted AI access (+0.13 SD, not significant, vs +0.34 SD against control; unrestricted minus restricted +0.21, p = 0.066), and the authors' hypothesis that restriction would help was rejected. — [IZA DP 18338](https://docs.iza.org/dp18338.pdf)
- Counter-evidence on Socratic-only: LearnLM sometimes persisted with Socratic questions longer than students' patience. — [arXiv 2512.23633](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23633v1)
- Hint wording: ChatGPT-3.5 hints and tutor-written hints both beat bottom-out-only hints and did not differ from each other. — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/ai-math-tutor-chatgpt-can-be-as-effective-as-human-help-study-suggests/)

#### D3. Who triggers help: structure after a mistake versus optional chat
- Khanmigo two-year trial (S1). 96% of students messaged it at least once, but only 17% of mistake sessions (9,362 of 56,362) contained a message and 39.4% of messages were bare answers; the authors credit "structured, individually targeted practice rather than conversational tutoring" and call engagement the binding constraint. — [EdWorkingPaper 26-1551](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1551.pdf)
- NUMI field experiment (S2). LLM tutoring can add value over standard computer-assisted learning "but only with structure that turns mistakes into learning opportunities"; AI students attempted fewer questions but were more accurate and needed fewer attempts after a mistake; mastery progression alone did not improve delayed learning. — [NBER w35621](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35621)
- Maryland. Only 14.6% of offered students used the assistant at all, and LMS participation fell 0.90 SD in the exact-match sample. — [IZA DP 18989](https://docs.iza.org/dp18989.pdf)
- Khan's May 2026 A/B tests [Carried from the earlier notes]: a summary of recent attempts gave +3.4% next-item correctness, surfacing unmastered prerequisites +2.7%, and example problems had no measurable effect (vendor-run). — [Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/?p=21314)

#### D4. A human or a curated frame sits around the model
- Tutor CoPilot: students of tutors with access were 4 percentage points more likely to master topics (p < 0.01) and 9 points more for students of lower-rated tutors, at about $20 per tutor per year; interviews flagged suggestions that were not grade-level appropriate. — [Stanford NSSA](https://nssa.stanford.edu/studies/tutor-copilot-human-ai-approach-scaling-real-time-expertise)
- Eedi: 74.4% of drafted messages were accepted unchanged (the abstract says 76.4% had zero or minimal edits), 25.6% were edited or rewritten, and 44.3% of the edits concerned pacing. — [arXiv 2512.23633](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23633v1)
- Nigeria: teachers introduced each topic, kept students on task, helped them understand feedback (including spotting AI "hallucinations") and worked to prevent over-reliance; each session opened with a teacher-provided prompt and the lesson guides positioned the model as a tutor; teachers did not give direct instruction. — [World Bank blog](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/addressing-the-learning-crisis-with-generative-ai--lessons-from-); [working paper PDF](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/cd9cca5b-2ffd-4be1-8313-0f4d2ed6ef4f/download)
- Imperial's assistant added a content-moderation step and limited queries per student in its test deployment. — [arXiv 2601.03458](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.03458)

#### D5. Students are told how to use it
- Emails encouraging adoption did not raise midterm scores (+0.053 SD, not significant); emails giving guidance on learning-oriented use (tutor-style interaction, structured prompts, verifying answers) raised the final exam by 0.218 SD (p < 0.05), driven by open-response items (+4.99 points). — [IZA DP 18513](https://docs.iza.org/dp18513.pdf)

#### D6. Metacognitive side effects
- GPT Base students did not think they had learned less, and GPT Tutor students believed they did significantly better on the exam than they did ("overly optimistic"). — [Bastani PDF](https://nmdprojects.net/teaching_resources/bastani_generative_ai_can_harm_learning.pdf)
- Essays improved with ChatGPT help but knowledge gain and transfer did not differ from controls. — [arXiv 2412.09315](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.09315)
- AI-assisted programmers reported much lower ownership of their work (self-attributed ownership 44.6 vs 80.8, g = 1.05). — [arXiv 2609.21194](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.21194)
- OECD conference summary (secondary): generative AI removes the "productive friction" that learning requires. — [Reda Sadki summary](https://redasadki.me/2026/03/24/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026-how-can-ai-help-human-beings-learn-and-grow/)

### Inferences
- **Most of the helpful bundle is content and structure, not conversation.** Of the features above, only free-form natural-language follow-up needs a model at run time. Verified step-tagged solutions, misconception-keyed feedback, attempt-first flow, incremental hints, help triggered by a mistake and prerequisite surfacing are data and rules.

| Feature that helped | Evidence | Already in the Toishi spec? | Needs a runtime model? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Verified step-tagged solution available to the helper | H1 Tutor arm, B1, B5, B6 | Yes: provenance rules and step-tagged solutions ([spec.md L458-481](/docs/spec.md)) | No |
| Misconception-keyed feedback | B5 | Yes for known wrong answers, which show their feedback and preselect the step ([spec.md L262](/docs/spec.md)); the long tail is uncovered | No for the known list; yes only for the long tail |
| Attempt first, hints one rung at a time | B1, H1 Tutor, S1 | Yes: "Not quite" and nothing else on a first miss, two attempts, hint ladder with rating penalties ([spec.md L246-250](/docs/spec.md)) | No |
| Help triggered by a mistake, in flow | S1, S2, Khan A/B | Yes: the "What went wrong?" picker and check problems ([spec.md L262-267](/docs/spec.md)) | No |
| Prerequisite surfacing | Khan A/B | Yes: a miss starts a prerequisite check ([spec.md L252, L266](/docs/spec.md)) | No |
| Human or verified content frame | B4, B5 | Partly: per-provenance acceptance rules (blind solves, independent code or CAS checks, audits) and dropped disputed problems ([spec.md L458-469](/docs/spec.md)) | No |
| Guidance on how to use outside AI | B8, H4 | No | No |
| Free-form follow-up in natural language | B5 (supervised), B1 | No | Yes |

- **The best math-specific delayed-test result for a grounded tutor is a null.** H1's Tutor arm removed the harm but did not beat the textbook control on the exam (-0.004), while it raised practice scores by 127%. The positive results (B1, B5, B2) are against active-learning classes or static hints, in other subjects, or short horizon, so grounding looks necessary to avoid harm but not shown sufficient to produce retention gains in math.
- **Guardrails belong in code, not in the prompt.** Kestin's own team moved multi-part sequencing into the platform because the system prompt was unreliable, OpenAI concedes inconsistent study-mode behavior (Q1), and the Maryland default decided most instructors' choice.
- **Withholding is not free.** The Fischer result and Eedi's patience finding mean a strict never-give-the-answer rule can backfire; a design that escalates from hint to explanation after a bounded number of exchanges is more defensible than a hard refusal. This is an inference; no trial tested escalation rules.

### Gaps
- No trial isolates any single feature; every positive result is a bundle (grounding plus hints plus structure plus supervision), so the attribution is inferential.
- No randomized comparison of default settings (the Maryland instructors chose their mode).
- No head-to-head of question-form versus statement-form hints, or of escalation rules.
- No delayed-retention data beyond one week for any structured tutor; the single math-specific delayed null (H1) cannot be generalized.
- Tutor CoPilot sample sizes conflict between sources (900 tutors and 1,800 students on arXiv v3; more than 700 and more than 1,000 on the Stanford page).

## Q4. How accurate and safe are LLM tutors on math, and how do verified systems guard against errors?

### Takeaway
Every audit of ungrounded LLM math tutoring found material error rates: GPT-4 was right on about half of repeated practice queries, only 56.6% of tutoring dialogues were entirely correct, first-error localization ran about 20 to 65% depending on setup, and LLM feedback tracked grader scores only as well as two human graders track each other. Systems that report low error rates move correctness out of the model (injected solutions, calculators or solvers, majority voting, human approval; Eedi reached 0.1% factual errors with grounding plus human review). The audited models are 2023-25 vintage and no independent late-2026 audit of frontier models was found, so the numbers bound the risk and do not describe today's best models.

### Cited Findings

#### Raw-model audits of math tutoring
- GPT-4 was correct on average 51% of the time when each of 57 practice problems was queried 10 times (logic errors 42%, arithmetic errors 8%). — [Bastani PDF](https://nmdprojects.net/teaching_resources/bastani_generative_ai_can_harm_learning.pdf)
- Gupta et al. (AIED 2025), college algebra: tutor-based evaluation of 110 problems gave 85.5% correct final answers overall (GPT-3.5 Turbo 77.3%, GPT-4 74.5%, GPT-4o 97.3%, o1-mini 91.8%, o1-preview 85.5%); in 150 interactive tutoring dialogues final answers were 88.6% correct, 90.0% of dialogues were high quality but only 56.6% were fully correct (GPT-4o best at 75%); the authors estimate about 1 in 18 problems gets a wrong final answer, report correct final answers with flawed sub-steps, and note that "newer models often performed worse than older ones" (GPT-4o was the best model in both parts); they conclude LLMs are "not yet suitable as intelligent tutors for math without human oversight". — [arXiv 2503.16460](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16460)
- ChatGPT-3.5 hints had an initial error in about 32% of problems; generating ten hints and keeping the majority answer (self-consistency) cut errors to near zero for algebra and about 13% for statistics, with learning gains equal to human-written hints. — [PsyPost summary of Pardos & Bhandari](https://www.psypost.org/ai-math-tutor-chatgpt-can-be-as-effective-as-human-help-study-suggests/)

#### Error localization and grading
- MathTutorBench: mistake-location micro-F1 from 0.29 to 0.57 (LearnLM 0.57, LLaMA3.1-70B 0.56, Qwen-Math 0.47, GPT-4o 0.37); solution-correctness F1 up to 0.75 (LearnLM); the math specialist Qwen-Math scored 0.43 on solution correctness. — [arXiv 2502.18940](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.18940)
- First-error localization in student solutions (six models, none a dedicated reasoning model): without a reference 42.5 to 54.9% on grade-school word problems (random 18.3%) and 19.6 to 42.5% on harder MATH-style solutions (random 9.5%); with the gold solution up to 64.1% and 49.7%; the math specialist Qwen2.5-72B-Math solved problems well but localized poorly, and on the word problems its accuracy fell from 45.0% to 30.4% when it was given the gold solution; problem-solving ability correlated only weakly with localization (phi below 0.2). — [arXiv 2509.01395](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01395v1)
- Imperial College feedback assistant, undergraduate mathematics, pure LLM with no CAS or proof verifier: the two human graders correlated 0.491 on 65 solutions; the best LLM configurations reached worst-case correlations of 0.572 (gpt-4.1) and 0.521 (o4-mini) when graded by other LLMs, the weakest were near zero (-0.010 to 0.157); in stress tests (five questions, three models) "All models had complete failure cases" and gpt-4.1 failed all five; limitations include grades inferred from feedback by other LLMs and no direct expert evaluation of the feedback. — [arXiv 2601.03458](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.03458)
- Gupta et al.: the second-LLM grader used in their pipeline was itself wrong in 25 cases (6.3% of responses). — [arXiv 2503.16460](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16460)
- Photos of handwritten work (transcription errors in 38 to 81% of real homework solutions on 2025-era models, first-wrong-step localization 43 to 67%, error-cause classification 40 to 47% against about 80% for experts, but 88 to 91% rubric-check accuracy on one private October 2026 benchmark) are covered in `diagnosis_feedback_mistakes.md` Q7 and not repeated here. — [sibling note](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Toishi%20full%20evidence%20review/diagnosis_feedback_mistakes.md)

#### What deployed systems do about it
- Khanmigo: a calculator for numerical problems and an instruction to write out every way the student may have reached the answer; Khan still says it "occasionally makes mistakes" and publishes no error rates. — [Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/why-were-deeply-invested-in-making-ai-better-at-math-tutoring-and-what-weve-been-up-to-lately/)
- Photomath: a neural network reads the image and a separate problem-solving algorithm (an "expert system") produces the steps; no accuracy statement. — [Google Photomath help](https://support.google.com/photomath/answer/14328660?hl=en)
- Eedi/DeepMind: grounding plus a human approving every message gave 5 factual errors in 3,617 messages. — [arXiv 2512.23633](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23633v1)
- Duolingo Max: humans write the Roleplay scenarios and opening messages, the company says it reviews AI content, and learners can report a bad response. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-max)
- ChatGPT study mode: OpenAI admits "inconsistent behavior and mistakes across conversations". — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-study-mode/)
- Informal photo-solve test (September 2025, not peer reviewed): all apps solved basic integration by parts, Question.AI and Zuoyebang were wrong on a trig integral, Doubao produced 31 unrelated steps, Gauth solved it. — [ChinaTalk](https://www.chinatalk.media/p/cheating-apps-chinas-latest-tech)

#### Safety beyond correctness
- Students over-trust: GPT Tutor students were over-optimistic about their exam performance (see D6). — [Bastani PDF](https://nmdprojects.net/teaching_resources/bastani_generative_ai_can_harm_learning.pdf)
- Eedi's audit found zero harmful or risky content in 3,617 drafted messages. — [arXiv 2512.23633](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23633v1)
- Brainly's store listing claims "100% kid-safe" monitoring (vendor claim, no evidence cited). — [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/745089947)
- Gemini API terms require users to be 18 or older and bar use in applications "directed towards or likely to be accessed by individuals under the age of 18". — [Gemini API terms](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms)

### Inferences
- **Correctness must live in code, explanation in the model.** Every low-error system above (Eedi, Khanmigo's calculator, Photomath's solver, Pardos's voting) takes the correctness decision away from free generation. Grading and key-making should stay with the CAS and the provenance rules; a model should only ever phrase what the verified solution already says.
- **The app already owns the verified artifacts a grounded prompt needs.** Every key must pass the acceptance rule for its provenance (blind model solves, independent code or CAS solves, back-substitution and audits; [spec.md L458-469](/docs/spec.md)), and solutions are stored as tagged steps ([spec.md L473](/docs/spec.md)). That is comparable to the teacher-written solutions in H1 and B1 and stronger than the unedited model-precomputed solutions in the Imperial system.
- **The weak point of any optional helper is the question that goes beyond the card.** Once a student asks something the stored solution does not cover, the model improvises, and the audits say improvised math is wrong a meaningful share of the time. Mitigations that exist in the literature or follow from it: instruct the model to defer to the supplied solution, label output as AI-generated with a Report button, and (design idea, no evidence found) run a CAS check on any numeric or symbolic claim it makes.
- **Self-consistency voting is a build-time tool for this project, not a chat feature.** Ten samples per reply multiplies cost and latency; the content pipeline already runs blind solves, which is where voting belongs.
- **Treat the error rates as a ceiling on trust, not a forecast.** They come from GPT-3.5 to o1-era models; frontier 2026 models are likely better, but the Imperial stress test (gpt-5 and o4-mini included) still found complete failures, and localization was never re-measured on reasoning models.

### Gaps
- No independent late-2026 audit of tutoring-dialogue correctness for current frontier models; no first-error localization results for dedicated reasoning models.
- No evaluation of CAS-in-the-loop verification of LLM explanations (the design idea above is untested).
- Photomath, Mathway, Gauth and Question.AI publish no accuracy data; the only comparison found is one informal journalistic test.
- Eedi's 0.1% error rate is for a grounded, human-supervised, single-subject deployment and cannot be transferred to unsupervised use.
- The AE Studio "99%" claim could not be assessed (title only).

Short grounded threads are cheap: by my estimate about $0.002 per five-turn thread on the cheapest tiers and about $0.03 on mid-tier models, or roughly $0.25 to $5 a month at five threads a day, and BYOK costs the project nothing. That gives 1,100 + 1,600 + 2,100 + 2,600 + 3,100 = 10,500 input tokens and 1,250 output tokens per thread, before caching and before any hidden reasoning tokens.

- Sensitivity: if hidden reasoning tokens made output five times larger (an arbitrary multiplier), a Sonnet-class thread would cost about $0.083 (about $12.50 a month). Prompt caching of the repeated context would lower the input side; it is not modeled. At the assumed usage a cheap tier costs the student about a fifth of Khanmigo's license per student-year ($15) and a mid tier about four times as much.
- **Money is not the binding constraint; policy and architecture are.** The project pays nothing under BYOK, but the student's thread leaves the machine, the app stops being network-free, and the offline tripwire and two "Not in 1.0" exclusions would need an explicit decision record. A separately built, off-by-default helper (its own binary or build flag) would keep the default app verifiably offline; this is a design suggestion, not a researched practice.
- **Free-tier keys are the privacy trap.** On Gemini's unpaid tier prompts may be read by human reviewers and used for product improvement; the app cannot tell which tier a key belongs to, so it can only instruct users to use a paid-tier key and warn them. OpenAI and Anthropic defaults (30-day retention, no training use by default on OpenAI; Anthropic's training use not stated on the page read) are milder but retain content.

The OpenAI and Anthropic age rules were not read. An 18+ acknowledgement and a no-identifiers rule are the minimum.
- **The payload can be small and non-personal.** The card is an open-source problem with an official or verified solution, so what leaves the machine is the card id or text, the student's typed answer and their question; a "what will be sent" preview lets the student see it, and free text is the only place personal data could slip in.
- **The Apple on-device model is the only option that keeps the app offline, key-free and cost-free**, but nothing found says how well it does math, the SDK is pre-1.0 and framed around evaluating Swift app features, and it needs macOS 26 and Apple Intelligence. Because the app owns a bank of CAS-verified problems, it could be benchmarked on that bank before any student sees it (design suggestion).

### Gaps
- OpenAI and Anthropic age and minors policies for API use were not read; COPPA, FERPA and GDPR obligations for a distributed app where the user supplies the key were not researched.
- Anthropic's retention page does not state whether API data is used for training; the OpenAI data page was only read through 100,000 of 128,880 characters and the Gemini pricing page through 100,000 of 143,326.
- Keychain storage practice, free-tier rate limits and the providers' terms on automated use of a user-supplied key were not researched.
- Reasoning-token overhead on each provider is unquantified; list prices and model names are a November 2026 snapshot and will change.
- Apple on-device model size, context length and math quality are unknown; no benchmark of it on math was found.
- No data on how much a student would actually use an optional feature in a self-study app; the closest numbers are Khanmigo's 17% of mistake sessions and Maryland's 14.6% uptake, both in school and course settings.

## Q6. What should a free, offline practice app do about AI? (a) the no-runtime-AI stance, (b) optional BYOK features, (c) what to copy without AI, (d) what to avoid

### Takeaway
(a) The evidence supports keeping AI out of the core loop: the harms come from substitution, the benefits come from content and structure the app already has, optional chat is mostly ignored, and the one math-specific delayed test of a grounded tutor was a null; the stance costs most in explaining the long tail of wrong answers and in follow-up questions. (b) If anything is offered, the best-supported candidate is a post-submission "ask about this step" thread grounded in the card's verified solution, off by default, on a paid-tier key, hint-first and counted as a hint, with a cheaper zero-network alternative of exporting a grounded prompt. (c) Most of what works in AI tutors can be copied as rules and data. (d) Avoid answer-first UX, default-on chat, ungrounded math, an LLM as grader or key source, counting AI-assisted success as mastery, free-tier keys, and claims that rest on vendor numbers or the retracted meta-analysis.

### Cited Findings
- Project scope: no network, accounts, telemetry or update checks, and no model calls at run time are listed under "Not in 1.0". — [spec.md L64-69](/docs/spec.md)
- The study loop is attempt-first: the student works on paper and types only the answer; a first miss shows "Not quite" and nothing else; two attempts by default; Shift+Enter submits as "unsure"; hints come from the card's ladder, any hint caps the rating at Hard and the last hint (the first step) counts as Again; every miss opens a "What went wrong?" picker over the numbered worked solution, and known wrong answers show their feedback with the step preselected; find-the-error problems are drawn from known common mistakes. — [spec.md L228-262](/docs/spec.md)
- AI is already used outside the app: the content pipeline drafts skills and tags with a model, every key must pass the acceptance rule for its provenance (blind model solves, independent code or CAS checks, audits), tags are spot-checked to at least 85% main-tag precision, and a ready tagging prompt is published with each course so students can tag imported decks with their own AI tool; untagged imported problems stay "unverified" until checked. — [spec.md L46, L454-481](/docs/spec.md)
- The backlog gates any recognition feature behind a spike of offline open-source math OCR models. — [backlog.md L188-194](/docs/backlog.md)
- Handwriting and photo evidence is in the sibling note: cloud models are decent at rubric checks but weak at transcription of imperfect work and at locating the first wrong step. — [diagnosis_feedback_mistakes.md Q7](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Toishi%20full%20evidence%20review/diagnosis_feedback_mistakes.md)
- The Khanmigo two-year trial's control was business-as-usual remedial math, so the earlier notes' description of its effect as "similar to" or "equal to Khan practice without AI" is not a randomized comparison (the paper says so itself). — [EdWorkingPaper 26-1551](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1551.pdf); earlier notes in [practice_platforms.md](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Practice%20systems%20and%20learning%20science/practice_platforms.md)
- Evidence anchors used below (full detail in Q2 to Q5): harm without guardrails ([Bastani PDF](https://nmdprojects.net/teaching_resources/bastani_generative_ai_can_harm_learning.pdf), [IZA DP 18989](https://docs.iza.org/dp18989.pdf), [CEPR/VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/generative-ai-learning-penalty-secondary-school)); grounded or structured benefit ([Kestin preprint](https://biblioteca.unisced.edu.mz/bitstream/123456789/3656/1/AI_Tutoring_Outperforms_Active_Learning.pdf), [arXiv 2512.23633](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23633v1), [NBER w35621](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35621)); small effects at scale ([EdWorkingPaper 26-1551](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1551.pdf)); guidance on use ([IZA DP 18513](https://docs.iza.org/dp18513.pdf)); retracted meta-analysis ([Plagiarism Today](https://www.plagiarismtoday.com/2026/07/15/study-claiming-ai-helps-students-learn-retracted/)).

### Inferences

**(a) Does the evidence support the no-runtime-AI stance, and where does it cost us?**
- *Supports it.*
  - Harm tracks substitution. The plain chatbot cost 17% on the later exam (H1), the Maryland assistant lowered final grades 0.37 SD while 73.8% of requests were for answers (H2), and the Chinese panel's closed-book scores fell 20% while homework scores rose 18% (H3). The app is built the other way round: attempt first, hints penalized in the rating, answer after the attempt.
  - The helpful bundle is mostly content and structure the spec already has (Q3 table). The conversational layer is the only piece that needs a model, and no trial isolates its contribution.
  - Optional chat is largely ignored: 17% of mistake sessions at Khanmigo's scale, 14.6% uptake and 0.7% feedback-on-attempt requests at Maryland. The authors of the Khanmigo trial attribute the gains to structured practice.
  - The expected upside is small and uncertain. The school-scale AI add-on gave 0.039 SD pooled (0.062 stacked per school year, 0.084 in Year 2) (S1), human tutoring effects shrink to a third to a half at scale (M1), and the only test of AI on top of mastery practice found marginal delayed gains concentrated on practiced material (S2). The best math-specific delayed result for a grounded tutor is a null (H1).
  - Ungrounded model math is unreliable (Q4), and every positive result is short-run while the one long-run panel shows a penalty that builds (H3).
- *Costs of the stance.*
  1. Wrong answers outside the known-wrong-answer list get only the worked solution. Tutoring on the wrong answer lifted second-attempt correctness from 65.4% (static hints) to 91.2% (human) and 93.0% (supervised LearnLM) and next-unit transfer from 56.2% to 60.7% and 66.2% (B5), so the loss could be real, but it is exploratory, human-supervised, N = 165, and the control was static hints and not a full worked solution with a step picker.
  2. No follow-up Q&A for conceptual confusion. The spec routes this to textbook references and worked examples per skill ([spec.md L453](/docs/spec.md)); structured guidance on using outside AI helped adults in one course (B8), so a static guide recovers part of the value.
  3. No help with free-response or proof grading and no step-level handwriting diagnosis. Pure-LLM feedback matched human-human agreement at best and had complete failure cases (Q4), and handwriting reading is error-prone (sibling note), so this cost is small.
  4. Students will use outside AI anyway: about 80% adoption in the Chinese county (H3) and about 47% "Direct" use in Anthropic's data. The app cannot stop this but can make it less harmful (see (c)).
- *What would change this verdict:* a field trial with a delayed test of at least four weeks showing that a grounded post-submission explanation raises next-item correctness or seven-day redo in a spaced mastery app. None exists.

**(b) Which AI capabilities are worth offering as optional, off-by-default, bring-your-own-key features?** Ranked by evidence and risk:
1. **"Ask about this step", available only after a first submission or Give up.** Grounded in the card's verified step-tagged solution, the student's answer and the engine's diagnosis (including known-wrong-answer text and the step picked), as in H1's Tutor arm, B1 and B5. Design rules:
   - Hint-first by default, with bounded escalation to a plain explanation (Fischer and Eedi show strict withholding can backfire).
   - Counts as a hint for scheduling: when it reveals a step it is the last rung and the rating is Again, per the existing hint rule ([spec.md L250](/docs/spec.md)); it never changes grading or the key.
   - Follow it with a fresh variant of the skill, because the evidence for benefit is about next-item performance (Eedi transfer, Khan's metric), not about the explanation itself.
   - Label output "AI-generated, may be wrong" with Report, and (design idea, untested) run a CAS check on any numeric or symbolic claim the model makes.
   - Evidence strength: moderate for grounded hinting under supervision, weak for unsupervised follow-up chat, none for retention.
2. **Grounded rephrasing of the worked solution** (simpler wording, another worked angle). Low correctness risk because the verified solution is the source; the nearest evidence is a vendor lab study (60 students, p = 0.03 on immediate and three-day tests) that cannot say which component helped. Weak.
3. **Feedback on a one-sentence self-explanation against a rubric.** Experimental; no learning-effect evidence for it was found in this review, and LLM feedback has complete failure cases (Q4), so feedback only, never a grade.
4. **Offline-pipeline helpers** (tagging, key checking, misconception drafting). Already external to the app and student-facing risk is nil; the pipeline's blind-solve and CAS rules are the guard.
- *Preconditions for any of 1 to 3:* BYOK only; a paid-tier key (instruct and warn; the app cannot verify); key stored in the macOS Keychain (not researched); an exact "what will be sent" preview; no identifiers; a per-day cap with a cost estimate shown; hidden when offline; an 18+ acknowledgement; a separately built component or flag so the default app stays verifiably network-free; and a decision record amending the two "Not in 1.0" lines and the offline tripwire.
- *Evaluation plan if built:* micro-randomize whether the offer appears on each eligible miss, and compare next-item correctness on the fresh variant and seven-day redo, with local logs only (the spec forbids telemetry). With a single learner this has little power and can only catch gross harm, so set a kill rule in advance (for example, switch it off if next-item correctness falls). Micro-randomised trials are the method used in a September 2026 AI-tutoring paper listed by SCALE ([SCALE repository](https://scale.stanford.edu/genai/repository/impact-randomized-controlled-trial); title only seen).
- *Not recommended:* photo-of-work grading at run time, run-time problem generation (it would skip the blind-solve and code-solve acceptance rules), open-ended chat and companion personas.
- *Zero-network alternative worth considering first:* a "copy a grounded prompt" button (see (c)).

**(c) Which AI-tutor features can be copied without AI?**
- Attempt-first, one-rung hints and a penalty for hint use, already specified ([spec.md L246-250](/docs/spec.md)); evidence: B1, H1 Tutor, S1.
- Misconception-keyed feedback, extended at build time: have a model plus the CAS enumerate likely wrong answers and the misconception each reveals, accept an entry only if the CAS reproduces the wrong answer from the stated misconception, and feed Reports and logged wrong answers back into the list. This is Eedi's grounding applied to content instead of a live reply; the pipeline pattern exists in the spec ([spec.md L454-481](/docs/spec.md)). Design idea.
- Help triggered by a mistake and tied to the step ("What went wrong?", check problems, prerequisite checks), already specified; evidence: S1, S2, Khan's prerequisite result.
- Hints written as questions where they fit, since question-form Socratic prompting is what the learning modes imitate; ChatGPT-written and human-written hints worked equally well (B9), so authoring effort can use model drafts that a human and the CAS check. Design idea; no direct test of question-form against statement-form hints.
- A short recap of the student's recent attempts on the skill before the next item (Khan's A/B gave +3.4% next-item correctness; vendor-run, carried). Whether the spec already shows one was not checked.
- Show calibration back to the student (confidence against outcomes), because tutored students were over-optimistic (H1). The spec has "unsure" submissions and calibration logging ([spec.md L245, L849](/docs/spec.md)); whether calibration is displayed to the student was not checked.
- An "I used outside help" flag that downgrades the rating like a hint, since success with AI help is performance, not learning (H1, H3, H6). Design idea consistent with the hint rule.
- A one-page static guide to using outside AI with the app: try first, paste your attempt, ask for hints before explanations, ask it to check against the official solution, verify numbers in the app, and log the help. Evidence: guidance raised final-exam scores 0.218 SD where encouragement did not (B8); explanation-seeking beat solution-seeking (H4).
- A "copy a grounded prompt" button, shown only after a submission: it places the problem, the student's answer, the verified solution and a tutor-style instruction on the clipboard for whichever AI the student already uses. It gives the grounding and guidance that the evidence favors with no network call, no key, no cost to the project and no privacy surface in the app, and it follows the existing precedent of a published tagging prompt for outside AI tools ([spec.md L456](/docs/spec.md)). Limits: the outside model's compliance is a soft guardrail (study mode is admitted to be inconsistent), the student leaves the app, and there is no direct test of this bridge. Design idea.
- Next-item correctness and seven-day redo as the internal measures of whether any help works (the metrics used by Khan and Eedi).

**(d) Which AI-tutor features should be deliberately avoided?**
- Answer-first interfaces (Gauth shows the answer first) and photo-solve as a core loop.
- Open chat that is on by default or whose default is direct instruction (Maryland).
- Ungrounded model math anywhere a student could take it as correct (51% and 56.6% in the audits).
- An LLM as grader, answer-key source or sole error localizer (localization 20 to 65%; Imperial complete failures); the CAS and provenance rules stay in charge.
- Counting AI-assisted success as mastery or letting an AI explanation count as a clean pass.
- Free-tier API keys, telemetry, and any payload with identifiers.
- Prompt-only guardrails; put sequencing and refusals in code, as Kestin's team had to.
- Overclaiming from vendor numbers (Google's unquantified "educators preferred", OpenAI's relative +15% in one subject, Learn Your Way's small lab study) or from the retracted Wang and Fan meta-analysis or the heterogeneous Huang preprint (math g = 0.342, not significant).
- Treating optional chat as the thing that moves outcomes: the Khanmigo authors credit structured practice.

### Gaps
- No study tests an LLM add-on on top of FSRS-style spaced mastery practice, in a free self-study app, with adult self-directed learners; the evidence is from classes, labs and paid online samples.
- No grounded-tutor result with a delayed test beyond one week; the one math-specific delayed result (H1) is a null.
- The estimated cost of the stance (the share of wrong answers outside the known-wrong-answer list) is unknown for this app's content; it should be measured from logs.
- Items marked "design idea" (CAS check of model claims, escalation rules, question-form hints, calibration display, outside-help flag, grounded-prompt export, build-time misconception enumeration) have no direct evidence found; they are inferences from the studies cited.
- Self-explanation prompts and their effects belong to the learning-science notes and were not researched here.
- Not researched: the CS50 duck, Alpha School, independent accuracy of the Chinese tutors, and any randomized study of photo-solve apps (none found).
