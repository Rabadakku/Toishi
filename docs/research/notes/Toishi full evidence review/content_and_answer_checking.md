## 1. How do production graders decide that two math answers are equal, and what is documented about their false accepts and false rejects?

### Takeaway
**What we do.** A six-stage checker (spec "Math system"): shape, exact canonical match, numeric comparison at 12 points per variable at 30 digits inside the domain with at least one point per connected piece ("an answer undefined where the key is defined is wrong"), symbolic confirmation in a worker killed at 2 s that "can confirm a match but never overrule a numeric mismatch", an optional form check, then correct, incorrect or undecidable (undecidable falls back to self-grading and is logged). Own Lark grammar with no eval (ADR-0003, MATH-8); MathLive LaTeX lowered to the plain syntax (ADR-0005, MATH-4/5); "read as" preview and syntax errors that cost no attempt (MATH-6, card-format "Two guarantees"); equations by STACK's ratio test. Quality gates: MATH-11 (at least 600 golden cases, asserted in `tests/grader/test_golden.py`), MATH-12 (property tests), MATH-13 (every key grades itself correct, every `wrong` answer incorrect), MATH-14 (every confirmed dispute becomes a golden test first), MATH-15 (95% under 300 ms, 2 s hard limit). ADR-0007 records an independent 1,514-case attack that found 74 false accepts (4.9%, [OWN CALC]) and the fixes.

**What others do.** Production systems use one of two engines or both: random-point numeric evaluation (WeBWorK, Numbas, the Beevers-style systems Sangwin cites) or CAS simplification (STACK on Maxima, PrairieLearn on SymPy, Maple, Mathematica). The vendors' own documents state that the probabilistic path has one-sided error and that zero-testing is undecidable in general. None of them publishes a false-accept or false-reject rate. The only measured figures found are from a 100-pair test by the authors of a competing method (symbolic-only comparison in Maple and Mathematica rejected 70% and 84% of the correct answers, with no false accepts), which is weak evidence. Public trackers show recurring failure classes: floating-point errors in significant-figure counting, assumptions not honored, variable names ignored, nondeterministic test points, and constant-fitting false accepts.

**Verdict.** The pipeline matches the field's consensus design (numeric evaluation as the workhorse, CAS as a secondary confirmer, validity separate from correctness) and is deliberately stricter than STACK and WeBWorK on holes (ADR-0007). The evidence for its correctness is internal (golden suite plus one attack round); there is no external benchmark to compare 74/1,514 against. The residual risks are (1) numeric-only "correct" verdicts whose symbolic confirmation was cut short, (2) false rejects caused by strictness (holes, input syntax, a 0.1% default tolerance), and (3) having had only one independent attack round.

### Cited Findings

The paper gives no mis-marking, false-positive or false-negative figures for STACK. ### Inferences
- The strictness choices in ADR-0007 are not standard practice. WeBWorK's default `checkUndefinedPoints = 0` (earlier note) and STACK's AlgEquiv both treat `(x^2-1)/(x-1)` and `x+1` as equal, so there is no production precedent for "holes are wrong" and no external evidence on how often it causes false rejects. It is a policy bet whose cost is measurable only from the app's own disputes.
- Recommendation R1.1, measure two different rates. (a) Adversarial false-accept rate by attack class on a held-out set that is never used for fixing. The 1,514 cases that found 74 failures are now in the golden suite and no longer measure anything. (b) Natural-input false-reject rate: confirmed disputes (MATH-14) divided by incorrect verdicts, plus a monthly blind re-grade of 50 to 100 randomly chosen incorrect verdicts by a person or a second checker. Disputes alone undercount because many students do not dispute.
- Recommendation R1.2, run at least one more attack round before the first public pack, written without sight of the golden suite (the ADR-0007 round did exactly this and found 74). Retire each round into the golden suite and repeat until a fresh round of about 300 new cases finds zero false accepts; zero in 300 gives a 95% upper bound of about 1% by the rule of three [OWN CALC].
- Recommendation R1.3, log the deciding stage for every correct verdict and whether symbolic confirmation was skipped or timed out. ADR-0007 says differences below 1e-40 at every sample point "are found only by the symbolic step, which the 2 s hard kill can cut short (the earlier, numeric verdict then stands)". That is the one channel through which a real but tiny difference can be accepted. Re-run those verdicts offline with a long symbolic limit while the app is idle and tell the student if one flips. Maple's three-valued result (false always right, true possibly wrong, FAIL outside the class) is the precedent for carrying an "unconfirmed" state.
- Recommendation R1.4, add a differential oracle to CI only (never shipped): an independent route for the golden suite and the MATH-12 rewrite/perturbation pairs, for example mpmath interval evaluation or Maxima. SymPy currently does the template answers (`diff`, `solve`, `limit`, `integrate` in card-format Variants), the symbolic confirmation and the sampled evaluation, so a SymPy bug could be common to key and grader (see Section 12).
- Recommendation R1.5, count hole-driven rejects (message "undefined at x = ...") separately in stats. In [OWN RUN] they fired on ordinary rewrites such as multiplying by a conjugate (Section 2), so this class is the most likely source of natural false rejects.

### Gaps
- No published false-accept or false-reject rate for STACK, WeBWorK, Möbius, Maple TA, Numbas or PrairieLearn was found; Sangwin and Köcher (2016) could not be fetched, and a paper on the STACK item bank (Nakamura et al., eJMT) reports no accuracy data.
- Möbius and Maple TA grading internals were not re-fetched this session (the earlier note has Möbius defaults); no Mathematica-specific error rates were found beyond the documentation.
- No data on how often students' natural answers contain removable-singularity rewrites, so the cost of the hole rule cannot be estimated from literature.

## 2. Numeric sampling versus symbolic equivalence: what are the limits of each (domains, holes, branch cuts, floating point, large numbers), and how does our pipeline behave?

### Takeaway
**What we do.** Numeric first (12 points per variable inside the domain, region and critical-point sampling, 30 digits, mismatches rechecked at 60/120/240 digits with no absolute floors), symbolic confirmation second, exponent and size caps (MATH-10, ADR-0007). The card's symbols are real by default (MATH-9); `domain` states where expressions are compared.
**What others do.** Vendors document the same limits: Mathematica says zero-testing is undecidable and its default numeric path "may give incorrect positive answers"; Maple's `testeq` is one-sided; PrairieLearn's symbolic route times out and marks the answer invalid; Numbas and WeBWorK sample random points and accept when enough agree.
**Verdict.** For the failure classes the vendors document, the pipeline behaved correctly on every analog I could build inside its input limits [OWN RUN]. Its distinctive property is that domain semantics are one-directional (the answer must be defined wherever the key is), which removes false accepts and creates false rejects.

### Cited Findings
- Mathematica's `PossibleZeroQ` uses "basic symbolic and numerical methods" and states "The general problem of determining whether an expression has value zero is undecidable". By default it may use numeric approximations; "Approximate methods may give incorrect positive answers" (example: zero plus 10^-20000); it "may return True for nonzero numeric expressions that are close to zero"; `Method -> "ExactAlgebraics"` is provably correct for explicit algebraic numbers; `Sqrt[x^2] - x` is zero only under the assumption `Re[x] > 0`. [DOCS] — [PossibleZeroQ](https://reference.wolfram.com/language/ref/PossibleZeroQ.html)
- Maple's `testeq` "tests for equivalence probabilistically" (it cites Gonnet 1984): "The result false is always correct; the result true may be incorrect with very low probability" (no bound given); it returns FAIL when the expressions fall outside the supported class or when it "cannot find a workable modulus after seven trials". [DOCS] — [Maple testeq](https://www.maplesoft.com/support/help/maple/view.aspx?path=testeq)
- PEV bounds the false-positive probability for multivariate polynomials by d/|V| (degree over sample-set size) with no false negatives, and extends the same bound to general functions with d = max{2deg(f), 2deg(g)} + 1; points are sampled uniformly and independently. [PEER; the general-function extension is the authors' own argument] — [IJCAI 2013 paper](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/13/Papers/299.pdf)
- PrairieLearn `pl-symbolic-input` grades symbolically with SymPy, always simplifies, and has "no numeric evaluation or tolerance"; "SymPy's check runs for a few seconds before timing out" and timed-out answers, "correct or incorrect", are marked invalid with "Your answer did not converge, try a simpler expression."; names such as `e`, `pi`, `cos`, `sin` cannot be variables. [DOCS] — [pl-symbolic-input](https://docs.prairielearn.com/elements/pl-symbolic-input/)
- Numbas evaluates the student's and the correct answer "at random sample points for the free variables" and marks correct if they agree on enough points; the knobs are the checking type (absolute difference, relative difference, decimal points, significant figures), the number of points, the maximum number of failures and the sampling range; the page documents no defaults. [DOCS] — [Numbas mathematical expression part](https://docs.numbas.org.uk/en/latest/question/parts/mathematical-expression.html)
- STACK #1727 and the WeBWorK nondeterministic-points thread (Section 1) are the documented cases where context (assumptions, evaluation points) changes the verdict. [ISSUE] — [STACK #1727](https://github.com/maths/moodle-qtype_stack/issues/1727); [WeBWorK forum thread](https://webwork.maa.org/moodle/mod/forum/discuss.php?d=3508)

### Inferences
[OWN RUN] results, grouped by the failure class they probe (`battery.py`, `battery2.py`, `battery3.py`, `battery5.py`). In each line "key X versus Y" means the card's key is X and the student typed Y.

- Tiny differences, all graded incorrect as intended: key 1 versus `1+10^(-300)` and versus `1+10^(-45)`; key 2^1000 versus `2^1000+1`; key 10^100 versus `10^100+1` and versus `10^100+10^50`; key 0 versus `exp(-1000)` and versus `10^(-30)`; key x versus `x+exp(-1000*x^2)` and versus `x+sin(10^6*x)*10^(-12)`; key x^2 versus `x^2+10^(-50)*x`; key 2 versus `sqrt(2)*sqrt(2)+10^(-200)`.
- Decimal slack, by design: key 1 versus the decimal literal "1." followed by 44 zeros and a 1 (that is 1+10^-45 typed as a decimal) is graded correct, because a typed decimal gets the relative tolerance, while the exact expression `1+10^(-45)` is incorrect.
- Positive controls, graded correct: key 0 versus `sin(pi)`; key 1 versus `cos(1)^2+sin(1)^2`; key 5 versus `ln(exp(5))`; key 2 versus the Cardano form `(sqrt(108)+10)^(1/3)-(sqrt(108)-10)^(1/3)` (80 ms); key 2*sin(x)*cos(x) versus `sin(2*x)`; key (1-cos(2*x))/2 versus `sin(x)^2`.
- Size and denial-of-service guards: `9^9^9` is invalid ("Exponents are limited to 1000"); `10^1000` is accepted as input and graded incorrect against key 2; `((10^1000)^1000)^1000` and `sin(10^100)` come back undecidable ("The answer is too large to compare"); `100000!` is invalid (no factorial syntax); 1000 nested parentheses and a 3000-term sum are invalid (400-character limit).
- Real-domain semantics, as designed: key abs(x) accepts `sqrt(x^2)` and key x rejects it; key sqrt(x*y) rejects `sqrt(x)*sqrt(y)`, while key sqrt(x)*sqrt(y) accepts `sqrt(x*y)`; key x^x rejects `exp(x*ln(x))` but accepts it with `domain: x > 0`; key pi/2 rejects `arctan(x)+arctan(1/x)` but accepts it with `domain: x > 0`; key -2 accepts `(-8)^(1/3)` (real cube root).
- The hole rule is one-directional: key (x^2-1)/(x-1) accepts `x+1`, but key x+1 rejects `(x^2-1)/(x-1)` ("undefined at x = 1.0, where the correct answer is defined"); key 1/(x-1) rejects `x/(x^2-x)`, while key x/(x^2-x) accepts `1/(x-1)`; key tan(x) accepts `sin(x)/cos(x)`; key sin(x) rejects `tan(x)*cos(x)` (accepted with `domain: -1 < x < 1`); key 1 rejects `sec(x)^2-tan(x)^2`; the antiderivative key `ln(abs(x)) + C` rejects `ln(x)` and accepts `ln(abs(x))` and `ln(x^2)/2 + 7`.
- Natural-looking false rejects: key 1/(1-cos(x)) rejects `(1+cos(x))/sin(x)^2` ("undefined at x = -5*pi"); key (x+1)/(x-1) rejects `(x^2+x)/(x^2-x)` ("undefined at x = 0.0", run with `form: simplified`). A human marker would normally accept both.
- Direction matters for domains: key 2*ln(x) accepts `ln(x^2)` (the student's expression is defined on more points), while key ln(x^2) rejects `2*ln(x)`.

- Verdict detail: no absolute floors (ADR-0007) held for every analog of Mathematica's documented `zero + 10^-20000` failure that fits inside the 400-character and exponent-1000 limits. The only documented failure I could not probe is one that needs a difference smaller than 10^-1000.
- The domain rule makes the key's form part of its meaning: a key written in an unsimplified form with a hole (`(x^2-1)/(x-1)`) accepts the simplified answer, while a key in simplified form rejects any equivalent answer that carries an extra removable singularity. That is a consistent rule, but card authors must know it.
- Recommendation R2.1, make the importer print, for every expression/equation/antiderivative card, the domain the checker will use and the points where the key is undefined, next to the "read as" text in the authoring preview. Authors then see when a key would reject natural rewrites.
- Recommendation R2.2, show the student a specific message for hole-only rejects ("Your answer matches the key except at x = 1; rewrite it so it is defined there, or the card needs a restriction") and log the event as its own category (R1.5), so the rate can drive a decision on whether such answers should instead be accepted with a note.
- Recommendation R2.3, keep the three-valued outcome visible in logs (decided numerically, confirmed symbolically, unconfirmed) as in Maple's design (R1.3).
- Complex-valued answers and branch cuts were not probed: MATH-9 makes a card's symbols real by default and `i` the imaginary unit only when the card says so, so the real-domain probes above are the behavior that matters for Calculus I.

### Gaps
- No probe of complex answers, matrices with symbolic entries, or multi-variable domains with constraints; Calculus I does not need them, but linear algebra and differential equations packs will.
- The 10^-20000 style cases are outside the input limits (exponents are limited to 1000) and therefore untested.
- STACK's AlgEquiv and WeBWorK's comparison code were not read; claims about them rest on documentation and the earlier note.

## 3. Intervals, sets, equations, units, significant figures, "simplified" forms, exact versus decimal: what is best practice, and where does our design differ?

### Takeaway
**What we do.** Answer types and options in `docs/card-format.md`: intervals converted to canonical sets with unions merged and inequalities accepted; sets ignore order; equations by the ratio test; `tolerance` defaults to 0.1% relative; `exact: yes` rejects decimals; `form` options factored, expanded, simplified, lowest-terms, `decimal(n)` checked on the unsimplified answer; units and significant figures are specified now but graded only in version 1.1 with the Physics I pack (spec "Units and significant figures", the `quantity (1.1)` row, Pint in the stack table); in the current build a `quantity` answer returns status "unsupported". Syntax errors never cost an attempt.
**What others do.** STACK separates input validity from correctness and gives separate tests for form (FacForm, Expanded, LowestTerms, SingleFrac, PartFrac, CompSquare, listed in the earlier note) and for numeric format (NumRelative, NumAbsolute, NumDecPlaces, NumSigFigs, StrictSigFigs); PrairieLearn offers `relabs`, `sigfig`, `decdig` comparison modes with Pint units; Numbas offers absolute, relative, decimal-point and significant-figure checking; WeBWorK converts units with `NumberWithUnits`.
**Verdict.** Interval and set handling is strong and the form checks work for polynomial and fraction forms. Four weak spots: (1) `simplified` accepts unsimplified radicals; (2) scientific notation (`1.2e4`) is rejected because `e` is Euler's number, which will bite in the Physics pack; (3) an interval card with no declared variable accepts a condition in any variable name, even two different names joined by `and`; (4) units and significant figures are not built, and the documented failure of the best-known implementation (STACK #107) is exactly the kind of floating-point trap to avoid.

### Cited Findings
- STACK input options include "Forbid floats" (answers containing a float "will be rejected as invalid"), "Require lowest terms", "Check students answer's type" (sets, lists, equations, inequalities, matrices) and "Student must verify" (the input is shown back before scoring in a two-step validation); invalid input is not scored. [DOCS] — [STACK inputs](https://docs.stack-assessment.org/en/Authoring/Inputs/)
- Sangwin: "validity is a concept tied to the input. It is separate from the notion of an answer being correct"; teachers may reject floats or unreduced rational coefficients as invalid rather than wrong, "so students are not penalised on technicalities"; "an unambiguous informal syntax is impossible", so input options are tailorable. [DOCS/PEER] — [Sangwin, ICME-12 paper](https://www.mathunion.org/fileadmin/ICMI/Conferences/ICME/ICME12/www.icme12.org/upload/submission/1886_F.pdf)
- STACK `StrictSigFigs` "enforces the strict rules of significant figures", "does not check for numerical precision and looks only at surface features", and trailing zeros can make intent impossible to read from the form; `NumSigFigs` is "a more liberal test" that checks both the count and the accuracy, with `[n,0]` (count only) and `[n,-1]` (at least n) variants, and rounds the teacher's answer to the requested figures before comparing. [DOCS] — [STACK numerical tests](https://docs.stack-assessment.org/en/Authoring/Answer_Tests/Numerical/)
- STACK #107 (closed 2014-12-30): `NumSigFigs` rejected 0.1667 for a key of 0.1667 because Maxima's `floor(0.1667*10^4)` returned 1666; proposed fixes were a near-machine-precision correction or conversion to a bigfloat and then a rational; the maintainers asked for failing unit tests and the thread does not confirm the final fix. [ISSUE] — [STACK #107](https://github.com/maths/moodle-qtype_stack/issues/107)
- STACK's tracker also has "Problem with scientistic notation" (#148, 2015) and "units input: Ω (U+03A9) should be accepted as synonym for ohm" (#1739, closed 2026-05-03): notation and unit-synonym gaps get patched one report at a time. [ISSUE; titles read through the GitHub search API] — [STACK #148](https://github.com/maths/moodle-qtype_stack/issues/148); [STACK #1739](https://github.com/maths/moodle-qtype_stack/issues/1739)
- PrairieLearn `pl-number-input`: `comparison` defaults to `relabs` with `rtol` 1e-2 and `atol` 1e-8; `sigfig` and `decdig` modes use `digits` (default 2); `allow-fractions` defaults to true. `pl-units-input` uses Pint, defaults to a `with-units` mode that converts units automatically, offers `exact-units` and `only-units`, and has optional partial credit for correct magnitude with wrong units. [DOCS] — [pl-number-input](https://docs.prairielearn.com/elements/pl-number-input/); [pl-units-input](https://docs.prairielearn.com/elements/pl-units-input/)
- WeBWorK: `NumberWithUnits` accepts equivalent units (m/s for ft/s); the wiki page does not discuss tolerance or significant figures; old-style `num_cmp` cannot handle formulas with units. The PG tracker still lists open "Legacy NumberWithUnits issue" reports (#1301, #1311) and a `FormulaWithUnits` problem in the Fraction context (#1137). [DOCS/ISSUE] — [WeBWorK units page](https://webwork.maa.org/wiki/ProblemsWithUnits); [PG #1301](https://github.com/openwebwork/pg/issues/1301); [PG #1311](https://github.com/openwebwork/pg/issues/1311); [PG #1137](https://github.com/openwebwork/pg/issues/1137)
- Numbas offers four checking types (absolute difference, relative difference, rounded decimal places, rounded significant figures) for the comparison, with no defaults documented. [DOCS] — [Numbas mathematical expression part](https://docs.numbas.org.uk/en/latest/question/parts/mathematical-expression.html)

### Inferences
[OWN RUN] on the shipped grader (`battery.py` to `battery6.py`; "key X versus Y" means the card's key is X and the student typed Y):

- Intervals and sets, graded correct: key (-inf,2] versus `x <= 2`; key [1,3) U (3,5] versus `1 <= x <= 5 and x != 3` and versus `[1,3) ∪ (3,5]`; key (1,3) versus `1<x<3`; key [0,2] versus `0 <= x <= 2`; key [0,1] U [1,2] versus `[0,2]`; key (-inf,0) U (0,inf) versus `x < 0 or x > 0`; key `empty` versus `empty`; set {1,2} versus `{2,1}`; set -1,0,1 versus `1, 0, -1` and versus `-1, 0, 1, 1` (duplicates are ignored).
- Intervals, graded incorrect: key (1,3) versus `(1,3]` and versus `(1.0000000001, 3)`; key [0,2] versus `(0,2)`; key (0,1) U (1,2) versus `(0,2)`; key (-inf,0) U (0,inf) versus `(-inf, inf)`.
- Variable names in interval answers (`battery.py`, `battery6.py`): with no declared variable, key (1, inf) accepts `x > 1`, `t > 1` and even `x > 1 and y > 1` (two different names), while `x > 1 or t < 0` is incorrect; with `vars: sigma` declared, `sigma > 1` is correct and `x > 1` or `t > 1` are invalid ("t isn't a variable in this problem"). The loose case reproduces the symptom of STACK #1061 (variable name ignored); the declared case does not.
- Equations, graded correct: key y=2x+1 versus `y-2x=1` and `2y=4x+2`; key y=x^2 versus `x^2=y`. Graded incorrect: key y=2x+1 versus `y=2x+1.0000001`; key x^2+y^2=1 versus `y=sqrt(1-x^2)` ("Your equation is undefined where the correct one is defined"); key y=x^2 versus `y/x^2=1` (the message names the missing point (0, 0)); key x=4 versus `sqrt(x)=2` (the known ratio-test limit in ADR-0007).
- Entry syntax, invalid with a message and no attempt used: `sinx`, `sin 2x`, `sin2x`, `sin^2(x)`, `sin^2 x`, `sin^-1(x)`, `xe^x`, `x e^x`, `√2` ("sqrt needs parentheses"). Accepted: `2x^2` and `(2x)^2`; `1/2x`, which is read as x/2 (key x/2 accepts it, key 1/(2*x) rejects it); `e^2x`, which is read as (e^2)*x (graded incorrect against key e^(2*x), correct against key (e^2)*x).
- Precedence, standard: -2^2 is -4, 2^3^2 is 512, 16/2*4 is 32, 4/2/1 is 2.
- Characters accepted: π, ², ·, ×, the Unicode minus (U+2212) and ∪.
- Number notation: `1e100` and `1.2e4` are invalid ("A number can't follow 'e' without an operator"); `1.2*10^4`, `1.2×10^4` and `.5` are accepted; `1,000` is invalid ("Write 000 without leading zeros"); `0,5` is invalid ("Expected a number, not a list"); `50%` is invalid.
- Tolerance (default 0.1% relative on typed decimals): key 1/3 accepts `0.333` (exactly at the boundary) and `0.3333` and rejects `0.33`; key 2/3 accepts `0.667` and rejects `0.67` and `0.66`; key sqrt(2) accepts `1.414` and rejects `1.41`; key pi accepts `3.14` and `3.14159265` and rejects `3.1`; key 100 accepts `100.05` and rejects `100.2`; key pi rejects the exact fractions `22/7` and `355/113` (the tolerance applies to decimals, not to exact expressions); a card with `tolerance: 1e-9` is rejected as a card problem ("expected a number or a percentage, got '1e-9'").
- `exact` and `lowest-terms`: key sqrt(2)/2 with `exact` rejects `0.7071`; key 1/3 with `exact` rejects `0.3333` and accepts `2/6`; key 3/4 with `lowest-terms` rejects `6/8`, `0.75`, `-3/-4`, `1.5/2` and `1/2+1/4` and accepts `3/4` and `(3/4)`.
- `factored`: key (x+1)^2 rejects `x^2+2x+1` and `x*(x+2)+1` and accepts `(x+1)*(x+1)` and `1*(x+1)^2`; key (x-1)(x+1) rejects `x^2-1`.
- `expanded`: key x^2+2x+1 rejects `(x+1)^2` and accepts `x*x+2*x+1`, `1+2*x+x^2`, `x^2+x+x+1` and `x^2+2*x+1+0` (like terms need not be collected).
- `simplified`, radicals accepted although not simplified: key 2*sqrt(2) accepts `sqrt(8)` and `4/sqrt(2)`; key 2*sqrt(3) accepts `sqrt(12)` and `6/sqrt(3)`; key 3*sqrt(5) accepts `sqrt(45)`; key 5*sqrt(2)/2 accepts `5/sqrt(2)` and `sqrt(50)/2`; key sqrt(3)/3 accepts `1/sqrt(3)`; key sqrt(2)/2 accepts `1/sqrt(2)`; with no `form` set, key 2*sqrt(2) accepts `sqrt(8)` (expected).
- `simplified`, rejected as intended: key 2*sqrt(2) versus `sqrt(2)+sqrt(2)` and versus `2^(3/2)`; key sqrt(2) versus `2/sqrt(2)` (the ADR-0007 fix); key 2x versus `x+x`; key 1/2 versus `3/6`; key 7/3 versus `2+1/3`.
- Units: any `quantity` answer returns the status `unsupported` ("arrive in version 1.1").

- The `simplified` check (read in `core/grader/forms.py`, function `_simplified`) first rejects leftover like terms, repeated numeric factors, powers of 0 or 1, added zeros and fractions not in lowest terms; it then accepts any answer whose syntax tree has no more nodes than the largest of the key and SymPy's `simplify`, `factor`, `expand` and `cancel` rewrites of the key. A radical such as `sqrt(8)` for `2*sqrt(2)` is no larger than the key, so it passes. ADR-0007 fixed the case `2/sqrt(2)` for `sqrt(2)`; a size rule cannot see that a radicand has a square factor or that a radical sits in a denominator. The value is right, so this is not a false accept of a wrong answer, but it teaches that unsimplified radicals are fine on a card that asks for simplified form.
- The 0.1% default means a student who rounds 2/3 to two decimals (0.67, 0.5% off) is marked wrong unless the card says how to round. WeBWorK's default relative tolerance is also 0.1% and STACK's NumRelative default is 0.05 (both in the earlier note `answer_checking_and_verification.md`), and PrairieLearn's `rtol` default is 1e-2 (above), so defaults differ by a factor of up to 50 across systems and there is no consensus.
- Recommendation R3.1, replace the node-count test for radicals with named predicates (radicands squarefree, no radical in a denominator, fractions reduced), following STACK's separate property tests rather than one generic "simplified". Add the rows above to the golden suite as expected-reject cases.
- Recommendation R3.2, for version 1.1 compute significant figures with exact decimal arithmetic (Python `Decimal` on the typed string), never with a floating-point `floor(log10(...))`; STACK #107 is the documented failure. Golden cases: 0.1667 at 4 s.f., 0.1666, 0.167, 1.005 at 3 s.f., 9.80 at 2 and 3 s.f., 1.667e-1, 5 mS versus 5 ms, 20 degC versus 293 K, 980 cm/s^2 for 9.8 m/s^2.
- Recommendation R3.3, decide separately for every quantity card whether the count of figures matters (surface check, StrictSigFigs-like) or only accuracy (NumSigFigs-like); STACK ships both because each alone fails for some questions.
- Recommendation R3.4, accept E-notation (`1.2e4`, `3E-5`) in `quantity` answers, show "read as 1.2×10^4", and keep rejecting it elsewhere because `2e3` could mean 2000 or 2·e·3; improve the message ("Write 1.2*10^4") in both cases.
- Recommendation R3.5, add an importer warning when a `number` key is irrational or non-terminating, there is no `exact`, no `form: decimal(k)` and the question text has no "round" or "decimal places" instruction; that is where a 0.1% tolerance rejects reasonable roundings.
- Recommendation R3.6, for unit conversion handle offset units (degC), case-sensitive prefixes (mS vs ms), and symbol synonyms (Ω vs ohm), and derive the default tolerance from `sig-figs` (half a unit in the last place) instead of the 0.1% number default.
- Recommendation R3.7, require `vars` on every `interval` card that accepts inequality input (or reject any input with more than one free name), so that a wrong letter or `x > 1 and y > 1` cannot be accepted; add `t > 1` with `vars: sigma` and `x > 1 and y > 1` to the golden suite as expected-invalid cases. Also decide on purpose whether `ln(x)` for the antiderivative of 1/x (graded incorrect against `ln(abs(x)) + C`) is the intended outcome, because it is a very common answer and the rejection follows only from the hole rule.

### Gaps
- The STACK units page (`Authoring/Units/`) and Möbius unit handling were not retrievable this session; the earlier note's Section 7 covers physics units at a higher level.
- No evidence was found on how students react to strict versus lenient syntax handling in a self-study setting, or on how often a 0.1% default rejects reasonable roundings.
- Interval-notation conventions outside the US (for example ]1,3[) were not probed.

## 4. Parameterized (templated) problems: are variants equally difficult, does variation help learning, and how should parameter ranges be chosen?

### Takeaway
**What we do.** Template cards (card-format "Variants"): `vars` draw parameters, `let` helpers, `require` conditions, answers and `wrong` answers computed in code, tested on up to 200 instances, 50 to 200 shipped as data per template (PACK-3); parameter sets do not repeat within 180 days and a "repeated forms beat first-seen forms by 15 points over 30 or more pairs" notice looks for pools that teach patterns (ENG-5); a template answer must pass a sweep of 1,000 or more seeds (acceptance table).
**What others do.** Item-generation research distinguishes isomorphic clones from variants that differ in difficulty and treats cloning as risky unless a cognitive model fixes what varies; operational randomized-homework systems monitor variants after the fact.
**Verdict.** Code-computed answers are the strongest correctness guarantee available. Difficulty equivalence is not guaranteed: the evidence shows small average effects (a fraction of a percent on totals in one university, 2 to 5 points of exam-score swing in another) but real question-level differences, and recent work says solution path and calculation effort are the drivers. With one learner, per-template difficulty cannot be measured, so equivalence has to be designed in and checked offline.

### Cited Findings
- Wolfert, Neumann and Sommerhoff (2026, TMA 45(1)) propose a cross-content model in which two features of a task, the solution path and the calculation effort, explain difficulty differences between randomized variants, and test it with generalized linear mixed-effects models on 105 mathematics freshmen; they report that the findings "substantiate our hypotheses". The visible abstract gives no effect sizes, odds ratios or solution rates. [PEER; abstract only; WEAK on magnitude] — [Leibniz-IPN page](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/randomized-variants-of-tasks-in-mathematics-computer-aided-assessment-a-theoretical-model-and-empirical-validation); [OUP abstract](https://academic.oup.com/teamat/article-abstract/45/1/59/8443557)
- Jordan, Jordan and Jordan (2012, Open University): for most modules the effect of variants on students' overall scores is "very small (typically ±0.5%)", larger when more questions had significantly different variants, when there were fewer such questions overall, or when the e-assessment weighed more; the excerpt gives neither the number of questions analysed nor how many differed. [PEER; counts missing] — [Jordan et al.](https://oro.open.ac.uk/33705)
- The same group's method for checking equivalence of variants: facility index and standard deviation per variant, score-distribution plots, and a significance test of whether score probabilities are equal across variants (reject at p < 0.05). [REPORT] — [Jordan blog](https://www.open.ac.uk/blogs/SallyJordan/?p=90)
- Zilles et al. (SIGCSE 2022) on exam question pools in two CS courses: mean absolute deviation of a student's score across exam permutations was 4.65, 2.28 and 2.37 points in three semesters of a Python course (worst case about 5 points) and 2.77 in a data-structures course, with some programming exams up to 19 points; they attribute large swings to unbalanced pools, exams with few questions, and analogous questions with different difficulty. Student ability correlated 0.89 to 0.95 with exam score in the Python course. [PEER; exam-level fairness from IRT simulation, programming courses] — [Zilles et al.](https://zilles.web.engr.illinois.edu/papers/exam_variant_sigcse_2022.pdf)
- UGMathBench (randomized, WebWork-generated undergraduate problems, three versions each): o1-mini solved 56.3% of problems in all three versions (EAcc) against 68.00% average per-version accuracy (reasoning gap 11.70); gaps for top models range 9.95% to 16.67%; 19 of 300 versions (2.7%) in the error analysis were "bad problems" that no model solved. The paper says 5,062 problems in the text and 5,562 in a table. [PREPRINT; shows variant-to-variant differences for models, not students] — [UGMathBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.13766v2); [UGMathBench leaderboard page](https://alphaxiv.org/benchmarks/hkust/ugmathbench)
- Lathrop and Cheng (2017, Psychometrika): unaccounted variance in clone difficulties "propagates to other parameters in the model, causing specific and predictable patterns of bias"; person parameters are "largely unaffected by the choice of model". [PEER; simulation; abstract only] — [Lathrop and Cheng](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychometrika/article/abs/item-cloning-variation-and-the-impact-on-the-parameters-of-response-models/A5BF63CE245B62C14EB54C10B2998BF5)
- Item-family research (ETS): Enright et al. (1999) generated "two families of word problem variants" for GRE quantitative items and suggest variant information as collateral information to reduce pretest sample size; Sinharay, Johnson and Williamson (2003) introduce the family expected response function so that calibrating families "might be enough rather than calibrating each item". Neither abstract reports findings on within-family variability. [PEER; abstracts only] — [Enright et al. 1999](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/1999/ihdq.html); [Sinharay et al. 2003](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2003/hsgg.html)
- Gierl and Lai's NCME module: a 1-layer item model in their example yields 256 items and an n-layer model 16,384; 1-layer items "can look too similar" and are called "clones", "ghost" items or "Franken-items"; constraints are needed or some combinations "would not be sensible or useful"; field testing is "often not feasible when thousands of new items have been generated"; "strong theory" generation uses a cognitive model to specify the elements that drive difficulty; the module reports no empirical comparison of cloned and cognitive-model items. [DOCS/tutorial] — [Gierl and Lai module](https://ncme.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Module-34-Automated-Item-Generation-Gierl-Lai.pdf)
- Likourezos, Kalyuga and Sweller (2019): in two experiments with pre-university mathematics (103 and 56 adults), variability had a main effect regardless of guidance, but an expertise interaction in the second: more experienced learners learned more from high-variability tasks and less experienced learners learned more from low-variability tasks; the recommendation is to start with low variability and raise it as expertise grows. [PEER] — [Likourezos et al.](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-019-09462-8)
- Variation theory in mathematics teaching: the Cambridge Mathematics essay lists contrast, generalisation, separation and fusion and says "well-structured tasks are not sufficient on their own", while its own wording is hedged ("suggests", "may be"); a ZDM article reports a 1.5-year learning study with twelve teachers, gives no effect sizes and states the authors "cannot conclude whether the enacted aspects were actually critical for student learning". [WEAK] — [Cambridge Mathematics essay](https://www.cambridgemaths.org/Images/espresso_20_variation_theory_in_mathematics_education.pdf); [ZDM 2017 article](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11858-017-0858-4)

### Inferences
- Variant-to-variant difficulty differences are real but their average effect is small; the damage in this app is a less accurate "closest to 0.80" selection (ENG-5) and a noisy first-seen-versus-repeated metric, not a wrong key. The per-skill ability estimate absorbs template differences, in line with Lathrop and Cheng's finding that person parameters are barely affected.
- With one learner a template's observed success rate after n attempts has a 95% half-width of about 0.35 (n = 5), 0.23 (n = 12), 0.14 (n = 30) at a true rate of 0.8 [OWN CALC], so per-template difficulty cannot be estimated from the student's own data in a realistic time. Treating the 15-point first-seen-versus-repeated notice as a test of two independent groups of 30 (differences come in steps of 3.3 points, so "15 points or more" means at least 5 of 30, i.e. 16.7 points), its false-alarm rate is about 7% when both rates are 0.8 and 12% when both are 0.6, and its power to detect a true 0.95-versus-0.80 gap is about 49% [OWN CALC, independence assumed; exact binomial sums in `stats2.py`].
- Recommendation R4.1, add authoring and build rules that fix the two drivers inside a template. Solution path: all instances must go through the same sequence of method decisions, so split a template whenever a `require`-free parameter region changes the path (discriminant sign, a root that cancels, a factorable versus non-factorable case, a limit that needs L'Hôpital versus direct substitution). Calculation effort: bound integer and rational sizes and the number of arithmetic steps, and compute an effort score per instance (operation count of the solution, largest absolute intermediate value, number of sign cases) at build time. Flag instances whose effort is an outlier within the template (for example above the 90th percentile) and trim or re-range them. This operationalizes Wolfert et al.'s model and Gierl and Lai's "constraints"; the exact thresholds are mine and untested.
- Recommendation R4.2, tie parameter ranges to the tier: small positive integers and few sign cases for tiers 1 and 2, wider ranges, negatives and fractions at tiers 3 to 5, because novices learn more from low variability and the effect reverses with expertise (Likourezos et al.). Keep the same range across instances of one template so the difficulty is homogeneous.
- Recommendation R4.3, use the build-time difficulty probe in Section 6 (a model's solve rate or step count per instance) as an outlier detector for templates, not as a calibration.
- Recommendation R4.4, at the build, assert that every instance has the same answer type and the same answer-structure fingerprint (number of solutions, number of intervals) except where the template documents otherwise; the `require` conditions already exist for this.
- The 50 to 200 instance cap is not the binding constraint: with FSRS intervals of 30 days or more and no parameter repeats within 180 days (ENG-5), a template rarely exhausts 200 instances. The risk named in the spec (retrieval turning into recognition of the solution pattern) is about the form, which the first-seen metric tests.
- Variation theory offers no evidence strong enough to set ranges; use it only as a prompt for including contrast pairs for confusable skills (GRAPH-7 near-miss examples already do).

### Gaps
- Effect sizes and solution rates of Wolfert et al. (2026), the Sinharay and Johnson within-family variance findings, Enright et al.'s feature results, and the Open University counts of significantly different variants were not available from the pages retrieved.
- No study of randomized variants in calculus skills with a single repeated learner was found.
- A search on randomized exam variants returned titles only; the ASEE 2019 papers on exam pools were not opened.

## 5. Item quality and worked solutions: what is known about writing guidelines, step granularity and subgoal labels?

### Takeaway
**What we do.** `docs/prompts/make-cards.md` rules: one main skill per card, step-tagged numbered solutions starting with "Step 0" (choose the method), machine-checkable answers, up to three `wrong` answers each tied to a step, two or three hints (mildest first), method cues removed from the question and kept in a cued twin, a "well-posedness audit" and an "adversarial audit" in the acceptance table, and a learning phase that starts a new skill with a worked example (ENG-2).
**What others do.** The worked-example literature supports studying correct examples before problem solving; step-based tutoring beats answer-only feedback; neither literature shows that finer steps or explicit subgoal labels matter in mathematics.
**Verdict.** The solution format is consistent with the evidence on worked examples and step-level feedback. The specific devices (Step 0, subgoal-style labels, three hints) are design choices without direct evidence in mathematics. The evidence on item-writing guidelines concerns multiple-choice tests, not constructed-response math.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis of the worked-examples effect on mathematics performance: 43 articles, 55 studies, 181 effect sizes (8,033 abstracts screened); average g = 0.48 (p = 0.01); correct examples alone produced larger effects than incorrect examples alone or correct plus incorrect combined; conditions with self-explanation prompts showed a negative effect compared with conditions without; examples helped both for practice and for initial skill acquisition. [PEER; abstract only, no subgroup values] — [Barbieri et al. meta-analysis](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance)
- Algebra by Example (SERP Institute program page): over 300 classrooms, more than 130 teachers and 6,000 students, randomly assigned; students who typically struggle gained 10 percentage points on conceptual knowledge over same-teacher controls, all students gained 4 points on procedural knowledge while solving half as many problems, and scored 7 points higher on released state-test items; the main format alternates worked examples and problems 50/50 and targets common misconceptions. No effect sizes or p-values on the page. [REPORT] — [SERP Algebra by Example](https://serpinstitute.org/algebra-by-example/research)
- VanLehn (2011): answer-based tutoring d = 0.3; intelligent tutoring systems (mostly step or substep-based) reviewed d = 0.76 (believed 1.0); human tutoring reviewed d = 0.79 (believed 2.0); "This review did not confirm these beliefs", i.e. the belief that finer interaction granularity is always better was not confirmed. [PEER; abstract only] — [VanLehn 2011](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ946764)
- Subgoal labels: a pooled analysis of three programming studies (loop writing) found medium-to-large effects for provided labels with far transfer and for learner-generated labels with near transfer (no values on the page). [PEER; programming] — [Margulieux et al. pooled analysis](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/ltd_facpub/37)
- Guan, Du and Lin (2022): 130 fourth graders in four groups; splitting a solution into subgoal steps did not improve near transfer (2.76 versus 2.82) or far transfer (1.52 versus 1.73) over a holistic solution and left working-memory capacity lower (41.79 versus 36.18, p = 0.039); the choice-based labeled format had lower effort and higher near transfer than the plain subgoal format (4.19 versus 2.82, p = 0.001); no effect sizes, and the text and Table 2 swap the values of two groups. [PEER; WEAK] — [Guan et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2022.832901/pdf)
- Haladyna, Downing and Rodriguez (2002): 31 multiple-choice item-writing guidelines derived from 27 educational-testing textbooks and 27 studies since 1990; the abstract does not say how many have empirical support. [PEER; abstract only] — [Haladyna et al.](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/a-review-of-multiple-choice-item-writing-guidelines-for-classroom/)
- Epoch AI's review of 48 randomly sampled Humanity's Last Exam questions found 22 (46%) with "substantial accuracy-altering errors", of which 12 were impossible as written because of missing assumptions, contradictory premises, multiple valid interpretations, or an objective answer demanded for a subjective question. [REPORT; adversarially selected benchmark] — [Epoch AI review](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/hle/review)
- Gupta et al. (AIED 2025) coded 150 LLM tutoring dialogues on college algebra: about 90% were rated high-quality yet only 56.6% were fully correct; the failure list includes a correct final answer despite errors in sub-steps (6 cases) and correct sub-steps flagged as incorrect (3 cases). [PEER/PREPRINT; small] — [Gupta et al. abstract](https://tail.cc.gatech.edu/publications/gupta-aied-2025); [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.16460v1)

### Inferences
- The worked-example, step-feedback and 50/50 interleaving evidence supports a learning phase that opens with a correct worked example and then alternates with problems (ENG-2). It does not show that "Step 0 = choose the method" or step labels are what make the difference; the Guan result is a null on step splitting for primary-school learners.
- Recommendation R5.1, introduce skills with correct examples only and keep erroneous examples for "find the error" items after learning; the meta-analysis found correct-only larger than mixed, although moderators are confounded.
- Recommendation R5.2, do not add self-explanation prompts to every solution by default; the only moderator the meta-analysis reports for prompts is negative. Test prompts against no prompts on the student's own data if wanted.
- Recommendation R5.3, vary step granularity with tier and novelty (finer in the learning phase and tiers 1 and 2, coarser later), as a design hypothesis; VanLehn does not support granularity as a universal lever. Keep step tags at full granularity regardless, because the tags serve diagnosis and credit, not instruction.
- Recommendation R5.4, since no validated writing guideline exists for constructed-response math items, treat the `make-cards.md` rules as the hypothesis and test them through dispute and report data. Add the Epoch error categories to the well-posedness audit rubric: missing assumptions, contradictory premises, multiple valid interpretations, answer-format ambiguity (exact versus decimal, rounding, units, domain).
- Recommendation R5.5, verify the displayed worked solutions, not only the final answer (Section 8): Gupta et al. show correct final answers with wrong sub-steps.

### Gaps
- Booth et al. on incorrect worked examples in algebra and the self-explanation literature were only reached through a search listing (titles, no content); the earlier note `learning_science.md` covers worked examples more broadly and was not re-read for this section.
- No evidence was found on hint-count (two or three) or on method-cue removal specifically.
- The Haladyna review concerns classroom multiple-choice tests; its guidelines were not extracted individually.

## 6. Difficulty calibration with one learner: cold start, Elo versus IRT, how many attempts are needed, and how to handle per-template difficulty

### Takeaway
**What we do.** ENG-5: an Elo model picks the problem whose predicted success is closest to 0.80 within the skill's core band (ENG-4), with P(correct) = 1/(1 + e^-(theta_s - d_p)) and theta updated by (a / (1 + b n)) (o - P), a = 1, b = 0.05. Difficulty starts from the tier (tier 1 to 5 map to -1.5, -0.5, 0.5, 1.5, 2.5 logits) and is anchored: one student's results move it at most 0.05 per answer "because a single learner can't calibrate a problem (Elo needs about 100 learners for that)"; a run of surprising results flags a possible wrong tier for review instead. Elo never changes the FSRS schedule. Tiers come from the AI tagging pass (spec "Tagging with AI", step 6).
**What others do.** Elo with an uncertainty function is the standard lightweight online alternative to IRT for adaptive practice; item difficulty needs on the order of 30 to 100 or more responses per item; cold-start priors from language models correlate about 0.6 to 0.8 with measured difficulty.
**Verdict.** The anchored design is the right response to the evidence: calibrating items from one learner is impossible, so priors dominate. The constants are literature defaults from a many-learner system. The weak link is the quality of the tier prior, which is an AI-assigned label.

### Cited Findings
- Pelánek (2016, Computers & Education): Elo updates are theta := theta + K (correct - P) and d := d + K (P - correct); with an uncertainty function U(n) = a / (1 + b n) the paper recommends starting from "the basic version of the Elo rating system extended with a simple uncertainty function" with a = 1 and b = 0.05 (the values used for the geography system), then refit by grid search once data exist; initial values are 0 and a new item's difficulty is reset to 0; "the system needs at least 100 students to get good estimates of item difficulty"; in simulation about 10 answers per student gave a skill correlation of 0.8 with the ground truth; Elo gives estimates "very similar" to joint maximum likelihood; with adaptive item selection, proportion correct "fails"; per-skill estimates from different continents correlate only around 0.6, so a single global skill is approximate. [PEER] — [Pelánek 2016](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf)
- "Keeping Elo alive": Elo ratings are generally biased, their variances depend on context, and when items are selected adaptively from current ratings and item difficulties are updated with abilities the spread of ratings grows artificially and the ratings do not converge; two parallel chains are proposed. [PEER; abstract only] — [Tilburg University record](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/keeping-elo-alive-evaluating-and-improving-measurement-properties/)
- Linacre's sample-size table for Rasch item calibration: ±1 logit at 95% confidence needs 16 to 36 respondents (30 as the minimum for dichotomous items), ±½ logit needs 64 to 144 (100 for most purposes). [REPORT] — [Linacre, RMT 7:4](https://www.rasch.org/rmt/rmt74m.htm)
- LLMs as difficulty raters for new items without response data: three models (GPT-4o, DeepSeek-Chat, Qwen3 235B) on 360 Math Garden primary-school items (60 per domain) reached mean Spearman .673, .633 and .665 against Rasch-based difficulty; the best configuration (pairwise, hard decision, zero-shot) .730 and the weakest (absolute, hard, zero-shot) .486; pairwise judgments beat absolute by about .12 to .15; by domain, subtraction .771 and text problems .501. [PREPRINT; primary-school arithmetic] — [LLMs as expert raters of item difficulty](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.18562.md)
- A model trained to predict whether about 1,800 known students would answer a new math question correctly, using embeddings plus LLM-extracted features (solution step count, Bloom level, misconception count), reached Pearson .7791 and Spearman .7606 with benchmark 1PL difficulty on 470 held-out questions (embeddings alone .6822 and .7064); RMSE 1.1857 logits, equal to about 5,818 real answers or 22.87% of the holdout responses. Data: one Chilean platform, 251,851 answers. [PREPRINT] — [Zapien study](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.00034v1)
- A decade of adaptive online testing in undergraduate mathematics (2PL IRT, not Elo): 138,143 responses, overall correct-answer rate .353, item parameters estimated once from a 7,383-response phase with random assignment and then used live, no formal drift test, sections of 40 to 50 questions recommended. [PREPRINT] — [adaptive testing study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.08643)

### Inferences
- Tier anchors mean a student with theta = 0 has predicted success 0.818, 0.622, 0.378, 0.182 and 0.076 on tiers 1 to 5 [OWN CALC], so the "closest to 0.80" rule picks tier 1 for a new skill and moves up only as theta rises.
- The spec's constants (a = 1, b = 0.05, "about 100 learners") are exactly Pelánek's; they were tuned on a many-learner geography system, and with one learner there is no way to refit them (the paper itself says to refit). They are plausible starting values, not validated ones.
- Elo's documented bias under adaptive selection (Tilburg) is mostly neutralized by anchoring difficulties and letting only theta move, which is the right instinct; the remaining exposure is that theta alone absorbs template and tier errors.
- Tier error is likely: LLM-based difficulty estimates correlate .5 to .77 with measured difficulty, so adjacent-tier mislabels will be common. The effect is a slightly mis-targeted problem, not a wrong schedule, because Elo does not change FSRS.
- Recommendation R6.1, replace the pure tier prior with tier plus a within-tier offset (for example plus or minus 0.3 logits) from cheap predictors: pairwise LLM comparisons among a skill's problems (pairwise beat absolute by .12 to .15), solution step count and effort score (Section 4), and author tier. Keep tier means fixed and cap the offset.
- Recommendation R6.2, make the "surprising results" flag explicit: flag a problem or template when the standardized residual sum over at least 8 attempts exceeds 2 and never auto-adjust; at 8 attempts the standard error of a success rate is already about 0.14.
- Recommendation R6.3, if the app is ever used by several students, collect anonymous per-instance outcomes and calibrate at the template (family) level: about 30 responses per template give ±1 logit and about 100 give ±0.5 logit at 95% (Linacre), and pooling the 50 to 200 instances reaches those counts far sooner than per-instance calibration (the item-family idea, Section 4).
- Recommendation R6.4, keep tier, template and source IDs in every attempt log (the schema already stores tier, seed and card) so a later multi-learner fit is possible without re-collecting.

### Gaps
- No source on calibrating problem difficulty from a single learner was found; Klinkenberg et al. (Math Garden) and Wauters et al. (item difficulty from responses and judgment) were only reached through search listings.
- Pelánek's K schedule was tested on geography facts and binary correctness; no evidence on Elo for multi-step math problems with partial credit.
- The 0.80 target success rate has no source in this review.

## 7. Skill tagging (Q-matrix): how accurate are expert and AI tags, what validates a skill model, and does our solve, tag, verify, consensus procedure address the known failures?

### Takeaway
**What we do.** "Tagging with AI" in the spec (also `docs/prompts/tag-cards.md`): (1) the model solves in numbered steps and its answer must match the key; (2) candidates narrowed to 10 to 15 skills plus "none / new skill"; (3) one skill per step, key-step skills (at most two) as main tags and routine-step skills (at most four) as uses tags; (4) each tag confirmed by a yes/no question that includes the near-miss neighbor's definition; (5) steps 3 and 4 run three to five times, a main tag kept in at least 80% of runs is accepted, 40 to 80% goes to review, under 40% is dropped; (6) method cues flagged and a tier proposed; (7) model, prompt and skill-list versions stored. Before a chapter's tags are trusted the student spot-checks 30 to 50 problems with main-tag precision of at least 85% (a pack-ship criterion). GRAPH-4 and GRAPH-7 give each skill a definition, two examples and near-miss examples.
**What others do.** Skill models are validated by expert think-aloud and empirical fit, or by learning curves (Learning Factors Analysis, DataShop); LLM tagging has been tested as zero-shot or few-shot concept matching and as KC generation.
**Verdict.** The procedure targets the right failure (over-tagging, low precision), but the evidence says three of its safeguards are weaker than they look: repeated runs of one model measure consistency, not validity; the yes/no confirmation was not shown to fix precision; and a 30 to 50 problem spot check has little power. Recall of tags is not checked at all.

### Cited Findings
- Fan, Bialo and Li: AI-generated Q-matrices for a 20-item reading test compared with a validated matrix built from think-aloud protocols and expert judgment (Cohen's kappa). May 2025: Gemini 2.5 Pro .63, Claude 4 Sonnet .33, DeepSeek V3 .26, ChatGPT 4.5 .24, ChatGPT 4o .12. January 2026, newer models: Gemini 3 Pro .42, Claude 4.5 Sonnet .26, DeepSeek V3.2 .21, ChatGPT 5.2 .16, all below all four human raters. ChatGPT 4.5 and DeepSeek V3 agreed with each other at .97 while agreeing weakly with the validated matrix. [PREPRINT; reading comprehension, zero-shot, one test] — [Fan et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.08796)
- Moore et al. (GPT-4 generating knowledge components for 80 multiple-choice questions in each of two datasets): direct match with the human KC in 42/80 (52%) or 45/80 (56%) of Chemistry (the paper reports both) and 28/80 (35%) of E-Learning; the human KC appeared in the model's top five in 64/80 and 45 to 50/80; where they differed, evaluators preferred the LLM's KC in 57 of 87 cases (p = .017); the clustering algorithm's grouping accuracy was 65% (Chemistry) and 17.5% (E-Learning). [PREPRINT; WEAK: internal inconsistency] — [Moore et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.20526)
- Wang, Lin and Koedinger: scaled GPT-4o-mini KC generation on 646 multiple-choice questions produced 569 KCs against 101 for the expert model and a worse fit (RMSE .4285 versus .4206); merging labels at cosine similarity .8 gave 428 KCs and RMSE .4259, still worse than the expert model; the authors conclude "scaled LLM generation alone is insufficient". [PREPRINT] — [Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09935)
- Math-KnowCT (arXiv 2403.17281): 1,359 questions, 12 knowledge concepts, 186 matches and 1,173 non-matches in Table 1 (the text says 1:8, the table gives about 1:6.3). Zero-shot GPT-4 had accuracy .9097, precision .4839, recall .8824, F1 .6250; Mixtral accuracy .8313 with precision .3208; self-reflection raised precision for all models. One concept per prompt, binary judgment, no human baseline. [PREPRINT] — [Automate Knowledge Concept Tagging on Math Questions with LLMs](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.17281v1)
- KnowTS (arXiv 2406.13885), 24 grade 1 to 3 concepts, 2,349 samples (about 1:4 matches): embedding and fine-tuned baselines reached roughly 79 to 80% accuracy; zero-shot GPT-4-turbo reached 89.00 accuracy (85.9% F1); naive 4-shot reached 91.11; reliability with many labels was not tested. [PREPRINT] — [KnowTS](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13885v1)
- Q-matrix misspecification in the DINA model (simulation, four attributes, one entry changed per item): deleting attributes overestimates slip parameters, adding attributes overestimates guess parameters, and combinations that were deleted are heavily misclassified; no percentages in the abstract. [PEER] — [Rupp and Templin](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/The-effects-of-Q-Matrix-misspecification-on/9984371085702771)
- Learning-curve validation: in DataShop a flat, noisy curve for "compose-by-addition" signalled a knowledge component that was too general; splitting it into a new "decompose" KC lowered AIC and BIC and removed the error peaks (error rate for compose-by-addition about 20% under the new model); no learning-gain data in that case study. [REPORT] — [DataShop case study](https://datashop.phzh.ch/about/case-studies/koedinger-geometry-script-for-web.htm)
- "Closing the loop" (JEDM): Learning Factors Analysis split circle-area items into forward and backward KCs; on 82 students and 72,404 steps the square-root-based model fit best by BIC; in a classroom experiment with 115 students (57 control, 58 redesigned; 91 completed) the redesigned tutor gave higher post-test scores after controlling for pre-test (beta = 0.09, p = .027, Cohen's d = 0.47) in similar time (105.4 versus 111.6 minutes). [PEER] — [Closing the loop](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/212/pdf_29)
- Earlier LFA-based problem assignment in the Geometry Cognitive Tutor area unit: the optimized group saved about 22 minutes (12%) and learned as much (post-test p = .772, retention p = .602); numbers of students not reported. [REPORT] — [LearnLab wiki](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Using_learning_curves_to_optimize_problem_assignment)

### Inferences
- Systematic bias is not removed by repetition. Two models agreeing at .97 while disagreeing with the validated matrix shows that agreement between runs (or models) is not accuracy; the 3 to 5 run consensus (step 5) controls sampling noise within one model only. No source tested self-consistency voting for tagging, so its value is unmeasured.
- Step 4's yes/no confirmation is not supported by the evidence as a precision fix. In the one yes/no study found (GPT-4, 12 concepts, grade 1 to 3), recall was high (.88) and precision low (.48) at a base rate of about 1 in 6. The spec's narrowing to 10 to 15 candidates plus the near-miss definition may help, but the effect is unmeasured. Evidence from other KC studies (Moore, Wang) points to over-generation (too many, too fine KCs) as the dominant error.
- A 30 to 50 problem spot check has low power [OWN CALC]. Passing at 85% observed (26 of 30; 43 of 50) happens with probability 25.5% / 19% when the true precision is 80%, 9.8% / 4.5% at 75%, and 52% at 85% (exact binomial; `stats.py`); the Wilson 95% interval at 26/30 is 0.70 to 0.95. To certify a true precision of 85% or more with 95% confidence you need 29 of 30, 47 of 50 or 92 of 100 correct. The gate is a screen that rejects bad chapters most of the time, not evidence that a chapter meets 85%.
- Only main-tag precision is checked. Missing tags (recall) matter as much for this app: deleted attributes inflate slip estimates and added ones inflate guess estimates in the Q-matrix literature, and the app uses tags for error routing (ENG-15 spreads blame over tagged skills), credit (encompassing edges) and diagnostic sets.
- Learning-curve validation needs many learners; one learner's curves are too noisy for that. The practical analog is the app's mis-tag flags from his results, which are slow.
- Recommendation R7.1, build a small gold set (about 60 problems per course, tagged independently by the student or an expert from the skill definitions) before locking the prompt, and report precision, recall and Cohen's kappa per skill against it. Choose the 80% and 40% run thresholds from the gold set instead of fixing them.
- Recommendation R7.2, use a different model family for the confirmation step than for the tagging step, and treat disagreement as "review", since same-family agreement can be shared bias.
- Recommendation R7.3, spot-check with a sample size and decision rule matched to the claim: at least 100 problems per course (or sequential sampling with early stop after four errors in the first 20) for the ship gate, stratified by confidence band with weights so the estimate refers to the whole chapter, and include a recall check (list the skills a problem needs without seeing the tags, then compare).
- Recommendation R7.4, prefer more specific skill definitions with explicit near-miss pairs (GRAPH-7 already requires them); the studies show conflicts concentrate in skills defined by implicit steps (linguistic skills in the reading study, finer-grained KCs in Moore).

### Gaps
- No study of LLM tagging for calculus or high-school mathematics at the granularity of 100 to 300 skills per course was found; the two math studies use grade 1 to 3 concepts with binary matching.
- Q-matrix validation methods from the diagnostic classification literature (for example de la Torre's data-driven validation) were not retrieved.
- No source on self-consistency (multi-run voting) specifically for concept tagging.
- Moore et al. and Math-KnowCT have internal numeric inconsistencies noted above.

## 8. LLM-generated problems and solutions in 2025 and 2026: what are the measured error rates, what does verification catch, and how many errors slip through?

### Takeaway
**What we do.** The acceptance table (spec "The content pipeline"): templates need a 1,000-seed sweep through an independent SymPy route, back-substitution and degenerate-case checks plus 5 to 10 blind model solves; sourced problems with a key need a blind solve match and a well-posedness audit; AI-original problems with a checkable answer need the intended answer, a blind solve and a code solve to agree, an adversarial audit and a check that the full answer set was found; AI-original proofs ship self-graded. A disputed problem "is dropped, never patched". The report button hides a card at once, and a pack ships after "three days without finding a wrong key" (spec "A content pack ships when").
**What others do.** Measured generation and solving error rates are in the 4% to 25% range for grade-school and college-algebra items, step errors hide behind correct final answers, and LLM verifiers rarely flag errors; deterministic checks (code, CAS) are the dependable layer.
**Verdict.** Template answers computed in code, a CAS route and multi-route agreement are the right backbone. No source measures how many errors survive an acceptance rule like ours, and the release gate cannot see the error rates that matter (a 0.5% wrong-key rate passes a 300-problem look about 22% of the time [OWN CALC]). The shown solutions are not verified at all except where templates compute them.

### Cited Findings
- MATHWELL (teacher-rated, 250 random outputs per model): share meeting all criteria 74.8% (MATHWELL), 78.8% (GPT-4 Turbo), 62.8% (GPT-3.5 Turbo), 62.4% (Llama-2 70B); accuracy 96.9%, 95.8%, 89.5%, 89.5%; solvability 89.2%, 94.8%, 88.0%, 84.0%. [PREPRINT; accuracy and solvability are marginal rates and cannot be combined] — [MATHWELL](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.15861v4)
- Gupta et al. (AIED 2025), college-algebra problems from an ITS: final-answer accuracy as solver 85.5% overall (GPT-3.5 Turbo 77.3%, GPT-4 74.5%, GPT-4o 97.3%, o1-mini 91.8%, o1-preview 85.5%; 22 problem types by 5 problems); in 150 tutoring dialogues 88.6% reached a correct final answer, about 90% were high-quality, and only 56.6% were fully correct. [PEER/PREPRINT] — [Gupta et al. abstract](https://tail.cc.gatech.edu/publications/gupta-aied-2025); [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.16460v1)
- GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 on calculus (data from 2023): average score on Stewart exercises 30% (GPT-3.5) and 60% (GPT-4), on Stanford tournament problems 14% and 24%, on course exercises 43% and 72% against students' 70.88%; arithmetic and calculation errors were the most common, models failed to self-correct without external ground truth, and grading misread equivalent expressions. [PREPRINT; not Calculus I] — [IntegralBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21130v1)

[PREPRINT; WEAK] — [ORCA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.02589)
- Zhang and Graf (ETS), tasks generated from item models in arithmetic, algebra and number theory: procedural slips were the most common error and hurt more than conceptual misunderstandings, and dual-agent configurations "substantially improve results"; no error rates on the page. [PREPRINT] — [Zhang and Graf](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.09932v2)
- Algebra complexity study: models stay at 100% up to 12 parallel branches and collapse between 20 and 30; GPT-4o Mini falls from 100% at chain step 4 to 0% at step 5; all models fail at 15-digit multiplication. [PREPRINT] — [algebra complexity](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.06799)
- UGMathBench error analysis: of 100 problems (300 versions) answered by o1-mini, 56% of problems were failed in at least one version; 2.7% of versions were "bad problems" no model could solve (Section 4). [PREPRINT] — [UGMathBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.13766v2)
- Hard2Verify (80 Olympiad problems, 200 responses, 1,860 annotated steps, over 500 annotator hours): best verifier GPT-5 had balanced F1 85.83 at step level, 89.52 at response level and 69.72 at first-error identification; Gemini 2.5 Pro dropped to 52.46 on error identification; weaker verifiers "rarely flag mistakes" (true-negative rate near zero); more sequential reasoning helped (59.69 to 70.93 for one model) but "majority voting over parallel samples does not". [PREPRINT; frontier Olympiad proofs, not routine items] — [Hard2Verify](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.13744)
- Expert-written questions are not error-free: FutureHouse estimated that 29.3% ± 3.7% (95% CI) of text-only chemistry and biology questions in Humanity's Last Exam conflict with published evidence (53.3% of 321 rationales were flagged by their agent; experts disagreed with 13.7% of "contradicted" labels), attributing it to adversarial selection and to first-round reviewers not being required to verify rationales that would take more than five minutes; a later HLE re-analysis found about 18% problematic; Epoch found 22 of 48 (46%). [REPORT; adversarially filtered benchmark] — [FutureHouse](https://futurehouse.org/research/hle-exam); [Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/hle/review)
- Earlier note (not re-fetched): rule-based answer verifiers have precision of at least 99% but average recall of about 86%; model-based verifiers can be fooled by attack strings; multi-model unanimity on question validity tops out at about 92% precision while halving recall. — `answer_checking_and_verification.md` (sources arXiv 2505.22203 and 2505.13903 listed there)

### Inferences
- The numbers describe different things: 4% to 15% wrong final answers for current solvers on routine algebra, 20% or more of generated word problems failing at least one criterion, and 29% to 46% flawed items in adversarially selected expert sets. Together they say that without verification an AI-original calculus pack would carry an error rate in the single digits to low tens of percent, and that independent routes are necessary. No source gives a residual rate after verification like ours.
- The spec's agreement rule is only as independent as its routes. The "intended answer", the blind solve and the code solve are produced by related models and one CAS, and the Fan et al. result (two models agreeing at .97 while both disagreeing with the validated reference) shows that model agreement does not imply correctness. Hard2Verify adds that majority voting did not help verifiers.
- Spot-check mathematics for the "three days" gate [OWN CALC]: if the true wrong-key rate is p and the student looks at N problems, the probability of not seeing a wrong key is (1 - p)^N. For N = 300 that is 0.22 at p = 0.5%, 0.05 at 1% and 0.55 at 0.2%; for N = 150 it is 0.47, 0.22 and 0.74. If the student notices a wrong key in only half or a quarter of encounters (a learner is the worst detector of a wrong key), the probability of noticing at least one at p = 1% and N = 300 falls from 95% to 78% or 53%. A zero-defect look at 50 problems bounds the true rate only below about 5.8% (95% upper bound; 3.0% for 100, 1.0% for 300).
- The report-button and "dropped, never patched" policies are sound but reactive; they do not bound the rate before release.
- Recommendation R8.1, measure the acceptance rule's catch rate with seeded errors: take about 100 verified-correct AI-original problems, inject plausible wrong keys (sign error, off-by-one, missing solution, omitted domain restriction, wrong branch), run the full acceptance rule and report the fraction rejected, by error type. This is the number the spec lacks; it also calibrates how many problems to over-generate.
- Recommendation R8.2, verify solution steps. Extract each displayed intermediate expression or equation from the step-tagged solution and check it with the CAS where possible (derivative, antiderivative, solve, simplify, substitution); for templates the `[[ ]]` values are computed already. Gupta et al. documented correct final answers with wrong sub-steps.
- Recommendation R8.3, rely on deterministic layers first (CAS, numeric probes, back-substitution, mpmath integration and limit checks) and use model audits only as a second line; keep the strongest model with extended reasoning for audits (the spec already does for blind solves).
- Recommendation R8.4, for "the full answer set was found", check solutions with `solve` plus a numeric root scan over the domain, and have the audit check the Epoch categories (missing assumptions, multiple interpretations).
- Recommendation R8.5, plan the pipeline for attrition: MATHWELL-style numbers imply that about one in five generated items fails some criterion, so generate about 1.3 times the target pool per skill and tier, and record per-skill rejection reasons.

### Gaps
- No 2025 to 2026 study of LLM-generated calculus or high-school problems with a measured post-verification residual error rate was found; the calculus-specific evidence is from 2023 models.
- Fetch summaries omitted several numbers (Zhang and Graf's per-operation error rates and dataset size; whether Gupta et al.'s correct-answer-with-flawed-reasoning counts are out of 110 or 150).
- Source inconsistencies noted: UGMathBench 5,062 versus 5,562 problems; ORCA categories and model labels.
- The accuracy of SymPy `solve`, `limit` and `integrate` on Calculus I problems is unmeasured in this review; no published error rate was located.

## 10. Writing original problems after studying copyrighted ones: what is the legal grounding of the clean-room workflow (idea and expression, merger, United States and China)?

### Takeaway
**What we do.** The clean-room workflow (`docs/prompts/clean-room-decks.md`, spec "Original problems from owned problem banks"): Session A reads the owned sources and writes plain-text copies for a checker plus a blueprint of ideas only (topics, problem types, abstract steps, difficulty, traps, weights); Session B never sees the sources and writes new cards from the blueprint; `similarity_check.py` flags a card with 30% or more shared 5-word runs, a 12-word run in common, or all question numbers from one source problem; flagged cards are rewritten from the reason only; the cards then pass the AI-original acceptance rule. The document says "This is not legal advice" and recommends having someone qualified review it before public sharing. Ideas, topics, problem types, methods and difficulty "are not owned by anyone".
**What the law says.** US and TRIPS text exclude ideas, procedures, methods of operation and mathematical concepts from copyright; protection covers expression, and where an idea can be expressed in only a few ways the expression may be unprotected (merger). Clean-room design is an accepted way to show independent creation. China follows the same dichotomy through originality and the exclusion of formulas and general tables, but I found no Chinese case on exam or practice questions.
**Verdict.** The process rests on settled principles and is better than most. Its weak points are factual rather than doctrinal: the similarity thresholds are engineering heuristics, the blueprint could carry a source's selection and arrangement, Session A's plain-text copies are reproductions, and AI-written output may carry no copyright at all.

### Cited Findings
- 17 U.S.C. 102(b): "In no case does copyright protection for an original work of authorship extend to any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept, principle, or discovery, regardless of the form in which it is described, explained, illustrated, or embodied in such work." [PRIMARY TEXT] — [17 U.S.C. 102](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/102)
- TRIPS Article 9(2): "Copyright protection shall extend to expressions and not to ideas, procedures, methods of operation or mathematical concepts as such." [PRIMARY TEXT] — [WTO TRIPS, Part II](https://www.wto.org/english/docs_e/legal_e/27-trips_04_e.htm)
- US Copyright Office Circular 33: copyright excludes any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept, principle or discovery; words and short phrases are not protected; numbers and mathematical symbols cannot be registered; blank forms are not protected; the circular does not use the term "merger" and does not mention quizzes. [PRIMARY/DOCS] — [Circular 33](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ33.pdf)
- Idea-expression distinction (encyclopedia summary): Baker v. Selden (1879) let a book's description be copyrighted but not the bookkeeping system; Morrissey v. Procter & Gamble (1st Cir. 1967) held sweepstakes rules not copyrightable because there were few ways to express the idea (merger); the Ninth Circuit in Ets-Hokin v. Skyy Spirits (2000) treats merger as a defense, the only circuit to do so, and courts elsewhere are divided on whether merger bars copyrightability or is relevant only to copying; a UK judge doubted the doctrine in Ibcos. [SECONDARY] — [Idea-expression distinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idea%E2%80%93expression_distinction)
- Computer Associates v. Altai (2d Cir.): abstraction-filtration-comparison test; filtration removes elements dictated by efficiency (merger), elements dictated by external factors, and public-domain elements before the comparison for substantial similarity; non-literal elements such as structure, sequence and organization can be protected, but only as expression. [SECONDARY] — [Altai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_Associates_International,_Inc._v._Altai,_Inc.)
- Clean-room design "relies on independent creation, which the page presents as a defense against copyright claims", is no defense against patents, uses a specification team and an implementation team with no exposure to the original, and has a lawyer review the specification (Phoenix BIOS); NEC v. Intel (1990) accepted the argument after an imperfect process, and Sony v. Connectix (9th Cir. 2000) involved a failed clean-room attempt and direct disassembly. [SECONDARY] — [Clean-room design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean-room_design)
- US Copyright Office AI report, Part 2 (published 2025-01-29): copyright "does not extend to purely AI-generated works", prompts alone do not give sufficient control for authorship, human-authored expression that is perceptible in the output is protectable, and the line between AI as a tool and AI as a stand-in for human creativity is decided case by case. [REPORT/SECONDARY summary] — [Copyright Office AI page](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/); [IPWatchdog summary](https://ipwatchdog.com/2025/01/29/part-two-copyright-office-ai-report-says-creative-prompting-doesnt-constitute-authorship/)
- China: the Copyright Law protects works that are original and expressed in some form (Article 3), and Article 5 excludes, among others, calendars, general numerical tables, general forms and formulas ("历法、通用数表、通用表格和公式"). [SECONDARY; encyclopedia text] — [Chinese Wikipedia, Copyright Law](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E4%B8%AD%E5%8D%8E%E4%BA%BA%E6%B0%91%E5%85%B1%E5%92%8C%E5%9B%BD%E8%91%97%E4%BD%9C%E6%9D%83%E6%B3%95)
- China, originality: a Beijing IP Court judgment of 2023 ((2023)京73民终1451号, design drawings) describes originality as independence ("独", whether created independently) plus a minimum of creativity ("创"), and held drawings made of simple points, lines and geometric shapes, "basic expression" of the patent's outline, were not a work. The case is not about exam questions. [SECONDARY; law-firm article] — [Guantao article](https://guantao.com/page1784)
- China, similarity: Liang Zhiwen (The Jurist, 2015) says Chinese courts apply the "total concept and feel" and "analytic dissection" tests from US law without clear scope and argues that comparison should cover only the protected original elements, that higher similarity does not mean infringement where originality is low, and that the audience standard should depend on the type of work. [PEER; abstract only] — [Liang Zhiwen](https://faxuejia.ruc.edu.cn/CN/Y2015/V0/I6/37)
- China is a Berne member since 1992 and TRIPS is described as the basis of its IP law; the same page says the Beijing Internet Court held that AI-generated content is entitled to copyright protection (no date, facts or reasoning given on the page). [SECONDARY] — [Copyright law of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_law_of_China)

### Inferences
- A short math problem is mostly idea and method, with a thin layer of expression (wording, scenario, selection of numbers); the doctrines above (idea/expression, merger for statements with few natural phrasings, filtration of external factors) point the same way as the workflow: copy nothing but the idea. Courts compare protected expression after filtering (Altai, and Liang's description of Chinese practice), so a problem of the form "find the critical numbers of a cubic" shares no protected material with another such problem.
- The thresholds in `similarity_check.py` are engineering heuristics. No source supports 30% shared 5-word runs or a 12-word run as a legal line; routine phrases ("Find the derivative of", "Determine whether") will create false positives, and non-literal copying (same distinctive scenario in new words) is invisible to an n-gram check. The blueprint rule against distinctive scenarios is therefore the main safeguard against non-literal similarity.
- The blueprint may reproduce a source's "selection, sequence and arrangement" (Altai protects non-literal structure subject to filtration). Group by skill, drop source order, numbering, exact counts per section and any unusual progression; the prompt already groups by skill, which is the right choice.
- Session A necessarily makes plain-text copies of every owned problem for the checker (a reproduction, private and never shipped). Keep them outside the repo and never distribute them (`sources/` and `source-text/` are already outside the project); do not describe the check output in a way that leaks text.
- Independent creation is the defense that clean-room design relies on, and its strength depends on records. Keep the blueprint, the PROGRESS.md logs, the checker output and the session split as evidence that Session B never saw the sources.
- AI-written problems: under the US Office's Part 2, purely AI-generated expression is not copyrightable, so a "CC BY 4.0" label on Session B's output is a license offer over material that may have no copyright to license; human selection, editing and arrangement can be protectable. Because Session B is an AI, the pack's value as a free gift is unaffected, but the claim "original work by the author" should be phrased as "AI-generated, human-reviewed". In China the Beijing Internet Court is reported to have gone the other way for AI output, but the page gives no details.
- Recommendation R10.1, replace the thresholds' implied status with a documented rationale ("a screen for accidental copying, not a legal test"), and add a second check for non-literal similarity: for each flagged-free card compute the top-3 nearest source problems by embedding similarity and have a person (not Session B) review any pair that shares an unusual scenario.
- Recommendation R10.2, have the clean-room process reviewed by a qualified person before the first public release, as the document itself says, and give that person the blueprint format, the checker rules and a sample of drafts; ask specifically about Session A's copies, the blueprint's arrangement and AI authorship.
- Recommendation R10.3, label AI-original cards "AI-generated; reviewed by the author" in provenance and consider releasing the pack's original content under CC0 or CC BY with a statement that no copyright is claimed to the extent the material is not protectable.

### Gaps
- No case law on copyright in exam, quiz or practice questions was retrieved (a search for Educational Testing Service v. Simon returned titles only), and no Chinese case on exam questions was found; the Chinese material is statute-level and generic.
- The USCO Part 2 report itself was not read; the conclusions come from a news summary and the Office's landing page, which lists the topic only.
- The statute and case summaries in this section are secondary sources (encyclopedia, law-firm article) except 17 U.S.C. 102, TRIPS 9(2), Circular 33 and the Liang abstract. This is not legal advice.

## 11. CC BY-NC-SA obligations when the app and packs are given away free: what does share-alike trigger, and what does NonCommercial allow?

### Takeaway
**What we do.** PACK-9: packs released under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 "to honor ShareAlike", a Credits screen generated from per-card provenance (PACK-4), a release checklist for per-source duties; PACK-8 keeps personal content out of every build; the app is free to share (PACK-7) and its code license is a separate decision ("one ADR settles signing, the fsrs-rs-python license, CC BY-NC-SA pack duties, REUSE/SPDX labels... before anyone else installs it").
**What the licenses say.** Share-alike attaches to adaptations, not to collections; it applies only when a work is publicly shared; NonCommercial means not "primarily intended for or directed toward commercial advantage or private monetary compensation", judged by the use and not the user; CC recommends against CC licenses for software.
**Verdict.** A free, ad-free app plus packs under BY-NC-SA 4.0 is a coherent plan for BY-NC-SA, BY-NC and BY sources. It breaks for BY-SA and GFDL sources that are adapted. Private use (the current plan: one developer's Mac) triggers none of this; obligations start when anyone else installs it.

### Cited Findings
- BY-NC-SA 3.0 legal code: a "Collection" holds the Work "in its entirety in unmodified form along with one or more other contributions" and "will not be considered an Adaptation"; Section 4(a) says the rest of a Collection need not be licensed under the license; Section 4(b) allows an Adaptation to be distributed only under (i) this License, (ii) "a later version of this License with the same License Elements", or (iii) a Creative Commons jurisdiction license with the same License Elements; Section 4(c) bars exercising the rights "primarily intended for or directed toward commercial advantage or private monetary compensation". [PRIMARY TEXT] — [BY-NC-SA 3.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-sa/3.0/legalcode)
- CC ShareAlike interpretation: SA "applies only for works considered adaptations under copyright law"; inclusion of an unmodified SA work in a collection ("mere aggregation") does not trigger it; "The ShareAlike condition only applies when a work is publicly shared"; the page does not address apps or software. [DOCS] — [ShareAlike interpretation](https://wiki.creativecommons.org/wiki/ShareAlike_interpretation)
- CC NonCommercial interpretation and FAQ: the NC definition is about the "main purpose" of the reuse ("primarily"); nonprofit status neither guarantees nor prevents a finding ("if you are a nonprofit or charitable organization, your use of an NC-licensed work could still run afoul of the NC restriction"); CC "cannot advise you on what is and is not commercial use"; the retrieved text does not address advertising, donations or cost recovery. [DOCS] — [NonCommercial interpretation](https://wiki.creativecommons.org/NonCommercial_interpretation); [CC FAQ](https://creativecommons.org/faq/)
- CC FAQ: "We recommend against using Creative Commons licenses for software"; CC licenses may be used for documentation and separate artistic elements; BY-SA 4.0 is one-way compatible with GPLv3; CC0 is acceptable for software; CC licenses are not revocable absent breach, and anyone with a copy may keep redistributing it. [DOCS] — [CC FAQ](https://creativecommons.org/faq/)

### Inferences
- Treat every template-derived or reworded card as an adaptation of its source and every verbatim imported card as an item in a collection. The pack as a whole is then a collection of items with their own licenses; BY-NC-SA 4.0 on the pack is accurate only if every adapted item's source license allows it (BY-NC-SA 3.0 or 4.0, BY-NC, BY, CC0, public domain; the 3.0 sources such as the OPL can move to 4.0 under 4(b)(ii) because the elements are the same).
- Per-card licenses (PACK-4) are the right unit. Recommendation R11.1, let the build produce one pack per license family (BY-NC-SA family, BY-SA family, GFDL family if used), each loadable alongside the others, instead of one BY-NC-SA pack containing adapted BY-SA items.
- Recommendation R11.2, keep code and content licenses separate: the app code under a software license, the packs under the CC licenses, and the Credits file listing both, in line with the CC FAQ's advice against CC licenses for software.
- Recommendation R11.3, record "NC discipline" for the project: no advertising, no paid tier or sponsorship tied to the packs, and no charge for access to NC content; if monetization is ever considered, remove or replace NC content first. A free distribution with a developer account cost is not commercial by the definition quoted, but CC does not say how it treats donations.
- Recommendation R11.4, put the attribution duties into the build: credit, license link, "indicate changes", the "remove credit on request" clause (4(d), mentioned in 4(a)) and keep a source-of-truth list per release so revocations or corrections can be handled. CC licenses are irrevocable, so every released pack stays licensed for those who hold it.

### Gaps
- The CC 4.0 legal code (BY-NC-SA 4.0, BY-SA 4.0) was not fetched; this section quotes the 3.0 text because the OPL and some older sources use it.
- The retrieved CC pages do not decide whether donations, advertising or cost recovery make a free app commercial.
- GFDL-to-CC compatibility and the BY-SA compatibility list were not checked; no source in this review settles whether a BY-SA adaptation may be relicensed BY-NC-SA.

## 12. The most important risks and missing safeguards

### Takeaway
In priority order (severity times how little is measured today):
1. **A wrong key slips into a pack through the AI-original or AI-tagged paths, and the release gate cannot detect a rate below about 1%.** No measured catch rate exists for the acceptance rule; shown solutions are not verified; the three-day look has little power.
2. **Common-mode failure: SymPy computes template answers, confirms matches and drives sampled evaluation.** A bug in one place is invisible to the others.
3. **False rejects by strictness (hole rule, syntax, tolerance default) corrupt the FSRS schedule and are measured only through disputes.**
4. **AI skill tags are checked by a low-power precision spot check and by agreement within one model family; recall is not checked.**
5. **Template variants are not guaranteed equal in solution path and effort; nothing measures this before release.**
6. **Licensing and authorship: BY-SA and GFDL sources in a BY-NC-SA pack, heuristic similarity thresholds, AI-written content with uncertain copyright.**
7. **Units and significant figures arrive in 1.1 with a documented class of bugs and an unfinished notation story.**
8. **LLM audits are weak at flagging errors; do not let them substitute for deterministic checks.**

### Cited Findings
- Verification evidence: step-level verifiers rarely flag mistakes and parallel majority voting does not help [Hard2Verify](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.13744); models agreeing at .97 can disagree with the validated reference [Fan et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.08796); correct final answers can hide wrong sub-steps (6 of 150 dialogues) and only 56.6% of tutoring dialogues were fully correct [Gupta et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.16460v1); expert review that did not require re-deriving rationales left 29.3% ± 3.7% of a chemistry and biology subset in conflict with the literature [FutureHouse](https://futurehouse.org/research/hle-exam).
- Grader evidence: vendor documents concede one-sided error and undecidability [Maple testeq](https://www.maplesoft.com/support/help/maple/view.aspx?path=testeq), [PossibleZeroQ](https://reference.wolfram.com/language/ref/PossibleZeroQ.html); the best-known implementation of significant-figure checking had a floating-point failure [STACK #107](https://github.com/maths/moodle-qtype_stack/issues/107); contexts such as assumptions and test points changed verdicts [STACK #1727](https://github.com/maths/moodle-qtype_stack/issues/1727), [WeBWorK forum](https://webwork.maa.org/moodle/mod/forum/discuss.php?d=3508).
- Template and calibration evidence: variants differ at the question level even when totals barely move [Jordan et al.](https://oro.open.ac.uk/33705), [Zilles et al.](https://zilles.web.engr.illinois.edu/papers/exam_variant_sigcse_2022.pdf); single-learner calibration is not possible and Elo needs about 100 learners per item [Pelánek 2016](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf).
- Tagging evidence: GPT-4 yes/no concept tagging had precision .48 and recall .88 [Math-KnowCT](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.17281v1); newer models did not improve agreement with a validated matrix [Fan et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.08796).
- Legal evidence: purely AI-generated output is not copyrightable in the US [IPWatchdog summary of USCO Part 2](https://ipwatchdog.com/2025/01/29/part-two-copyright-office-ai-report-says-creative-prompting-doesnt-constitute-authorship/); an adaptation may be shared only under the same license elements [BY-NC-SA 3.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-sa/3.0/legalcode).

### Inferences
Missing safeguards, each with a concrete test, in the order I would build them:
1. **Seeded-error catch rate for the acceptance rule (R8.1).** Inject 100 plausible wrong keys into verified problems, run the whole rule, publish the rejected fraction by error type. Block shipping AI-original content for a skill until the catch rate on that error type is known. This is the largest unknown in the review.
2. **Solution-step verification (R8.2, R5.5).** CAS-check every displayed derivative, integral, solve and substitution step. The release table should include "every displayed step verified or marked unverified".
3. **A second independent engine in CI (R1.4).** mpmath-based numeric differentiation, integration and limit probes, and `solve` residual substitution, run over all template instances (the 1,000-seed sweep); for the grader, a differential oracle on the golden suite and the rewrite/perturbation property tests. Count disagreements.
4. **A properly powered release gate.** Replace "three days without a wrong key" by a sequential audit: a person independently verifies a random sample sized to the claim (at least 150 problems per pack for a 2% bound; 300 for 1% by the rule of three), counting only independent verification, not whether the student happened to notice. State the bound in the release notes. For tags, use the gold set and sample size of R7.1 and R7.3.
5. **A false-reject measurement loop (R1.1, R1.5, R2.2).** Blind re-grade a monthly sample of incorrect verdicts; log hole-only rejects, invalid-input messages and tolerance-driven rejects as categories; decide from the measured rate whether the hole rule needs a softer outcome.
6. **Offline re-verification of numeric-only verdicts (R1.3).** Re-run symbolic confirmation with a long limit when idle and surface any flip.
7. **Template equivalence checks (R4.1, R4.4).** Effort score and answer-structure fingerprint per instance, with outlier trimming at build time; the existing first-seen-versus-repeated notice stays as a runtime canary, with the knowledge that at 30 pairs it has about 7% false alarms and about 49% power for a real 15-point gap [OWN CALC].
8. **License compatibility table in the builder (R9.1, R11.1).** One pack per license family and automatic refusal of incompatible adaptations; record the page-level license and retrieval date for each source; a qualified legal review of the clean-room process before the first public release (R10.2).
9. **Units and significant-figures test plan before 1.1 (R3.2 to R3.6).** Decimal arithmetic, offset units, prefix case, synonyms, E-notation, separate count-versus-accuracy checks, with the STACK #107 case and the cases in Section 3 as golden rows.
10. **Gate model audits behind deterministic checks (R8.3).** An LLM auditor adds information only after the CAS, numeric and back-substitution checks pass; its "no error found" is weak evidence.

### Gaps
- No measured residual error rate after verification exists for any pipeline resembling the spec's; the figures here bound the problem rather than size it.
- The cost of the hole rule and of syntax strictness in real use is unmeasured; the evidence is the design intent in ADR-0007 and probes I wrote, not student data.
- Several external numbers rest on abstracts or secondary summaries (Wolfert 2026, the Sinharay family-calibration papers, USCO Part 2, the legal case summaries); a lawyer's review of Sections 10 and 11 and a full-text read of those papers would firm them up.
- The web-search budget was exhausted, so items such as exam-question case law, Chinese exam-question cases, LibreTexts' default license, Khan Academy's terms, Q-matrix validation methods and 2026 calculus-specific LLM rates remain unretrieved.
