## Q1. How accurately can students locate the step where their own work went wrong? (error detection, self-diagnosis, metacognitive monitoring, novices vs experts, data from systems that asked students to self-diagnose)

### Takeaway
No source found measures how often students correctly pick the first wrong step in their own handwritten math. The nearest evidence all points one way: weaker students find planted errors in other people's solutions far less often than strong ones (2-5 of 8 vs 8 of 8 in a 2026 think-aloud study), merely having the worked solution changes later performance very little (normalized gain 0.11) while a required, incentivized correction submitted before seeing the solution produces large gains on repeated problems (d = 1.01), the quality of written self-diagnosis did not reliably predict transfer, and even expert humans reach only 78-82% on a seven-way error-cause classification of handwritten scratchwork. A tap is therefore useful evidence about where to probe, not a diagnosis, and the spec's choice to verify it with a check problem and log a confirmation rate is the right safeguard; the true tap accuracy is an empirical question the app can measure on itself.

### Cited Findings
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED, small] Strong vs average students detecting planted errors.** Luo, Yang, Zhang & Xiong (2026, *Behavioral Sciences* 16(5):635): think-aloud plus interviews, 16 senior-high students in one Shanghai school (8 high-ability, six of them national-competition prizewinners; 8 average-ability). Three flawed solutions: "prove 5 > 16" (invalid warrant), a non-equivalent transformation, and a minimum-value problem with inconsistent conditions. Identified the error: high-ability 8/8, 8/8, 8/8; average-ability 2/8, 5/8, 5/8. Explained the cause: 8/8 vs 2/8, 4/8, 4/8. Wrote a correct solution: 8/8 and 8/8 vs 3/8 and 2/8. Mean score (max 4): 4.00/3.88/3.88 vs 2.13/1.63/1.88; ability effect F(1,14) = 28.63, p < .001. All 8 high-ability students vs 1 average-ability student said they had ever found a flawed problem or habitually solve problems several ways (p = .001). Caveats: other people's solutions, not the student's own; N = 16; one school; no expert group. — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/16/5/635)
- **[MODERATE, quasi-experimental] Having the solution is not enough; a required correction attempted before seeing the solution is.** Upper-level quantum mechanics at one university, four problems repeated verbatim from midterms on the final. Comparison students (33) received the midterm solutions and written feedback with no incentive. Incentivized students (31) could earn back up to 50% of lost midterm points by submitting corrected solutions within four days and received the solutions only after submitting. The groups are described as the second and fourth versus the first and third years of the study (my reading: different course offerings, so assignment was not random at the student level). Repeated-problem scores: comparison 67.9% to 71.5% (+3.6, normalized gain g = 0.112, n.s.); incentivized 67.6% to 88.4% (+20.8, g = 0.642, Cohen's d = 1.01; repeated-measures F(1,62) = 11.6, p = .001). On four non-repeated final problems the incentivized group scored higher but not significantly. The paper cites its predecessor's finding that "many advanced students do not automatically exploit their mistakes as an opportunity for learning." Self-diagnosis accuracy was not quantified (corrected solutions were "almost perfect"). — [arXiv 1602.07611](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.07611)
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED] Self-diagnosis score vs later problem solving.** Introductory physics, about 200 students in four recitation groups (one control, three with different levels of guidance for diagnosing their own quiz mistakes). External support changed self-diagnosis scores, but in the atypical-quiz paper the score "was not correlated with subsequent problem-solving performance on a transfer problem"; the companion paper found a correlation only in the typical-problem situation and only when textbooks and notebooks were the sole guidance. Per-student diagnosis accuracy is not in the abstracts, and the fetch tool's summaries of these abstracts mixed up which paper reported which result (one cited the companion's DOI), so the paper-to-result mapping is approximate; the related Physics Teacher 2016 abstract states effects of different interventions on later problem solving but gives no findings. — [PRST-PER 8, 020109 abstract](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevSTPER.8.020109); [companion abstract](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/what-do-students-do-when-asked-to-diagnose-their-mistakes-does-it/); [Mason et al., Phys Teach 2016](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/learning-from-mistakes-the-effect-of-students-written-self-diagno/)
- **[MODERATE] Weaker learners need the wrong step highlighted.** Große & Renkl (2007) as summarized: weaker learners struggle to explain incorrect examples unless the erroneous step is highlighted; a 2025 systematic review of 40 studies repeats that highlighting errors reduced demands and helped low-prior-knowledge learners (Große & Renkl 2007; Wernecke et al. 2018). Original numbers not retrieved. — [LearnLab corrective self-explanation](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Corrective_self-explanation); [Dieterich, Rumann & Rodemer 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10071-x)
- **[MODERATE] Answer-level monitoring is real but partial.** Foster et al. (2022, *Educ Stud Math*): Eedi, 7,302 UK students aged 6-16, 219,826 multiple-choice responses with 5-point emoji confidence; confidence correlated with facility (r_s = .504), median confidence 82.8 on correct vs 68.8 on incorrect answers. This is whole-answer confidence, not step location. — [Foster et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10649-021-10084-7), via earlier notes `science_of_practice.md` Q5, which also holds Hacker 2000 (low performers stay overconfident) and Nederhand et al. 2018.
- **[MODERATE] Even experts disagree with a gold standard on the "why".** ScratchMath (Mar 2026): 1,720 authentic handwritten scratchwork samples from Chinese primary (1,479) and middle (241) students, seven error types. Human experts: error-cause classification 78.4% primary / 81.5% middle (random 12.5%); error-cause explanation 89.3 / 86.2. Annotator agreement on a 30-question trial set was over 90% after training. — [arXiv 2603.24961](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.24961)
- **[MODERATE, ITS] Required metacognitive acts with feedback worked; optional ones were mostly ignored.** The most robust domain gains came from self-explanation (static) and error self-correction (adaptive) support, "in both ... the targeted metacognitive behavior was required to complete the task"; free-form explanation prompts without feedback were largely ignored; the Help Tutor changed help-seeking but not domain learning. The authors also note that error detection was "relatively easy" in the Excel domain of the intelligent-novice study and may work less well where detection is harder. — [Koedinger, Aleven, Roll & Baker 2009](https://pact.cs.cmu.edu/koedinger/pubs/Koedinger,%20Aleven,%20Roll%20&%20Baker%2009-v129.pdf)
- **[MODERATE, ITS] Optional feedback is rarely requested.** In Corbett & Anderson's LISP tutor study, on-demand students did not ask for feedback in 90% of exercises until they had a preliminary solution. — [Koedinger & Aleven 2007](https://pact.cs.cmu.edu/pubs/Koedinger%20Aleven%2007.pdf)
- **[WEAK, small] Behavioral cues for careless errors.** A KAIST tablet system recorded handwriting and navigation at millisecond precision and found features linked to "simple mistakes"; 15 of 45 students trained on them improved about 14% (design and statistics not shown). — [KAIST DSpace](https://dspace.kaist.ac.kr/handle/10203/208802), via earlier notes `asian_practice_tools.md`.

### Inferences
- **The tap is evidence, not a verdict; the spec already treats it that way.** The spec's weights (0.6 on the tapped step, 0.8 when the answer matched a coded wrong answer), the check problem on the tapped step's skill, the logged confirmation rate and the "fewer than half confirmed, taps count for less" rule match what the evidence supports. The only change worth making is to measure the lift properly (see Q12: occasional control probes on an untapped step), because a confirmation rate alone cannot say whether taps beat chance.
- **Expected tap accuracy is probably middling for weaker students and better when a step-labelled solution is on screen.** That is an extrapolation from 2-5 of 8 vs 8 of 8 detection on planted errors and from the highlight results; recognizing the first differing line against a worked solution is an easier task than finding an error cold, but no source measures it. Treat any number as a hypothesis the app must test.
- **"Pick one to continue" (a required act) fits the evidence better than an optional shortcut.** Optional supports are used rarely (90% of LISP exercises) or ignored (free-form prompts without feedback). Required acts need verification, which the check problem supplies. The cost of a forced pick is satisficing (arbitrary taps to proceed); log time-to-tap and weight very fast taps less.
- **Cause labels beyond "which step" (careless, misread, different method) are inherently noisy.** If trained experts reach 78-82% on a seven-way label, a student's self-label will be less reliable; this supports the spec's guard of accepting "careless slip" only for strong skills and at most once per 30 days.
- **Self-review without a forced action should not be expected to help much** (g = 0.11 with solutions in hand; see also Q4's 21% reuse figure). The quantum-mechanics incentive group had to submit a correction before seeing the solutions, which parallels the spec's second attempt before the full solution is revealed (my inference).

### Gaps
- No study measuring step-level self-localization accuracy for a student's own handwritten math, and no published confirmation-rate data from any system that asked students to self-diagnose (MATHia, ASSISTments, Eedi, Khan Academy): none found in accessible sources.
- Große & Renkl (2007) original error-detection rates, Mason & Singh (2010) numbers, and the two Yerushalmi et al. (2012) papers' full results were not retrieved (abstracts only).
- Metacognitive-monitoring studies specific to multi-step math (Baars, de Bruin, van Gog and others) were not retrieved; the earlier notes cover only multiple-choice and general calibration.
- Chinese-language 错题本 (mistake notebook) and Newman error-analysis self-attribution studies were not retrieved.

## Q2. Bug libraries and misconception-based automatic diagnosis: coverage of real student error, accuracy and build cost (BUGGY/DEBUGGY, Repair Theory, Sleeman, model tracing, Eedi, ASSISTments common wrong answers, LLMs)

### Takeaway
Bug libraries worked in narrow, fully specified procedures (multi-column arithmetic) and were expensive to build; for algebra and calculus no source gives a coverage percentage, and the available evidence says single diagnoses are unstable, the long tail is huge (Eedi's Kaggle subset alone has 2,587 distinct misconceptions across 1,857 questions) and even strong LLMs find the right misconception for a distractor only about a quarter of the time (MAP@25 = 0.27). The cheap, evidence-aligned form is per-problem coded wrong answers (ASSISTments' "common wrong answers" are stable across school years), which is what the spec already does; its learning effect is small (odds ratio 1.07).

### Cited Findings
- **[MODERATE, historical] BUGGY/DEBUGGY cost and limits.** BUGGY (1975) and DEBUGGY (Burton 1982): enumerating the bugs took "considerable effort", restricting the approach to well-defined procedural domains; the fixed library limits it to known errors; it cannot represent why a bug arises; no measured diagnostic reliability is given. — [TECFA overview, 1998](https://tecfaetu.unige.ch/staf/staf-d/krige/staf11/buggy.html)
- **[MODERATE, historical] Repair Theory coverage.** Brown & VanLehn (1980): "The theory only generates 21 of the observed 89 bugs"; of its 33 generated procedures, 21 are well-documented bugs and 10 were not observed; only 16 of 33 were known bugs when first tested (Sept 1979), 6 predicted bugs were found by Dec 1979; adding interrupt conditions raised generated bugs from 22 to 43. The paper says thousands of student tests had been analyzed but gives no percentage of errors explained, and bug migration between tests is described only anecdotally. — [Repair Theory PDF](https://www.lri.fr/~mbl/Stanford/CS477/papers/RepairTheory-SeelyBrown.pdf)
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED, abstract-only] Instability of algebra bugs.** ETS report (1992): "many bugs were unstable across diagnoses, and the stable ones tended to be rare"; rule-space analysis gave comparatively more stable diagnoses; sample size not in the abstract. — [ETS 1992](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/1992/hxjo.html)
- **Sleeman, Kelly, Martinak, Ward & Moore (1989), "Studies of diagnosis and remediation with high school algebra students", *Cognitive Science* 13(4):551-558** exists; its abstract and results could not be retrieved. — [Huddersfield record](https://eprints.hud.ac.uk/id/eprint/7290/)
- **Modern mal-rule library size and sources.** An LLM paper's own library (MalAlgoPy) has 20 misconceptions for single-variable linear equations, drawn from Sleeman (1984, 1985) and Payne & Squibb (1990), and excludes systems of equations and polynomial factorization; it reports no coverage or cost for the earlier libraries. Fine-tuned Llama-3.1-8B learned many mal-rules from 400-800 examples (one reached over 90% misconception accuracy at 800) but training on misconceptions alone degraded correct solving unless correct examples were mixed in. — [arXiv 2410.12294](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.12294)
- **[MODERATE] Model tracing and the cost of modeling.** Anderson, Corbett, Koedinger & Pelletier (1995): bug messages come from buggy productions; the authors estimate cognitive modeling at "10 hr or more per production rule"; no figure for the share of student errors that buggy rules matched. Outcomes: LISP minicourse (1984) 30% less time and about one SD higher final test; Algebra (1987-88) no differences between tutor and control classes; sample sizes not given. — [Anderson et al. 1995](https://www.public.asu.edu/~kvanlehn/ITScourse2009/Readings/Anderson%20Corbett%20Koedinger%20Pelletier%201995.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] A fully built model-tracing tutor at scale.** RAND cluster-randomized trial of Cognitive Tutor Algebra I (54 high schools, 68 middle schools): year 1 no significant difference; year 2 high schools about 0.2 SD (50th to 58th percentile), middle schools similar but not significant. — [IES award page](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/effectiveness-cognitive-tutor-algebra-one-implemented-scale)
- **[V] Eedi's catalogue.** More than 4 million student responses; 12,000 diagnostic questions tagged with constructs and 36,000 distractors mapped to misconceptions; the same misconception recurs across topics (taking the smaller number from the larger appears in two-digit subtraction, integers, decimals and standard-form division). Eedi calls manual tagging "Painful. Slow. Inconsistent. Not scalable." Its Kaggle challenge asked solvers to match a distractor to a misconception from a list, with many test misconceptions unseen in training; the winning approach was retrieve-and-rerank on Qwen LLMs; no scores are on the page. — [Eedi Substack](https://eedi.substack.com/p/what-do-4-million-students-responses); [Eedi blog](https://www.eedi.com/news/from-wrong-answers-to-real-insights-how-we-used-a-kaggle-challenge-to-map-student-misconceptions)
- **[MODERATE, preprint] Size of the long tail and LLM accuracy on it.** Eedi Kaggle data: 1,857 questions, four options each (7,428 answer choices), 4,338 choices labelled with misconceptions (58%), 2,587 unique misconceptions. Misconception inference MAP@25: Llama-3.1-8B 0.178 base, 0.204 after the paper's training, GPT-3.5 0.206, GPT-4o 0.259, GPT-4.1 0.271. The paper gives no figure for how often a wrong answer maps to a single known misconception. — [arXiv 2510.11502](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.11502)
- **[MODERATE, preprint] LLMs reason much worse about flawed paths than correct ones.** MalAlgoQA (807 math questions, grades 3-11): GPT-4o identifies the correct-answer rationale 95.65% of the time but the malgorithm (wrong-answer rationale) only 66.10% (zero-shot chain of thought); LLaMA3-70B 95.47% vs 55.32%; GPT-3.5 86.28% vs 14.74%. — [arXiv 2407.00938](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.00938)
- **[MODERATE] ASSISTments common wrong answers (CWAs).** A CWA is a wrong answer given by 3 or more students. 1,045 grade 6-8 Illustrative Math/EngageNY problems had CWAs spanning at least two school years (the same top CWAs reappeared despite population shifts). Teacher-written CWA feedback (24 teachers; now 1,660 problems) was shown only for a problem's top 3 CWAs; randomized over 20,044 students and 1,387 problems; next-problem correctness within the same skill odds ratio 1.07 (p = 0.028; 12,175 students, 535 problems), within the same assignment 1.03 (p = 0.188). For one example problem the first CWA accounted for roughly 59-72% of incorrect attempts across four school years (my calculation from a garbled table; one problem only). No aggregate coverage share is reported. — [Gurung et al. 2023 (L@S)](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10417271); [ASSISTments definition](https://www.assistments.org/individual-resource/what-are-common-wrong-answers-and-how-are-they-calculated); [PAR copy with the example table](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10448515)
- **[MODERATE, preprint] Automatic step-level localization on typed student solutions is mediocre even with the answer key.** First-error localization on real student attempts (VtG, 1,002 grade-school attempts; PRM800K, 2,077 test items): GPT-4o 54.5% / 39.3% with no reference, 63.6% / 43.7% with the gold solution; LearnLM-1.5-Pro 54.9 / 42.5 no reference, 64.1 / 49.7 with gold; Llama3.1-405B 49.9 / 24.1 and 62.4 / 39.5; random baselines 18.3% and 9.5%. "Most models still struggle to pinpoint the exact error step." — [EMNLP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.553.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Do not build a student-level bug library (DEBUGGY style).** DEBUGGY needed many items from one student to find a consistent bug; the app sees one typed final answer per attempt, bug diagnoses are unstable (ETS), the observed bug space was far larger than the generated one (21 of 89), and the build cost is large. No calculus bug library was found.
- **Do the cheap, deterministic part.** (1) Per card, a short list of coded wrong answers with the step behind each and one sentence of feedback (the spec has this); the ASSISTments top-3 policy and year-to-year stability suggest 3 is the right list length. (2) A small reusable mal-rule generator per skill family (sign error, distribution, exponent, missing chain rule, and similar), applied by the CAS to the correct path so that typed wrong answers can be matched by exact equivalence; MalAlgoPy's 20 linear-equation misconceptions show the order of magnitude per family. Both give high precision and unknown recall, so display the result as "likely" and let the tap and the check problem decide.
- **Expect a small payoff.** The best field estimate of coded-wrong-answer feedback is an odds ratio of 1.07 on the next problem; a fully built model-tracing tutor gave about 0.2 SD at scale. The value of auto-diagnosis is mainly zero student effort and better preselection of the tap.
- **LLMs are not a substitute for the library.** Misconception retrieval MAP@25 near 0.27 and MalAlgoQA's 66% mean an LLM can propose candidates for authors to review but should not label students' errors unreviewed.

### Gaps
- No percentage of real student errors explained by a bug or mal-rule library in algebra or calculus was found (Burton 1982, Payne & Squibb 1990, Sleeman 1984-89 numbers not retrievable; Brown & VanLehn give only 21 of 89).
- Sleeman et al. (1989) remediation results (diagnosis-based vs re-teaching) not retrieved, so the claim that diagnosis-specific remediation beats re-instruction is untested here.
- Eedi Kaggle leaderboard scores and the share of free-response errors that match coded distractors were not found; multiple-choice formats force answers into coded options, so MCQ coverage overstates free-response coverage (my inference).
- Actual build-hours per mal-rule or per coded wrong answer are not reported (only "10 hr or more per production rule").

## Q3. Step-based tutoring and scaffolded re-solving after an error: learning, time cost, student acceptance (VanLehn 2011, Andes, MATHia, ASSISTments scaffolds, subgoal labels)

### Takeaway
Feedback at the step level is the best-supported feature in the ITS literature (VanLehn 2011: step-based d = 0.76 vs answer-based 0.31, each against no tutoring), but the direct step-vs-answer comparison rests on only 2 studies (d = 0.40), the deployed-at-scale effect of a full step-based math tutor is about 0.2 SD, and the evidence comes from tutors where the student types every step, which Toishi's workflow avoids (per the spec, the student works on paper and types only the final answer). Scaffolding questions after a miss performed better than hints in ASSISTments but not always significantly, and some students found forced scaffolds frustrating; hints on demand beat proactive hints (effect size 0.35). The supportable use is an on-demand guided redo after a confirmed failure, not a forced step-entry mode.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG] VanLehn (2011), *Educational Psychologist* 46(4), effect sizes against no tutoring.** Answer-based 165 studies d = 0.31 (40% reliable); step-based 28 studies d = 0.76 (68%); substep-based 26 studies d = 0.40 (54%); human tutoring 10 studies d = 0.79 (80%). Direct comparisons: step-based vs answer-based 2 studies d = 0.40 (50% reliable); human vs step-based 10 studies d = 0.21 (30%); substep vs step 11 studies d = 0.16 (0%). The "interaction plateau" hypothesis: human = substep = step > answer > none. The abstract confirms human tutoring d = 0.79 (not 2.0) and ITS d = 0.76. — [VanLehn slides](https://quality.mit.edu/files/2012/01/Quality-Symposium-Kurt-VanLehn.pdf); [ERIC abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ946764)
- **[MODERATE] Andes.** Second evaluation: mean posttest "approximately 1 standard deviation higher" than pencil-and-paper homework on the same problems (U.S. Naval Academy); authors attribute it mainly to the grain size of interaction but say they do not know which features are responsible; students "often complain about being required to define variables"; the full five-year effect-size table was not readable. — [VanLehn et al. 2005](https://www.public.asu.edu/~kvanlehn/ITScourse2009/Readings/IJAIED410VanLehnv7.pdf); [IJAIED abstract](https://content.iospress.com/articles/international-journal-of-artificial-intelligence-in-education/jai15-3-02)
- **[MODERATE] Newer, smaller ITS estimate.** 2025 K-12 meta-analysis: pooled g = 0.271 (18 studies, 77 effect sizes), math 0.253; on-demand hints 0.316, just-in-time hints 0.337 (n.s.); not yet peer reviewed. — [arXiv 2511.04997](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.04997), via earlier notes `science_of_practice.md` Q6.
- **[MODERATE] Scaffolds vs hints (ASSISTments).** Scaffolding questions asked after a wrong answer vs hints that tell the same information, 4 items: students with scaffolds "performed better" though not always significantly; the authors note some students found forced scaffolding frustrating; sample and effect size not on the page. — [Razzaq & Heffernan 2006](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2F11774303_63)
- **[MODERATE] Hints on demand beat proactive hints.** Repeated-measures design, 72 eighth graders (61 analyzed), two topics: gain 0.137 with hints on demand vs 0.041 with hints appearing after an incorrect answer; F = 4.42, p = 0.04 (df printed as F(59,1) in the paper); effect size 0.35 [0.02, 0.74]; no aptitude interaction (high ability 0.143 vs 0.043; low ability 0.128 vs 0.039); students already used on-demand hints, the study was short. — [Razzaq & Heffernan 2010](https://web.cs.wpi.edu/~nth/pubs_and_grants/papers/2010/ITS/Razzaq%20Hints%20Is%20It%20Better%20to%20Give%20or%20Wait.pdf)
- **[WEAK, abstract-only] Step-level feedback fading.** Circuit-analysis problem solving: static step-level feedback and a schedule that fades from step-level to problem-level feedback gave higher near-transfer posttest scores than static problem-level feedback and the reverse fading; no N or effect sizes in the abstract. — [Learntechlib](https://learntechlib.org/p/200516)
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED] Subgoal labels.** Margulieux & Catrambone (statistics and chemistry; programming earlier): subgoal-labelled worked examples "seem to be equally effective across the different domains"; subgoal-labelled procedural instructions most effective for more complex procedures; no sample sizes or effect sizes retrieved. — [CogSci paper](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/cognitivesciencesociety/article/25958/galley/15582/download)
- **Time.** LISP tutor: tutor students took 30% less time (1984 minicourse); immediate feedback took about one third the time of no feedback; Andes and ASSISTments time costs for step entry were not reported. — [Anderson et al. 1995](https://www.public.asu.edu/~kvanlehn/ITScourse2009/Readings/Anderson%20Corbett%20Koedinger%20Pelletier%201995.pdf)
- **Acceptance.** Students made many negative comments about the no-feedback tutor even when they eventually learned as much, and often grew impatient with long messages (Anderson 1995, same link; paraphrase from the fetch tool's summary, not a verbatim quote). For AI feedback on handwritten calculus quizzes (UC Irvine, 301 survey respondents): 60-61% rated the feedback accurate, 62-63% clear, 52-53% preferred a TA, 76-78% were open to AI with improvements. — [arXiv 2603.00895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.00895)

### Inferences
- **Grain size is the lesson, but only where the system sees the steps.** The step-vs-answer gap (0.76 vs 0.31) is a between-study comparison across different tutors; the one direct comparison is 2 studies. It supports giving students step-level information after a miss (the numbered worked solution plus a per-step check) more than it supports forcing step entry.
- **Guided redo should be on-demand and checkpoint-based.** Hints-on-demand beat proactive hints (0.35) and forced scaffolds frustrated some students, so offer "Walk me through it" on a fresh sibling after a failed check or a second miss, with 2-4 typed checkpoint values checked by CAS equivalence, rather than making every miss a step-entry task. Checkpoint keys need authoring per card (my estimate: they can be derived from the existing step-tagged solutions).
- **Cost to the student is the main risk.** Typed intermediate expressions on a Mac keyboard are slower than writing on paper (no measurement found); restrict the mode to skills that already failed a check.
- **Expect about 0.2-0.3 SD, not 0.76, as the realistic ceiling** for any step-level addition measured on external tests (RAND 0.2; 2025 meta-analysis 0.27).

### Gaps
- No measured time cost of typed step entry vs paper work; no study of scaffolded redo in a paper-first workflow.
- Razzaq & Heffernan 2006 sample size and effect sizes, the Andes five-year table, and subgoal-label effect sizes in algebra/calculus were not retrieved.
- MATHia step-entry acceptance data were not found.

## Q4. Learning from errors: erroneous and contrasting examples, self-explanation of errors, comparing your own work with a correct solution, mistake notebooks and test corrections

### Takeaway
Erroneous examples help a little on average (g = 0.136 across 42 papers, 177 effects), mostly when error-explanation prompts are included and often only on delayed tests, at the price of more load and "confrustion"; presenting an erroneous solution beside the correct one has the best vote count in the systematic review (9 of 12 comparisons favored it vs correct examples, 6 of 8 vs problem solving). For mistake notebooks and self-comparison the evidence is thin: passive solution access gave g = 0.11, only 21% of engineering students said they would reuse a graded exam, and required, incentivized correction produced d = 1.01 on repeated items but no significant transfer to new ones. No controlled study of photo-based mistake notebooks was found.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG-ish, abstract-level] Meta-analysis of erroneous examples.** *Review of Educational Research* 2025: 42 papers, 177 effect sizes (robust variance estimation) comparing erroneous examples with correct examples or problem solving: overall g = 0.136, "statistically significant but weak"; one significant moderator, the design of error-explanation activities: self-explanation prompts and instructional explanations improved learning from erroneous examples more than no explanation. — [TU Dresden record, DOI 10.3102/00346543251390901](https://fis.tu-dresden.de/portal/en/publications/a-framework-for-learning-from-erroneous-examples-and-metaanalysis-of-empirical-research(113f115d-59d4-43c3-b1c8-52c3c97b0b40).html) (secondary)
- **[MODERATE] Systematic review with vote counts.** Dieterich, Rumann & Rodemer (2025, *Educ Psychol Rev* 37:94): 40 studies in 33 articles (mathematics 26 studies). Erroneous vs correct examples: 9 of 21 comparisons favored erroneous, 11 no difference, 1 favored correct. Contrasting erroneous with correct (shown side by side) vs correct examples: 9 of 12 favored contrasting, 3 no difference. Erroneous vs problem solving: 7 of 14 favored erroneous, 6 none, 1 favored problem solving; contrasting vs problem solving 6 of 8 favored contrasting. Benefits appeared only at follow-up in Adams et al. (2014) and McLaren et al. (2012, 2015); erroneous examples generally increased cognitive load; prior-knowledge results contradictory. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10071-x)
- **[MODERATE] Delayed benefit and confusion cost, decimals.** Richey et al. (2019, *Computers & Education* 139), 598 analyzed sixth graders, erroneous examples vs problem solving in a tutor: immediate posttest d = 0.14 (n.s.), one-week delayed d = 0.27 (p < .001); confusion/frustration higher with erroneous examples (0.34 vs 0.25, d = 0.54) and negatively related to outcomes. — [PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10155531)
- **[MODERATE] Efficiency.** McLaren, van Gog, Ganoe, Karabinos & Yaron (2016, *Computers in Human Behavior* 55), two chemistry classroom experiments: no learning differences among worked examples, tutored problem solving, erroneous examples and problem solving; worked examples took 46-68% (Exp 1) and 48-69% (Exp 2) less time. — [Utrecht record](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/the-efficiency-of-worked-examples-compared-to-erroneous-examples-/)
- **[MODERATE] Algebra and order effects (earlier notes).** Barbieri & Booth data (N = 206): displayed-error condition beat control on equation solving (beta = .200) and did not increase errors; Wesenberg et al. (2022, N = 83): erroneous-first raised mental load (34.00 vs 31.07, p = .037) with no learning difference. — [UDel](https://udspace.udel.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/775c29a4-17fa-418d-b7af-a1595b0f8b33/content); [Wesenberg et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1032003/pdf), via earlier notes `science_of_practice.md` Q5.
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED] Explaining incorrect answers.** Siegler (2002): children who self-explained incorrect as well as correct answers learned mathematical equality better than children explaining only correct ones; Siegler & Chen (listed as in press, cited as 2008) found the same for water displacement; Booth, Koedinger & Siegler (2007) poster: preliminary, corrective self-explanation may uniquely improve conceptual knowledge in algebra; it "should be paired with experiences that build correct knowledge" or students may fall back on the wrong strategy. No sample sizes on the page. — [LearnLab](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Corrective_self-explanation)
- **[MODERATE, quasi-experimental] Required correction before seeing the solution** (Q1 numbers): comparison group given the solutions at once, no incentive, +3.6 points (g = 0.112); incentivized group submitted corrections first and got the solutions afterwards, +20.8 (d = 1.01) on repeated items, not significant on new items. — [arXiv 1602.07611](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.07611)
- **[WEAK] Required quiz corrections (practitioner paper).** Students correct graded assessments, state why they erred (diagnosis) and what principle they learned (generalization); the authors say students "can usually perform diagnosis but often struggle with generalization"; no controlled experiment; normalized gains of 0.47-0.56 in four courses (N = 194, 46, 210, 107) vs 0.23 and 0.15 in comparison courses (uncontrolled); a survey of 456 first-year engineering students found only 21% said they would use a graded exam again later; grading one set of corrections takes an instructor about an hour. — [Quiz corrections article](https://home.miracosta.edu/rfrench/documents/HendersonQuizCorrections_full.pdf)
- **[WEAK] Self-monitoring checklist of common errors.** Year-4 Malaysian pupils (32 vs 30, intact classes, 12 lessons of 30 minutes, one unit): posttest 70.25 vs 44.87, ANCOVA F(1,59) = 11.00, partial eta-squared = .157; mediation statistics inconsistently reported. — [Chee & Abdullah 2021](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1315831.pdf)
- **[WEAK] Teacher-written error analysis sheets.** 66 psychology students, eight case studies; sheets list the correct answer, the most common wrong answers and why they are wrong; over 80% agreed they helped; exam grades correlated with attendance (r = .347 for error-sheet classes), no control group. — [Núñez-Peña & Bono 2022](https://headconf.org/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/14656.pdf)

### Inferences
- **The contrasting layout in the spec is the best-supported form.** Showing the student's answer beside the correct one, and the find-the-error item type (wrong step in a stored solution, pick the step, pick the reason, type the fix), correspond to the contrasting/erroneous-example designs with the best vote counts. Keep explanation prompts in find-the-error items (the only significant moderator) and highlight the step for early-stage students (Große & Renkl highlight result). The gain is small (g about 0.14), so keep the item type rare (the spec's 1 in 8-10 reviews).
- **Self-comparison alone is weak; what works is the required act plus a later retest.** Passive access to solutions gave g = 0.11 and 21% reuse intention; a required correction attempted before the solution was shown gave d = 1.01 on repeats but no significant gain on new problems. Toishi's check problem (a required act) and the 3-7 day redo already supply what the QM study's incentive supplied; they should also include a fresh sibling, because gains on identical items overstate transfer.
- **A mistakes list is not itself an intervention.** No evidence was found that keeping a list helps; its value is as a queue for fresh-sibling retests (QUE-20), matching the earlier notes.
- **Option (d), photos for self-comparison without AI, has no direct evidence of benefit** and adds effort (taking, naming, opening photos). Its plausible value is as a personal record; treat it as a convenience, not a learning feature.

### Gaps
- No controlled study of photo-based or notebook-based mistake logs in math; Chinese 错题本 outcome studies and Korean 오답노트 studies were not retrieved.
- Error-management-training meta-analysis (Keith & Frese 2008), self-assessment/self-grading meta-analyses (Panadero; Sanchez et al. 2017) and Gadgil/Nokes-Malach/Chi comparison-with-own-misconception work were located only as search targets (search budget ran out) and are not reported.
- Whether contrasting the student's own wrong solution (rather than an experimenter-made one) matches the vote counts is not tested in any source found.
- Original effect sizes for Große & Renkl (2007), Booth et al. (2013, 2015) and McLaren et al. (2012, 2015) not retrieved.

## Q5. Hint design: hint abuse, bottom-out hints, help-seeking, penalties

### Takeaway
Students use hints badly (36% of actions in one geometry unit were hint abuse, 14% when rapid successive requests are counted once) and policing the behavior changes behavior more than learning: the Help Tutor improved help-seeking but not domain learning, a dissuasion intervention cut help requests from 22.8 to 7.4 without a significant overall learning difference, and an anti-gaming tutor cut gaming from 33% to 18% with no overall learning effect. Reading time on bottom-out hints correlates with learning (r about 0.4-0.5 in a small study), so the useful design target is a bottom-out hint that is worth reading, with hints on demand. No source tested hint penalties directly.

### Cited Findings
- **[MODERATE] Frequency of hint abuse.** In a Geometry Cognitive Tutor unit (Aleven et al. 2006) students abused hints on 36% of their actions; counting successive rapid hint requests as one action lowers this to 14%. "Clicking through" hints to the bottom-out hint, then doing what it says, is the typical pattern. — [LearnLab help abuse](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Help_abuse)
- **[MODERATE] Help-seeking behavior and learning.** In the Geometry Cognitive Tutor Study 1, 17% of student actions were help-seeking errors, correlated negatively with learning (r = -0.42). The Help Tutor reduced those errors and bottom-out-hint requests, and in the following month students spent longer reading hints (12 vs 8 s, p < .01; mean hint level 2.2 vs 2.6, p = .06), but there was no difference in domain learning in either study (the page's table lists four classroom studies of 40, 70, 60 and 84 students but does not say which two produced these results). — [LearnLab Help Tutor](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/TheHelpTutor_Roll)
- **[MODERATE] Metacognition support summary.** Help Tutor weakest result; "Scooter" anti-gaming: gaming seen in 33% of control students vs 18% of experimental (marginally significant), no overall learning effect, though the third of students who received the most supplementary exercises learned significantly more; self-explanation and error self-correction gave the most robust gains. — [Koedinger et al. 2009](https://pact.cs.cmu.edu/koedinger/pubs/Koedinger,%20Aleven,%20Roll%20&%20Baker%2009-v129.pdf)
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED, small] Bottom-out hints can work as worked examples.** Shih, Koedinger & Scheines (2008): 39 students with pre/post scores; estimated time spent thinking about bottom-out hints correlated with posttest r = 0.53 and adjusted gain r = 0.48 (control, n = 18) and r = 0.38 / 0.41 (experimental, n = 19); the authors conclude that thinking time on bottom-out hints is beneficial. — [EDM 2008](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/philosophy/docs/scheines/Shih_Koedinger_Scheines_2008_EDM.pdf)
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED] Dissuading help requests.** Calculus tutor (proactive help offered on about 50% of opportunities), 60 students (27 vs 33; the dissuaded group was asked not to request help unless needed, told there would be a delay, and the tutor waited 10 s before reactive help): dissuaded students averaged 7.4 help requests vs 22.8 (p < .01); overall learning gain n.s.; low-pretest dissuaded students gained 4.9 vs 1.8 of 28 (p = .099); across conditions more help requests went with lower posttests (standardized beta = -.472); a new abuse appeared: repeatedly selecting a step and cancelling until proactive help arrived. — [ASU paper](https://www.public.asu.edu/~kvanlehn/distrib/effects%20of%20dissuating%20unnecessary%20help%20requests%20while%20providing%20proactive%20help.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Help avoidance and proactive help.** Logic tutor, 140 completers: data-driven help-need prediction cut possible help avoidance to 3.59% vs 14.59% (p < .01) with help abuse unchanged (6.99% vs 7.3%); no effect sizes. Hints on demand beat proactive hints (effect size 0.35, Q3). — [Alam et al. 2023](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10525850)
- **[WEAK/CONTESTED] Direct vs indirect hints and reading time.** Digital math game, 986 students and 9,041 hint-action pairs: longer reading predicted a valid next attempt and higher efficiency; direct hints predicted higher efficiency (B = 0.04, p < .001) on the same and the next problem, indirect hints lower on the same problem (B = -0.13) but higher on the next problem (B = 0.118); R-squared only 0.026-0.028; no learning posttest. — [Lee et al.](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10596299)
- **[MODERATE] Design lineage.** Anderson et al.: help only on request, successive hints, "tied to knowledge tracing to discourage hint abuse"; students often used bottom-out hints without reading earlier hints and often did not request help after multiple errors (Aleven & Koedinger log data, in Koedinger & Aleven 2007). — [Anderson et al. 1995](https://www.public.asu.edu/~kvanlehn/ITScourse2009/Readings/Anderson%20Corbett%20Koedinger%20Pelletier%201995.pdf); [Koedinger & Aleven 2007](https://pact.cs.cmu.edu/pubs/Koedinger%20Aleven%2007.pdf)
- **2025 meta-analysis (earlier notes):** on-demand hints g = 0.316 vs just-in-time 0.337 (n.s.); the authors warn that less self-regulated learners may game on-demand help. — [arXiv 2511.04997](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.04997)

### Inferences
- **The spec's hint design is within the evidence.** On-demand, laddered hints, rating capped at Hard after any hint and Again after the bottom-out hint are a mild penalty. The evidence neither supports nor refutes that a penalty reduces abuse; no study isolating penalties was found, and heavy policing changed behavior without improving learning.
- **Invest in the bottom-out hint, not in policing.** Make the last hint a worked step plus a one-line reason (a "worked example"), and consider a short minimum dwell before it can be dismissed (reading time 12 vs 8 s was the Help Tutor's lasting change). Log rapid click-through (several hints under about 2 s apart) as a student-level signal rather than penalizing it.
- **Do not auto-push hints.** Proactive hints did worse than on-demand (0.35) and invite the "select-and-cancel until help appears" abuse seen in the dissuasion study.
- **Hint use is also diagnostic.** Because help requests track weakness (low-ability students requested 11 hints vs 5.4), a first-hint request is a cheap signal for the "didn't know where to start" category without asking.

### Gaps
- No study of the effect of rating penalties for hints on hint-seeking or help avoidance; Aleven et al.'s help-seeking review and the 2026 LAK paper "Revisiting the Hint Button" were found by title only (robots restrictions).
- No data on hint abuse in paper-first workflows or among adult self-directed learners.
- Help Tutor per-study sample sizes could not be mapped from the page.

## Q6. Feedback timing and type for multi-step math problems

### Takeaway
Elaborated feedback beats right/wrong feedback (d = 0.49 vs 0.05 in a 40-study meta-analysis), delay generally hurts, and for multi-step work the best-supported pattern is: flag the error immediately, allow a self-correction attempt, then give the full explanation. The spec's "Not quite", second attempt, then full solution with the student's answer beside the correct one fits that pattern. Feedback on a correct answer needs no elaboration, and minimal feedback can hurt those who already have a correct strategy.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG] Content and timing meta-analysis (earlier notes).** Van der Kleij, Feskens & Eggen (2015): 40 studies, 70 effect sizes; elaborated feedback d = 0.49, knowledge of correct response 0.32, knowledge of result 0.05; larger for mathematics; negatively affected by delayed feedback. — [Van der Kleij et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314564881), via earlier notes `science_of_practice.md` Q5.
- **[MODERATE] Immediate vs flagged vs on-demand (LISP tutor).** Posttest 55% (immediate), 55% (error flagging), 58% (demand) vs 43% with no feedback; immediate feedback took about one third the time of no feedback; explanatory messages vs none (the fetch summary labels this "explanatory vs. no feedback", so the comparison condition is not fully clear): errors per production 15% vs 22%, first-attempt corrections 65% vs 38%, final exam 76% vs 80% (n.s.); terse messages did slightly better than longer ones (n.s.). — [Anderson et al. 1995](https://www.public.asu.edu/~kvanlehn/ITScourse2009/Readings/Anderson%20Corbett%20Koedinger%20Pelletier%201995.pdf)
- **[MODERATE, small field] Letting errors be caught (intelligent novice).** Excel formula tutor, about 3 hours, temporary workers (not a course; N not in the text): an "intelligent novice" tutor that tolerates initial errors a student can catch and then supports error detection and correction beat the expert-model tutor on the delayed transfer test (74% vs 60% correct) and on an immediate isomorphic test (88% vs 79%). The authors suspect plain delayed feedback without the reasoning support would not have worked and note error detection was relatively easy in that domain. — [Koedinger et al. 2009](https://pact.cs.cmu.edu/koedinger/pubs/Koedinger,%20Aleven,%20Roll%20&%20Baker%2009-v129.pdf)
- **[MODERATE, abstract-level] Elaboration and timing in math practice.** Attali & van der Kleij (2017, *Computers & Education* 110), 2,445 participants randomized, isomorphic item pairs in consecutive web-based math tests: elaborated feedback beat knowledge-of-correct-response after incorrect first responses and gave no advantage after correct ones; immediate feedback plus a delayed review outperformed delayed feedback after incorrect first responses (worse after correct ones), and immediate feedback without delayed review did worse in both cases; no effect sizes in the abstract. — [ACU record](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8610z/effects-of-feedback-elaboration-and-feedback-timing-during-computer-based-practice-in-mathematics-problem-solving)
- **[MODERATE] Step-level feedback fading** (circuit analysis; Q3): step-level then problem-level schedules outperformed problem-level feedback. — [Learntechlib](https://learntechlib.org/p/200516)
- **[MODERATE] Minimal feedback can hurt (earlier notes).** Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson (2016): children already taught a correct strategy did worse with feedback (about 62% vs 81%, Exp 1, N = 108). — [Vanderbilt PDF](https://news.vanderbilt.edu/files/FyfeRittleJohnson2015_JEP.pdf), via earlier notes.
- **[PRACTITIONER] Koedinger interview.** "Err on the side of immediacy", but students need "space to self-correct"; of delayed feedback he said "That's just too long. Students aren't even in the mind frame, anymore." — [The Learning Agency](https://www.the-learning-agency.com/blog/a-conversation-with-ken-koedinger-on-metacognition-and-the-importance-of-feedback)

### Inferences
- **Keep the three-stage flow.** (1) Immediate "Not quite" (a flag, equal to immediate feedback on learning in the LISP study), (2) one more attempt (space to self-correct, the intelligent-novice principle), (3) full elaborated solution after the last miss. Do not defer feedback to the end of a session; keep the redo as the delayed review (Attali: immediate plus later review did best after errors).
- **Correct answers: one-line confirmation unless unsure.** Elaboration gave no advantage after correct first responses; the spec's unsure and guessed paths already add the solution.
- **Keep messages short.** Terse messages did at least as well as long ones; the "likely cause" line from auto-diagnosis should be one sentence.
- **Do not assume the child-study caution applies to adults;** it is why the earlier notes suggested a two-step reveal for stable skills (a design to test, not a finding).

### Gaps
- Attali & van der Kleij effect sizes, Attali (2015) on multiple-try feedback, Shute (2008), and Mathan & Koedinger's sample size not retrieved.
- No study of feedback timing for paper-based multi-step work entered as a final answer only.

## Q7. Photos of handwritten work (a): how accurate are 2025-2026 cloud vision models at transcribing handwritten math and at grading or locating errors?

### Takeaway
Frontier cloud models are not "trash" at deciding whether handwritten work earns rubric points (about 88-91% rubric-check accuracy on an independent private benchmark in October 2026; 87-99% rubric-item accuracy in a university course study), but they are weak at the two things a mistake diagnosis needs: verbatim transcription of imperfect work (38-81% of real student circuit-homework transcriptions contained at least one error in 2025-era models) and locating the first wrong step (about 43-67% on 2024-25 models; error-cause classification 40-47% vs about 80% for experts). Their characteristic failure is to auto-correct or hallucinate plausible content, which hides exactly the student mistake you want to find. Current-generation models (Claude 5.x, GPT-6.x, Gemini 4) are not yet measured on step localization.

### Cited Findings
**Reading (transcription)**

- **[MODERATE, preprint] Russian exam scans.** 122 handwritten EGE solutions: best exact-match score agreement 56.56% (o4-mini given the answer), Gemini 2.0 Flash 36.89% without the answer, 47.54% with it, 46.72% with the true solution; geometry weakest. — [arXiv 2507.22958](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.22958v1)
- **[WEAK, small] GPT-4o on a probability exam.** 18 students x 5 questions: best prompt (answer plus rubric) accuracy 46.7%, MAE 0.0766, correlation 0.617; no reference answer overestimated scores; the authors call accuracy "still too low for real-world settings". — [arXiv 2411.05231](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.05231v2)
- **[WEAK, Chinese journal] Multi-agent grading prototype.** SCNU Journal 2026 (dataset size not on the page; it matches scoring points on basic math solution problems and describes no step-level recognition or error localization): recognition edit distance 0.40 and 85% accurate recognition; scoring mean absolute human-machine error 0.96 points, RMSE 1.92, 61% consistency with humans. — [SCNU Journal](https://journal-n.scnu.edu.cn/article/doi/10.6054/j.jscnun.2026021)
- **[WEAK] A Claude-based "red pen" grader announced on Hacker News (PenMentor, Oct 2026):** the write-up reports no accuracy, sample size or dataset and says completeness and stability "still need community verification". — [80AJ](https://www.80aj.com/2026/10/21/penmentor-claude-math-grader/)

### Inferences
- **The owner's suspicion is half right for AI in general.** Deciding rubric points from a clean scan is now roughly 88-91% accurate for the best models; transcribing a student's real, error-containing work verbatim and finding the first wrong line is far less reliable, and the failure is systematic (auto-correction hides the student's actual error). That is the exact capability a diagnosis feature needs.
- **Older numbers probably understate current models, but by an unknown amount.** Most papers tested models from 2024 to mid-2025 (GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5-2.5, Claude 4.5); WildHandBench (Aug 2026) and SAGE (Oct 2026) include Claude Opus 4.8/5.5, GPT-6.1 and Gemini 3.x/4. One hypothesis, not tested in any paper: Claude 4.5 ran at the standard 1,568-px image cap, and the 2,576-px high-resolution tier only arrived with Claude 4.7+ (Q8), which may have helped small handwriting. No source isolates this.
- **Giving the model the stored worked solution and final answer is cheap for this app and sometimes helps, but the effect is model-dependent.** In CHECK-MAT, exact-match agreement rose from 36.9% to 46.7-47.5% for Gemini 2.0 Flash, from 32.0% to 35.3-38.5% for Gemini 2.0 Flash Lite and from 31.2% to 43.4% for Qwen 2.5 VL 32B (true solution only), but barely moved o4-mini (55.7% to 54.1-56.6%) and was mixed for Gemini 2.5 Flash Preview (44.3% to 41.0-45.9%); in the GPT-4o probability-exam study, adding the answer and rubric raised the correlation with the instructor from 0.28 to 0.62.
- **So the only defensible AI role is propose-then-confirm:** the model proposes which line of the stored solution first differs from the photo, the student confirms with one tap, and the check problem verifies. The AI output must never set a rating by itself. Expect the proposal to be right roughly half to two-thirds of the time on messy notebook photos until measured (my estimate from 43-67% localization on older models); log agreement with the student's own tap to measure it.
- **Image quality is a first-class requirement:** blurry, out-of-focus or rotated images caused transcription errors, hallucinated text and sometimes inflated scores in the course study, and Claude could not process 3 of 49 forms because of image-size limits, so photos should be downscaled before upload. Rotation is model-dependent: GPT-4o scored the same on VEHME's 100 heavily rotated samples (73% / 63%) as on its full AIHub test (73.00 / 62.88).

### Gaps
- No benchmark of Claude 5.x, GPT-6.x or Gemini 4 on first-wrong-step localization or verbatim transcription of handwritten math; no calculus-specific first-error localization; no study of lined-notebook phone photos specifically (most are scans, exam booklets or webcam shots).
- SAGE's dataset, labelers and human agreement are not published; its "check accuracy" measures rubric decisions, not step location.
- Claude was not evaluated in FERMAT, VEHME, ScratchMath, OmniHandwritingOCR or the course studies.

## Q9. Photos of handwritten work (c): local options on Apple silicon (open vision-language models, Pix2Text, TexTeller, pix2tex)

### Takeaway
The owner's "trash" suspicion is well supported for small general vision models (small 2-3B-class local models scored CER 0.67-1.31 on synthetic handwritten forms, not math) and for pix2tex (BLEU 0.01-0.07 on handwritten expressions); it is mostly supported for 8B-class models (best open model under 10B: CER about 31% on mixed handwriting, 57% on hard multi-line formulas). Specialized math-expression recognizers (TexTeller) report strong numbers on cropped single expressions (author-reported 81-91% expression rate) but no source shows them on lined-paper notebook photos, they return LaTeX per crop (no step location), and none of the sources gives Apple-silicon speed or memory.

### Cited Findings
- **[MODERATE, preprint, off-domain] Small local models on handwriting.** 4-bit quantized models limited to consumer T4 memory, tested on the paper's 4 synthetic handwritten forms (the local models were not run on the 49 real forms): Qwen2-VL-2B accuracy 0.43 (CER 1.00), Qwen2.5-VL-3B 0.48 (CER 0.67, hallucination 0.36), dots.ocr (two-stage, with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) 0.39 (CER 1.31, hallucination 0.41); on the same synthetic forms Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite scored 0.86 (CER 0.21) and Gemini 3.1 Pro 0.84 (CER 0.15). — [arXiv 2604.16504](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.16504)
- **[MODERATE, preprint] Open models on mixed handwriting and formulas.** OmniHandwritingOCR CER: Qwen3-VL-8B 30.94%, InternVL3-78B 32.19%, Qwen2.5-VL-72B 38.88%, Nanonets-OCR2-3B 42.27%, Gemma-3-27B 65.82%, PaddleOCR-VL 71.14%, DeepSeek-OCR 73.84%, GOT-OCR2 84.19%, MinerU2.5-1.2B 125.59%; Qwen3-VL-8B's multi-line formula CER rises from 24.95% (easy) to 57.08% (hard). — [arXiv 2608.18586](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18586v1)
- **[MODERATE, preprint] Open OCR specialists trail general VLMs on wild handwriting.** WildHandBench formulas (CDM, higher better): Qwen3.5-Plus 73.77, Qwen3-VL-235B 69.06, PaddleOCR-VL-1.6 51.75, MinerU2.5-Pro 61.79, DeepSeek-OCR2 18.33; the top six overall are general-purpose models; GPT-5.4 not in the table. — [arXiv 2608.22959](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.22959)

- **[V-ish, author-run] TexTeller.** 298M-parameter ViT encoder plus Transformer decoder, trained on Tex80M (80 million synthetic LaTeX renderings) mixed with a small handwritten set. Expression rate (ExpRate): CROHME 2014/2016/2019 88.0 / 85.9 / 85.8 (best prior UniMERNet 67.4 / 68.4 / 65.4); HME100K 90.7 (prior best PosFormer 69.5); CROHME 2023 61.8, MNE 70.0, MathWriting 81.0, vs GPT-4o 19.3 / 22.6 / 33.8 and Qwen2.5-VL-72B 33.5 / 16.7 / 12.0 (LVLM numbers depend on the authors' prompts and output cleaning; Gemini and Claude not tested; no CER reported). - **[MODERATE, preprint] UniMERNet handwritten-expression test.** HWE set of 6,332 expressions (3,332 CROHME + 3,000 HME100K): BLEU / edit distance: pix2tex 0.012 / 0.920 (0.067 / 0.800 in another table of the same paper), Texify 0.341 / 0.522, UniMERNet-B 0.895 / 0.072. Sizes and throughput: UniMERNet-T 107M parameters (7.20 images/s), S 202M (6.04), B 325M (5.06), Texify 312M (4.16); GPU not specified in the extract. — [arXiv 2404.15254](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.15254)
- **Pix2Text.** Version 1.1.4 (2025-07-25), formula models MFR/MFD 1.5, MIT license, a separate macOS desktop app (Pix2Text-Mac); the README gives no handwriting claim, accuracy numbers, model sizes or Apple-silicon notes. — [Pix2Text GitHub](https://github.com/breezedeus/Pix2Text)
- **Ink-trained reference.** MathWriting-trained models (CTC Transformer, PaLI, PaLIGemma) reach CER 5.5-6.0% on online ink rendered to images; common errors: z vs 2, misplaced sub- and superscripts. — [arXiv 2404.10690](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.10690)
- **A mid-size open model on rubric grading.** Vals SAGE lists Gemma 4 31B IT at 82.78% (rank 23, cost "Free", 6.19k tokens, 106.81 s latency, hosting not stated) vs 89-91% for the leaders; the page does not label which models are open-weight. — [Vals SAGE](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/sage)

### Inferences
- **Verdict on "a local vision model would produce trash":** correct for 2-3B models, correct for pix2tex on handwriting, largely correct for 8B VLMs used as verbatim transcribers (CER about 31%, error in 85% of real homework transcriptions), uncertain for specialized recognizers on cropped lines (strong author-reported numbers, no notebook-photo test), and not correct for much larger open models used as rubric graders (Gemma 4 31B 82.8% on SAGE, if it can be run locally, which is unverified).
- **The only plausible local pipeline** is line detection, a TexTeller-class recognizer per line, then SymPy checking whether each line is equivalent to the previous one or to the stored step. A recognition error then appears as a false "wrong step", so the student must confirm or correct each recognized line, which likely costs as much time as typing the lines; this is a research project, not a v1 feature.
- **Apple-silicon fit is unproven** for every candidate in the sources (no speed or memory numbers), and the app's packaging rules (PyInstaller, pinned dependencies, no network) make bundling multi-gigabyte models a separate engineering cost (AGENTS.md: PyInstaller onedir .app, pinned dependencies, offline at runtime).

### Gaps
- No Apple-silicon (MLX, CoreML, MPS) speed, memory or accuracy measurements for any handwriting-capable model; no check of Apple's own Vision framework or Math Notes for handwritten equations; no TexTeller or Pix2Text test on lined-paper phone photos; Pix2Text's and TexTeller's own handwriting accuracy documentation not found; whether Gemma 4 31B IT is open-weight and how it runs on a Mac.

## Q11. Comparison of the diagnosis designs: accuracy, learning benefit, student effort, cost

### Takeaway
No design has direct evidence that it improves learning relative to the others for a paper-first practice app. The evidence ranks them by what each contributes: verification by a check problem and a required act (current design) is best supported; deterministic auto-diagnosis from coded wrong answers is cheap but modest; guided redo is supported at the step-feedback level but expensive in student effort; unstructured photo self-comparison has no direct support; cloud photo reading is affordable but not accurate enough to be a verdict; local photo reading is not viable now.

### Cited Findings
| Design | Accuracy of the diagnosis | Learning benefit (best evidence) | Student effort | Build / run cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Current: tap first differing step, then check problem** | Tap accuracy for own work never measured. Proxies: average-ability students found planted errors 2-5/8 vs 8/8 strong ([Luo 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/16/5/635)); cause labels agree with a gold standard only 78-82% even for experts ([ScratchMath](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.24961)). The check problem supplies ground truth on the tapped skill | Required metacognitive acts with feedback gave robust ITS gains; optional ones were ignored ([Koedinger 2009](https://pact.cs.cmu.edu/koedinger/pubs/Koedinger,%20Aleven,%20Roll%20&%20Baker%2009-v129.pdf)). No trial of a tap itself | One tap plus one short check item (time not measured; my estimate a few seconds plus 30-90 s) | Already specified |
| **(a) Auto-diagnosis from wrong answers** (coded answers, mal-rules) | Exact when the typed answer matches a coded/generated one; recall unknown. One ASSISTments example problem: top wrong answer about 59-72% of incorrect attempts ([Gurung 2023](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10448515), my calculation); library covered 21 of 89 observed bugs ([Brown & VanLehn](https://www.lri.fr/~mbl/Stanford/CS477/papers/RepairTheory-SeelyBrown.pdf)); diagnoses unstable ([ETS 1992](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/1992/hxjo.html)); LLM misconception retrieval MAP@25 0.27 ([arXiv 2510.11502](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.11502)) | Small: coded-wrong-answer feedback odds ratio 1.07 for next-problem correctness, 12,175 students and 535 problems in the within-skill analysis ([Gurung 2023](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10417271)); full model-tracing tutor about 0.2 SD at scale ([RAND](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/effectiveness-cognitive-tutor-algebra-one-implemented-scale)) | None | Authoring per card (ASSISTments: 24 teachers wrote feedback, 1,660 problems); shared mal-rule generator per skill family (my estimate: low-medium); DEBUGGY-style libraries: "considerable effort" ([TECFA](https://tecfaetu.unige.ch/staf/staf-d/krige/staf11/buggy.html)); modeling "10 hr or more per production rule" ([Anderson 1995](https://www.public.asu.edu/~kvanlehn/ITScourse2009/Readings/Anderson%20Corbett%20Koedinger%20Pelletier%201995.pdf)) |

| **(d) Photos attached for self-comparison, no AI** | As good as the student's own comparison (unmeasured) | No direct evidence. | **(e1) Cloud AI reads the photo** | Transcription: 38-81% of real student solutions had at least one error in 2025-era models ([arXiv 2602.00095](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.00095)); first-error localization 43-67% on 2024-25 models ([FERMAT](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.07244v1), [VEHME](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.1619.pdf)); rubric decisions 88-91% on the best 2026 models ([SAGE](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/sage)); error-cause classification 40-47% vs about 80% humans ([ScratchMath](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.24961)) | None shown for diagnosis; AI feedback in calculus: 60-61% of 301 students rated it accurate ([arXiv 2603.00895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.00895)) | Medium: photo plus a 29-197 s wait per multi-page submission across the SAGE top five (single photo not measured) | $0.001-0.05 per photo (Q8); own key; breaks offline default; privacy |

### Inferences
- **Ranking by evidence per unit of effort:** (current design with check problem) > (a)-lite deterministic matching > (b) on demand > (d) as a convenience > (e1) as optional propose-and-confirm > (e2) not now. (c) is dominated: it keeps the same accuracy problem and drops the required act that the ITS evidence favors.
- **The expensive options buy accuracy the cheap ones can get another way.** The check problem already converts an uncertain tap into a measured skill result; AI would mainly raise the quality of the preselected step, which auto-diagnosis from coded answers also does for the common cases.
- **Where AI could add real value** is for the long tail of wrong answers that match no coded answer and for "different valid method" detection, both of which need the model to compare a photo with the stored solution; those are the cases where propose-and-confirm saves the student the most thinking. This is a hypothesis to be tested with the in-app logs in Q12.

### Gaps
- Time costs for every effort cell are unmeasured in the sources; the app should time them (Q12).
- No head-to-head study of any two designs for adult self-directed math practice.

## Q12. The best-supported design, with specifics

### Takeaway
Keep the tap, but change what it means and add cheap evidence around it. The best-supported design is: (1) a required, low-effort tap against the numbered worked solution (a required metacognitive act), (2) deterministic auto-diagnosis from coded wrong answers and a small shared mal-rule set to preselect the step, (3) verification of every tap by a check problem, plus occasional control probes to measure whether taps carry information, (4) an on-demand checkpoint-based guided redo after a confirmed failure, (5) photo capture and cloud AI reading deferred to an optional, off-by-default, bring-your-own-key propose-and-confirm feature, and (6) no local vision model for now. Direct answers to the owner's questions: Is tapping a good idea? Yes as cheap evidence, not as the diagnosis; Is something better? Not as a replacement, but adding auto-diagnosis and an on-demand guided redo improves it; Are photos better? Not on current evidence, and small local models would indeed produce poor transcriptions of handwritten math today (best 8B model: CER about 31%; specialized recognizers unproven on notebook photos).

### Cited Findings
These are the findings the design rests on (full citations in Q1-Q10):
- Required acts with feedback gave robust ITS gains, optional ones were ignored or unrequested ([Koedinger 2009](https://pact.cs.cmu.edu/koedinger/pubs/Koedinger,%20Aleven,%20Roll%20&%20Baker%2009-v129.pdf)); solutions in hand without a required act gave g = 0.11 vs d = 1.01 with a required correction submitted before the solution was shown ([arXiv 1602.07611](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.07611)).
- Weaker students detect errors much less often (2-5 of 8 vs 8 of 8) ([Luo 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/16/5/635)); highlighting the wrong step helps novices ([Dieterich 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10071-x)).
- Coded common wrong answers are stable across years and their feedback has a small positive effect (odds ratio 1.07) ([Gurung 2023](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10417271)); student-level bug diagnoses are unstable ([ETS 1992](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/1992/hxjo.html)).
- Step-level feedback d = 0.76 vs answer-based 0.31 ([VanLehn 2011](https://quality.mit.edu/files/2012/01/Quality-Symposium-Kurt-VanLehn.pdf)); hints on demand beat proactive hints, effect size 0.35 ([Razzaq 2010](https://web.cs.wpi.edu/~nth/pubs_and_grants/papers/2010/ITS/Razzaq%20Hints%20Is%20It%20Better%20to%20Give%20or%20Wait.pdf)).
- Erroneous and contrasting examples help modestly (g = 0.136; contrasting 9 of 12 comparisons favorable) with explanation prompts as the only significant moderator ([RER 2025 record](https://fis.tu-dresden.de/portal/en/publications/a-framework-for-learning-from-erroneous-examples-and-metaanalysis-of-empirical-research(113f115d-59d4-43c3-b1c8-52c3c97b0b40).html); [Dieterich 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10071-x)).
- AI photo reading: rubric decisions 88-91% but localization 43-67% on older models, transcription errors in 38-81% of real solutions, systematic auto-correction ([SAGE](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/sage); [arXiv 2602.00095](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.00095); [FERMAT](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.07244v1); [WildHandBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.22959)); cost $0.001-0.05 per photo (Q8); local models CER 31-131% (Q9).

### Inferences
**Build now (v1, no new dependencies, offline)**
1. **Keep the miss flow:** "Not quite", second attempt, full solution with the student's answer beside the correct one, then the picker. Keep the required pick ("Pick one to continue") because required acts outperform optional ones, but make "Can't tell" a first-class option with the same visual weight and no extra clicks, and record time-to-tap. Taps under about 1.5 s count for less (my threshold; tune from data).
2. **Add a deterministic "likely cause" before the tap.** Match the typed wrong answer, by exact CAS equivalence, against (i) up to 3 coded wrong answers per card, each with its step index and one sentence of feedback (ASSISTments' top-3 policy), and (ii) candidates generated by a small reusable mal-rule set per skill family (order of 20-40 rules per family, the scale of MalAlgoPy's 20 linear-equation misconceptions). Preselect the step, label it "likely", and let the student override. Use the spec's 0.8 blame weight only for the card-coded matches; use a lower weight for generated matches until the log shows they confirm.
3. **Keep the check problem as the verifier** and treat its result as the measurement. Add **control probes**: on about 10-20% of picks (my suggestion), also schedule a check on one weak, untapped step, so the app can estimate the lift P(check fails | tapped) vs P(check fails | untapped) per student and per source (coded match, generated match, student tap). The spec's "fewer than half confirmed" rule cannot tell chance from signal; the lift can. Keep the rule, but compute it against the control rate.
4. **Guided redo, on demand.** After a failed check or a second miss on the same skill within a week, offer "Walk me through it" on a fresh sibling: 2-4 checkpoint fields (intermediate values typed, CAS-checked), hints on demand, never forced. This is the step-level-feedback mechanism (d = 0.76 vs 0.31) used only where the student has shown a gap; it keeps paper-first work for everything else.
5. **Hints:** keep the ladder and the rating caps; make the last hint a worked step with a one-line reason (bottom-out hint as worked example), add a short minimum dwell before it can be dismissed, log rapid click-through as a student-level signal, never auto-push hints.
6. **Find-the-error items:** keep them rare (1 in 8-10 reviews); add the explanation step (pick the reason) and highlight the suspect step for early-stage skills.
7. **Instrument the miss flow** (this is what settles the open questions with real data): time-to-tap, tap source (student, coded, generated), confirmation and lift per source, "can't tell" share, hint dwell, and the time spent on the picker and the check. Review after about 200 misses.

**Build later, optional (v2)**
8. **Photo capture without AI:** only if the owner wants a personal record; label it a convenience (no evidence of learning gain). If added, tie it to the required act (the tap or a one-line "what I would change") rather than a free-floating gallery.
9. **Cloud AI propose-and-confirm, off by default, bring-your-own key:** send the photo (long edge about 2,000-2,576 px, under the 10 MB API file limit, good light, upright; my suggestion, consistent with the vendors' image-token caps), the stored worked solution and the final answer; ask for the first line that differs from the stored solution and a one-sentence reason; present it as a preselected, labelled suggestion in the same picker; the student confirms with one tap; the check problem verifies; the AI never sets a rating. Default to a Haiku/Flash-Lite tier ($0.001-0.004 per photo) and offer a Sonnet/Pro tier ($0.02-0.05) as an upgrade, but test each tier on 20-30 real notebook photos first: the forms benchmark shows large reading gaps within one vendor (Haiku 4.5 CER 0.69 vs Sonnet 4.6 0.37; Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite 0.40 vs 3.1 Pro 0.31), even though SAGE (a rubric task) has Haiku 5.5 near the top. Log AI-vs-student-tap agreement and AI-suggestion confirmation lift; expand only if the lift is clearly above the control rate (a hypothesis: roughly 50-65% agreement is what older benchmarks predict). State plainly in the UI that images leave the Mac.
10. **Local vision model:** do not build now. Revisit if an open model reaches CER under 10% on real notebook photos (test with 50 photos) or a TexTeller-class recognizer proves itself on cropped lines of real handwriting; the Apple-silicon speed and memory question remains unmeasured.

**What would change this recommendation**
- If the in-app lift of student taps over control probes is near zero, make auto-diagnosis and the guided redo primary and shrink the tap to a single "which step felt hardest" nudge.
- If AI-suggestion lift is clearly higher than tap lift and agreement exceeds about 70% on his own work, promote the AI proposal to the default preselection (still confirmed by tap).
- If a controlled study of step-localization accuracy on handwritten math appears (none found), reweigh the AI cost-benefit.

### Gaps
- All learning-benefit claims for the tap, auto-diagnosis and photo features are extrapolations; no source tested these designs in a paper-first, adult, self-directed math app.
- Thresholds in item 1 (1.5 s), item 3 (10-20% probes) and the agreement figures in item 9 and the promotion triggers are my suggestions, not research results.
- Pending lookups that could change details: Sleeman 1989 remediation results, error-management-training and self-grading meta-analyses, OpenAI image-token costs, Mathpix pricing, Apple-silicon handwriting benchmarks, benchmarks of Claude 5.x/GPT-6.x/Gemini 4 on step localization.
