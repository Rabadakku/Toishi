# Toishi learning-engine and scheduling decisions: evidence review

*Conventions (applies to every section).* Spec and ADR rules are cited as local files: [spec](/docs/spec.md), [ADR-0006](/docs/decisions/0006-learning-engine-core.md), [ADR-0009](/docs/decisions/0009-daily-queue.md), [ADR-0013](/docs/decisions/0013-fsrs-fitting-clock-and-memory.md), [ADR-0014](/docs/decisions/0014-honest-grading-and-queue-parity.md), [credit.py](/core/engine/diagnosis/credit.py). Evidence strength tags: **[Strong]** meta-analysis or replicated RCTs; **[Moderate]** one good RCT/quasi-experiment, a meta-analysis with real caveats, or a large observational study; **[Weak]** small, lab-only, correlational, preprint or disputed; **[Convergent practice]** several unrelated products or communities arrive at the same rule, with no controlled test; **[Vendor]** a company's own claim or data; **[None]** nothing found. Verdict scale: Supported / Weakly supported / Unsupported / Contradicted. **[2024-26]** marks publications or posts from 2024 to 2026. **"My computation"** marks a local, model-internal calculation or simulation (self-consistent FSRS-6 default learner via py-fsrs 6.3.2, success drawn as Bernoulli(R)); these are inferences, not citations, and they inherit every assumption of the model (they show what the rule does, not what real students do). The current date is 2026-11-01. The web-search budget ran out during this task, so a few lookups are listed as gaps; earlier-session notes are cited with their own URLs.

## 0. Overall verdict map: which engine decisions are evidence-backed, and what to change first

### Takeaway
The principles behind the engine (spacing practice of problem types, worked example before practice, retrieval with feedback, prerequisite checks, raising review intensity before an exam, a guarded fitter) have moderate to strong support, but nearly every number (gate sizes, tier bands, 0.4 x stability, z <= -2, 6 lapses, 70% of median, 0.25 chain cutoff, fair-share weights) is a design value with no outcome evidence, and FSRS-6 on skills with fresh problems has never been tested (the spec says so itself). No decision is outright contradicted by the evidence found; the closest cases are the "1 to 3 items apart" confusable rule (the interleaving meta-analysis puts the benefit in immediate succession), the schedule-affecting "slow means Hard" rule, and the 200-review switch-on for partial credit, which my calculation shows cannot discriminate. Separately, my simulation shows that the placement walk as implemented (deepest skill first, passes propagate to prerequisites, fails prune nothing) seeds only about a tenth of the skills that a student who knows half the course actually knows, against 70 to 80% for a half-split order.

### Cited Findings
- The spec itself states the central caveat: "FSRS has never been validated for skills with fresh problems, so the engine logs enough to check its own predictions." — [spec, Learning engine intro](/docs/spec.md)
- FSRS-6 benchmark numbers come from Anki flashcard reviews (FSRS-6 log loss 0.3460, RMSE(bins) 0.0653, AUC 0.7034 without same-day reviews), not from skills or problems. — [srs-benchmark README](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)
- A forum volunteer (not a maintainer) wrote on 6 Sep 2026 that for practice problems "there's no evidence that either of the algorithms, or even spaced-repetition scheduling in general" applies, and that practice problems "work much better at getting the skill down" **[Convergent practice at most; opinion] [2024-26]** — [Anki forum thread](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/questions-about-tuning-fsrs-for-math-logical-reasoning-problem-cards/70949)
- An FSRS contributor (Expertium) wrote that "FSRS and SM-2 cannot access the review history of any other card", that handling that would need an app built for it "from the very beginning", and linked Math Academy's FIRe. — [Anki forum, sibling intervals](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/interval-determination-of-siblings/49267)
- Offline accuracy is not proof of scheduling benefit: a 2026 single-author preprint argues test-set accuracy is not enough because logs come from existing schedulers (the Lucas critique) and notes that A/B tests between state-of-the-art schedulers are absent from the literature **[Weak, preprint] [2024-26]** — [Schill 2026, arXiv 2610.10548](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10548); MaiMemo's preliminary 2025 report says offline gains did not carry over to product outcomes **[Vendor, not peer reviewed] [2024-26]** — [MaiMemo 2025 report](https://memodocs.maimemo.com/docs/2025_experiment).
- Model choice matters less than data and thresholds: Pelánek's review of knowledge-tracing models finds the choice among models inconclusive and says the data used and the thresholds matter more **[Moderate]** — [Pelánek, UMUAI overview](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/umuai-overview.pdf); extended BKT performs about like deep knowledge tracing — [Khajah et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.02416); logistic regression leads on moderate data and DKT on large data **[Moderate]** — [Gervet et al., JEDM](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/view/451).
- Rules inherited from the gap audit are already implemented as of ADR-0013/0014 (whole-study-day clock, guarded refits, a miss stays a miss, stretch unrated, rated reviews aimed at the course's desired retention, credit On only when credited predictions beat plain ones on at least 200 held-out reviews). — [ADR-0013](/docs/decisions/0013-fsrs-fitting-clock-and-memory.md), [ADR-0014](/docs/decisions/0014-honest-grading-and-queue-parity.md)

## 1. FSRS on skills with fresh problems, compared with cards, Math Academy FIRe, ALEKS retention checks, ASSISTments' ladder, knowledge tracing, DASH/DAS3H (ENG-1, ENG-3)

### Takeaway
Spacing the practice of a problem type is supported (a 216-student math experiment, a 2025 math meta-analysis with g = 0.28, college calculus classroom studies), and skill-level temporal models predict math logs better than item-only models (DAS3H), but nothing tests FSRS-6 itself on skills with fresh problems; the only skill-level schedulers with outcome data are ASSISTments' fixed 7/14/28/56 ladder (RCT, no effect sizes published) and ALEKS-style retention checks (vendor data). Verdict: **Weakly supported**; keep FSRS as the working hypothesis and compare it with two baselines on the app's own logs.

### Cited Findings
- **What the spec does.** One FSRS-6 card per skill via py-fsrs 6.3.2, `learning_steps=()`, desired retention from the goal, a fresh problem per review, due dates computed live from stability and current retention (ENG-1); a lapse is followed by an unrated same-session reinforcement problem and a real relearning review on the next study day (ENG-3). The spec calls its own approach unvalidated. — [spec ENG-1, ENG-3](/docs/spec.md)
- **Spacing a problem type works (math).** Rohrer & Taylor, 216 college students learning one kind of math problem: splitting 10 problems across two sessions a week apart had a nil benefit at 1 week but an "extremely large" one at 4 weeks; 3 vs 9 problems in one session made no difference at 1 or 4 weeks **[Moderate; lab-style, one problem type]** — [Rohrer & Taylor](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1770/).
- **Math-specific meta-analysis [2024-26].** Murray, Horner & Goebel (2025): spaced vs massed practice in mathematics, 27 studies and 53 effect sizes, g = 0.28; isolated-learning studies g = 0.43, course-embedded g = 0.24 **[Strong for spacing in general, thinner for math]** — [Murray et al. 2025](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/229807).
- **Calculus classrooms.** In a precalculus course for engineering students, spaced content was retained better than massed content within subjects; students given some spaced retrieval practice outperformed fully massed students on the precalculus final and on calculus exams the next semester **[Moderate; quasi-experimental]** — [Hopkins et al. 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-015-9349-8). A follow-up found spacing mattered more than the amount of retrieval practice **[Moderate]** — [Lyle et al. 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09489-x). A further engineering-mathematics spaced-retrieval study had no results on its page **[None]** — [ASEE](https://peer.asee.org/introducing-desirable-difficulty-in-engineering-mathematics-with-spaced-retrieval-practice).
- **Against, for complex tasks.** Donovan & Radosevich (1999; 63 studies, 112 effect sizes): overall d = 0.46, but the spacing advantage shrank with task complexity (cluster means 0.97, 0.42, 0.11, 0.07; the most cognitive cluster, high mental/low physical, 0.42), retention was tested after only 24 hours, intervals over one day averaged d = 0.16, and the authors concluded the effect "appears to be limited to relatively simple tasks" **[Moderate; old, thin cognitive evidence]** — [Donovan & Radosevich](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/1999-donovan.pdf).
- **Optimal gaps scale with the test delay.** Cepeda et al. 2008 (1,354 participants): the optimal gap fell from about 20-40% of a 1-week test delay to about 5-10% of a 1-year delay, and gaps too long cost less than gaps too short **[Strong-ish; word facts]** — [Cepeda et al. 2008](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf).
- **Skills decay at measurable rates [2024-26].** A Psychological Bulletin 2025 meta-analysis (1,344 effect sizes, 457 reports; motor-component procedural skills, not math) found performance differences growing 0.08 SD per month for accuracy and 0.06 for speed and mixed measures; half of gains were lost after about 6.5 months (accuracy), 13 (speed), 11 (mixed) **[Moderate; scope differs]** — [PubMed 40455501](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40455501/).
- **Skill-level temporal models on math logs.** DAS3H (skills per item, counts of earlier correct/attempts in time windows of 1/24, 1, 7, 30 days and forever) beat DASH, AFM, PFA and IRT on three math datasets (algebra05 AUC 0.826 vs best DASH 0.775); the authors proposed but did not test skill-review scheduling and warned that very early reactivation can hurt long-term retention and that an item involving only the target skill may not exist **[Moderate]** — [DAS3H, arXiv 1905.06873](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.06873).
- **ASSISTments.** Skill Builders require 3 correct in a row with a cap of about 10 questions; the ARRS adds one random question 7, 14, 28 and 56 days later, and a miss sends the student back through the Skill Builder **[Vendor/non-profit]** — [ASSISTments blog](https://www.assistments.org/blog-posts/how-assistments-skillbuilders-works-in-my-class-and-why-it-will-work-for-you-too). Its research post reports a randomized trial of reassessment and relearning with 97 eighth graders over 32 skills, with no effect sizes given — [ASSISTments research post](https://www.assistments.org/blog-posts/our-research-reassessing-and-relearning). Earlier notes recorded further ASSISTments RCT effects of about +0.10 and +0.18 SD for related interventions (not re-verified). — [practice_platforms.md](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Practice%20systems%20and%20learning%20science/practice_platforms.md)
- **ALEKS.** Retention is measured by extra problems on already-learned topics; across 8,352,006 such problems from 6,701,233 students (2016-2019) forgetting curves leveled near 0.6 (inner layer 1), 0.67 (layer 2) and 0.8 (layers 5 and up); a topic missed in a knowledge check moves back out of "mastered" and "learned" **[Vendor]** — [ALEKS ESSA document](https://www.mheducation.com/content/dam/mhe/research/prek-12/essa-tier-4/aleks-practical-perspective-knowledge-space-theory.pdf), [ALEKS support](https://www.mheducation.com/support/aleks-support-center/knowledge/why-did-my-number-of-mastered-or-learned-topics-go-down-after-a-knowledge-check.html). An adaptive-spaced-education RCT in 62 medical students reported 38% greater efficiency with comparable scores **[Moderate, small]** — [Kerfoot RCT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20022032/).
- **Math Academy FIRe.** Per-topic repetition counts and a memory value updated by a "raw delta" that is positive on success and negative on failure, scaled by quality of work and discounted when early; implicit credit flows down an encompassing graph; no validation is published and the exact algorithm is not given **[Vendor]** — [Skycak, FIRe](https://www.justinmath.com/individualized-spaced-repetition-in-hierarchical-knowledge-structures/), [Hecker, part 7](https://frankhecker.com/2025/02/14/math-academy-part-7/). Its scheduler "relies on the first-order signal: accuracy" — [Skycak, Spaced Repetition 2.0](https://justinmath.com/the-vision-for-version-2-of-math-academy-s-spaced-repetition-system/).
- **FSRS's own position.** FSRS treats cards independently; its wiki's composite-memory derivation (R = Ra x Rb, S = Sa x Sb / (Sa + Sb)) favours atomic skills; no FSRS-author statement on skills was found **[Practitioner]** — [gap audit notes](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Spaced%20repetition%20algorithm%20gap%20audit/fsrs_in_anki.md).
- **Knowledge-tracing comparisons.** See Section 0: model choice is inconclusive; logistic regression with time windows is competitive on moderate data. — [Pelánek](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/umuai-overview.pdf), [Gervet et al.](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/view/451)

### Inferences
- The spacing effect itself is the best-supported ingredient; it is what makes a scheduler of any kind (FSRS, a ladder, DAS3H) defensible at skill level. FSRS adds a specific functional form (power-law forgetting, stability growth that depends on R and difficulty) that was fitted on flashcards. With fresh problems, correctness depends on memory and on how hard this problem is relative to the skill, so the FSRS label "Good/Again" is a noisy measure of R. The spec's mitigation (core-band problems only, strict labels, Elo kept out of FSRS) is the right type of mitigation; a combination such as P(correct) = sigma(logit R + (theta - d)) is the DAS3H-style way to make the item-difficulty part explicit (my proposal, not from a source).
- ASSISTments' ladder is a legitimate baseline: it is simple and has a randomized trial behind it. It is also much more conservative than FSRS-6 at the spec's desired retention: with all-Good reviews on time, the default parameters give intervals of 2, 11, 46, 163 and 365 days at 0.90 (my computation, py-fsrs 6.3.2, whole days), against the ladder's 7, 14, 28, 56; the ladder's pace is closer to FSRS at about 0.95 (1, 3, 8, 19, 43, 89, 175). So the long FSRS intervals (46 and 163 days after only three or four successes) are extrapolations from flashcard-trained defaults that no skill-level outcome data validates; this is the first place a poor fit would show up as early lapses, and the reason the ENG-11 calibration bins and the ADR-0013 fitter matter more here than for cards.
- Donovan & Radosevich is the strongest statement against assuming that spacing gains carry over unchanged to complex multi-step skills; Murray 2025 and the calculus studies point the other way for course mathematics. The honest reading is that spacing helps and the size is uncertain.
- Recommended test (zero extra student burden): after about 300 skill-level review events (about 1,100 rated reviews are already planned for the DEV-16 experiment), score on the app's own log (a) FSRS-6 with default and fitted parameters, (b) a fixed expanding ladder as a predictor of recall (recall at the ladder's due dates), and (c) a DAS3H-lite logistic model with the same window counts, using time-ordered folds and the existing log-loss/RMSE(bins) code. If (a) is not better than (b) and (c) after adjustment, the spec's own adapter design allows swapping the scheduler. Be aware of the Lucas critique: scoring on logs generated by FSRS favours FSRS, so also keep a small skill-level randomized arm if the DEV-16 sample allows.

### Gaps
- No study tests FSRS, HLR or DASH as a scheduler for problem-solving skills with fresh items and a delayed measure; ALEKS and Math Academy results are vendor-reported without independent replication; the ASSISTments post gives no effect sizes or p-values; the Latimier et al. (2021) meta-analysis of spaced retrieval could not be retrieved; no variability-of-practice meta-analysis was retrievable, so "fresh problem vs same problem at review" has no direct evidence here.

## 2. Desired retention by goal (A 0.90, B 0.88, Keep 0.80) and the Elo 0.80 target, versus the "85% rule" and desirable difficulty (ENG-1, ENG-5; ADR-0014 "Problem selection")

### Takeaway
The retention values sit inside the only controlled range found (practice scheduled to a recall target of .86 to .94 beat .40, .98 and a fixed schedule) and match Anki's default, but the 0.02 gap between A and B and the choice of 0.80 for Keep are policy, not evidence. The 85% rule is theory with no human data and cannot support a 0.80 success target; ADR-0014 already changed rated reviews to aim at the course's desired retention (0.90 under goal A) and kept 0.80 only for learning phases and unrated stretch, but ENG-5's text still says 0.80. Verdict: **Weakly supported**.

### Cited Findings
- **Spec.** Desired retention A 0.90, B 0.88, Keep 0.80 (ENG-1); within the band the Elo model "picks the problem whose predicted success is closest to 0.80" (ENG-5); ADR-0014: "`Engine.pick_problem` aims rated reviews at the course's desired retention for the day (0.90 under goal A) and keeps 0.80 for learning phases and stretch." — [spec ENG-1, ENG-5](/docs/spec.md), [ADR-0014](/docs/decisions/0014-honest-grading-and-queue-parity.md)
- **Controlled evidence on a recall target.** Eglington & Pavlik 2020 (291 adults after exclusions, 30 Japanese-English pairs for 22 minutes, test 3 days later): practicing the item whose predicted recall was closest to, but below, a threshold of .86 or .94 beat thresholds of .40 and .98 and a fixed spaced schedule (mean recall about .48 and .47); .94 gave about 40% higher retention than the fixed schedule; an inverted-U pattern **[Moderate; lab, word pairs, 3-day delay]** — [Eglington & Pavlik 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7567101).
- **Practitioner guidance.** Anki's manual: default 90%, "above 90% the workload increases very quickly, and above 97% the workload can be overwhelming" **[Convergent practice]** — [Anki manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html). Practitioners advise raising desired retention "by a point or two" before an exam rather than capping intervals — [Anki forum exam thread](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/exam-specific-add-on-or-help-max-review-date/67433/2). One user's simulator output gave minutes/day of 19.8 (70%), 31.1 (79%), 45 (85%), 57.2 (88%), 76.2 (91%), 114.5 (94%), 230.3 (97%) **[Practitioner; one deck]** — [Anki forum workload thread](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/replace-cmrr-with-workload-vs-dr-graph-more/63234).
- **Overshooting is cheaper than undershooting.** Cepeda et al. 2008 as above — [Cepeda et al.](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf).
- **The 85% rule is theory.** Wilson et al. derive an optimal training error near 15.87% for binary classification learned by gradient descent; the preprint reports no human data, and for Bayesian learners there was no comparable sweet spot **[Weak as support for human practice]** — [Wilson et al., bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/255182v1.full).
- **Engagement-oriented difficulty targets.** In Papoušek & Pelánek's work, perceived appropriate difficulty peaked near 65% real success and satisfaction was highest for targets near 60-65% (about 70% real success); no learning outcomes were measured **[Moderate for engagement, [None] for retention]** — [Papoušek & Pelánek, AIED 2015](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/aied15.pdf). Pelánek's item-selection function targets 0.75 and his UMUAI paper says values around 65% may be a suitable target rate — [EDM 2014](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/EDM14-adaptive-facts.pdf), [UMUAI adaptive practice](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/umuai-adaptive-practice.pdf). In Jansen et al. (207 children, 6 weeks), higher pre-set success rates led to more problems attempted and larger gains in math performance (the abstract page gives no specific rates) **[Moderate]** — [Jansen et al. 2013](https://dare.uva.nl/id/789fc422-4935-49ca-bcba-803239522951). Math Academy says it tunes quizzes to average about 80% **[Vendor]** — [Math Academy, how our AI works](https://mathacademy.com/how-our-ai-works).
- **Feedback changes the picture.** Rowland 2014 (61 studies, 159 effect sizes, g = 0.50): without feedback, tests with initial success of 50% or less gave g = 0.03 and above 75% gave g = 0.56; with feedback g = 0.73 **[Strong]** — [Rowland 2014](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf). The app always shows the solution afterwards.
- **FSRS tooling treats DR as a cost trade-off, not a truth.** The "Compute minimum recommended retention" feature, the only place review time was used, was removed with FSRS-6 **[Practitioner]** — [Anki forum quoting the FSRS FAQ](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/does-anki-take-into-account-average-and-total-time-for-each-card-for-scheduling/70714); the manual's FSRS simulator estimates reviews or minutes per day for a chosen retention instead — [Anki manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html).

| 0.92 | 0.0138 | 1.17x | 0.957 | 8.0% | 0.918 |
| 0.95 | 0.0186 | 1.57x | 0.974 | 5.2% | 0.948 |

  Reading: 0.88 costs about 20% fewer reviews than 0.90 for about 1.8 points of mean recall (0.85 and 0.88 are indistinguishable here because intervals are whole days and capped at 365); Keep at 0.80 saves about a third for about 5.6 points; 0.95 costs about 1.6 times the 0.90 workload in steady state (1.7-1.9 times over a 120-day semester window in earlier runs). The spec's statement that 0.95 all semester "costs about twice the daily work" is about right for a semester window, a little high for steady state. The all-Good interval ladder under the default parameters (whole days, cap 365) is 2, 11, 46, 163, 365 days at 0.90; 3, 18, 86, 334, 365 at 0.88; 8, 75, 365 at 0.80; and 1, 3, 8, 19, 43, 89, 175 at 0.95. These curves depend on the default parameters (trained on flashcards) and a pure-Bernoulli learner; real learners with fresh problems will differ.
- **The A/B/Keep numbers are policy.** The only controlled study found (word pairs, 3-day test) says the .86-.94 band is good and the extremes are worse; .90 and .88 are both inside it, so no evidence separates them. Keep = 0.80 is below that band; it trades more forgetting for about a third less work, which is a policy choice for a "keep it warm" goal.
- **What the 0.80 means.** The Elo target decides what a Good/Again label measures. If a review problem is picked so that predicted success is 0.80 while FSRS expects R about 0.90, failures partly measure problem difficulty and FSRS will read them as forgetting, lowering stability and raising workload. ADR-0014's rule (aim rated reviews at the desired retention) removes that mismatch for rated events, which is the better-supported design; the engagement evidence (65-75%) concerns practice sessions, not the measurement of retention, and no study tests a success-rate target against delayed math retention. For the learning phase and unrated stretch, 0.75-0.80 is within the engagement literature, and Rowland's feedback result says low success is not harmful when solutions are shown.
- **Recommendation.** (a) Keep A 0.90; present B and Keep as workload choices, showing the curve above on the goal screen. (b) Update ENG-5 text to say "rated reviews aim at the course's desired retention; learning phases and stretch aim at 0.80", citing ADR-0014. (c) Add a DEV-16 arm for the learning-phase and stretch target (for example 0.70 vs 0.80), analysed on strict success at the next review. (d) Do not cite the 85% rule anywhere in user-facing text.

### Gaps
- No human test of the 85% rule or of success-rate targets against delayed retention in math problem practice was retrieved (the search that would have found one was refused). Jansen's specific success rates, and a summary claim that in-school students preferred about 75%, could not be verified from the retrieved pages. Eglington & Pavlik tested word pairs, not problems, and did not report .70 as different from the top conditions in the retrieved extract. The Anki workload numbers come from one user's deck.

## 3. Learning phase (worked example first, adaptive in-a-row gate within eight problems) and parking (ENG-2)

### Takeaway
Starting every new skill with a worked example is well supported (worked-example meta-analysis g about 0.43-0.48; a randomized field trial with about half the problems replaced by examples), and practising fresh problems until a short run of correct answers matches what ASSISTments (3 in a row, cap about 10), Math Academy (2 in a row) and ALEKS (target score 5, or 3 when fast-tracked) do; but the 2/3/4 gate sizes, the cap of 8 and the 3-day park are design values, and the one randomized test of a higher bar (ALEKS, 5 vs 3) found retention differences mostly under 0.02, so the gate matters little. Verdict: **Supported** for worked-example-first; **Weakly supported** for the gate, cap and parking.

### Cited Findings
- **What the spec does.** A new skill starts with a worked example (an `example` card, or a tier 1 problem shown solved), then fresh tier 1-2 problems, at most eight, until 2 in a row are correct without hints (first attempt clean), 3 after any miss, 4 for skills in the top quarter of lapse rate once 30 or more course skills are learned, never more than the number of distinct eligible problems; the FSRS card is created by replaying the attempts (first interval about 2 days); on failure the phase stops, prerequisites get quick checks, and it restarts next day with the worked example; after a second failed phase the skill is parked for 3 study days and listed under Weak areas; time is never judged. The app logs which rule fired and compares strict success at the next review; the adaptive gate is the first DEV-16 experiment. — [spec ENG-2, DEV-16](/docs/spec.md)
- **Worked examples.** A meta-analysis of worked examples in mathematics (43 articles, 55 studies, 181 effect sizes) reports g = 0.48; an earlier poster version reported g = 0.43 [0.31, 0.55] from 49 studies and 185 effects, and a follow-up paper notes smaller effects when examples included self-explanation prompts (beta = -0.24) **[Strong, with an unresolved 0.43 vs 0.48 discrepancy]** — [Barbieri et al., Kent summary](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance), [UDel follow-up](https://udspace.udel.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/775c29a4-17fa-418d-b7af-a1595b0f8b33/content), [published version](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1). In AlgebraByExample (randomized, more than 6,000 students and 130 teachers), assignments mixing worked examples and problems about 50/50 raised struggling students' conceptual knowledge by about 10 points and everyone's procedural knowledge by about 4 points despite half as many problems solved **[Strong, field scale]** — [SERP Institute](https://serpinstitute.org/algebra-by-example/research).
- **Problem-first is for concepts.** Productive failure meta-analysis (53 studies, 166 comparisons): g = 0.36 overall, with the advantage in conceptual knowledge and transfer rather than procedures **[Moderate]** — [Sinha & Kapur 2021 abstract](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.3102/00346543211019105?fields=title,year,venue,abstract,citationCount).
- **Higher threshold, small and shrinking gain [2024-26].** In a randomized ALEKS test of two mastery bars on new topics (high = score 5, low = score 3; April 2023 to November 2024; learning data about 33 million points; retention data 1,404,725 points from 806,279 students), the high bar cost about 1.5 minutes more per topic (6.6 vs 5.1) and about two more actions, retention differences were mostly under 0.02 and narrowed over time, reaching about 0.03-0.04 for students who struggled first; the topic-level gain was 0.021 on average and strongly related to low retention under the low bar (r = -0.86) **[Moderate; vendor-run randomized]** — [Matayoshi et al. 2025, JEDM](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/900/254). An older comparison (Kelly et al. 2015, cited there) found large differences between 3 and 5 consecutive correct answers with only 56 students and no long-term test **[Weak]** — [same paper](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/900/254).
- **Mastery-criterion choice.** Data sources and thresholds matter more than the modelling technique, and a simple exponential moving average is a suitable mastery criterion — [Řihák & Pelánek](https://www.ics.muni.cz/en/research/publications/1448676) **[Moderate]**.
- **Extra in-session repetitions add little; spaced return adds a lot.** Rohrer & Taylor: 3 vs 9 problems in one session had no effect at 1 or 4 weeks, while splitting across sessions a week apart had an "extremely large" effect at 4 weeks — [Rohrer & Taylor](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1770/). Rawson & Dunlosky (three experiments, 533 students, conceptual material): the effects of initial criterion and relearning were subadditive, relearning had "pronounced effects on long-term retention with a relatively minimal cost", prescription: initial criterion of 3 correct recalls then relearn about 3 times, widely spaced **[Strong for declarative material]** — [Rawson & Dunlosky 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023956). In a statistics-course study, distributed overlearning improved retention over practice to the mastery criterion (75% vs 65.3%) while fluency training added nothing (d = -0.02) **[Moderate; one course; figures from earlier-session notes]** — [Péladeau, Forget & Gagné 2003](https://provalisresearch.com/Download/AERJ.pdf).
- **Wheel spinning.** Zhang et al. 2019 (CMU tutor data): failing to get 3 correct in a row within the first 10 opportunities flagged 6.6%, 0.56% and 10.2% of student-skill pairs in three datasets; a Bayesian-knowledge-tracing-based definition flagged 24.2%, 2.17% and 13.2%; agreement between the two definitions was under 50%; early detection was poor (under 20% precision and recall in the first three steps for the 3-in-a-row definition); no intervention was tested **[Moderate]** — [Zhang et al. 2019](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED594575.pdf).
- **Products.** ASSISTments: 3 in a row, cap of "under 10" questions, then "try again tomorrow" **[Vendor]** — [ASSISTments](https://www.assistments.org/blog-posts/how-assistments-skillbuilders-works-in-my-class-and-why-it-will-work-for-you-too). Math Academy: a worked example, then up to 5 practice problems, two in a row advances, too many wrong halts the lesson to be retried later after unrelated work **[Vendor]** — [Math Academy](https://mathacademy.com/how-it-works). ALEKS: an item is "learned" at a target score, usually 5 (3 for fast-tracked items) **[Vendor]** — [ALEKS ESSA document](https://www.mheducation.com/content/dam/mhe/research/prek-12/essa-tier-4/aleks-practical-perspective-knowledge-space-theory.pdf).

### Inferences
- The evidence is lopsided: strong for the worked example, moderate for "a short run of correct answers is enough", and absent for the exact numbers. The ALEKS result is the best quantitative guide: going from a bar of 3 to a bar of 5 buys under 0.02-0.04 retention for 1.5 minutes. The spec's 2/3/4 range sits inside that zone, so the adaptive rule is mostly an efficiency device; a fixed 3-in-a-row would be equally defensible.
- "4 in a row for the top-quarter lapse-rate skills" follows the ALEKS finding that a higher bar helps mainly low-retention topics (r = -0.86); it is a hypothesis built from that pattern, not a tested rule.
- The bigger lever is the spaced return: ENG-3's next-study-day relearning and the roughly 2-day first interval match the finding that returning after a gap beats extra same-session trials.
- Parking: with a cap of 8 and two phases, the spec asks for up to 16 problems over two days before parking, which is conservative against false parking; but wheel-spinning prevalence depends strongly on definition (under 50% agreement), so the app should log how often skills are parked and what happens at re-entry.
- **Power of the planned experiment (my computation).** Randomizing gates at the skill level with about 1,100 rated reviews (about 550 per arm, strict success near 0.85) can detect only a difference of about 6 points at 80% power (two-sided alpha 0.05, ignoring clustering). The ALEKS data suggest the true difference is 2-4 points, so the first DEV-16 experiment will probably be inconclusive on retention; decide in advance that "no detectable difference" means keeping the cheaper gate, and compare time per learning phase as a second outcome.
- **Recommendation.** Keep ENG-2; keep the logging; record the minimum difference of interest (about 5 points) in the preregistration; optionally drop the 4-in-a-row tier unless the first semester's data show the high-lapse subgroup benefits.

### Gaps
- No study tests "worked example, then problems, restart next day with the example, park after two failed phases" as a package; wheel-spinning work tests no remedy; Doroudi (2020) and Kelly et al. (2015) were seen only as citations; the Péladeau, Forget & Gagné figures come from an earlier session and were not re-read; the bias-adjusted productive-failure estimate could not be verified; the Latimier et al. (2021) numbers on spaced retrieval were not retrievable.

## 4. Recall versus Level (ENG-10)

### Takeaway
Keeping "how likely you are to solve a fresh problem now" (Recall) apart from "what you have demonstrated" (Level) matches the learning-versus-performance literature and the way ALEKS (knowledge checks confer "mastered") and Khan Academy (mastery challenges) separate states, and strict in-session criteria still overstate delayed learning (ARTS); but the numeric Level criteria (two different days, a success after 7 or more days at predicted recall 0.90 or lower, a success above the exam tier or in a mixed check-up, median of the last three times at target) are design proposals with no validation. Verdict: **Weakly supported** (principle supported; criteria unvalidated).

### Cited Findings
- **What the spec does.** Learning / Learned / Exam-level / Mastered with the stated criteria; one miss marks an Exam-level or Mastered skill shaky, a second miss within its next three reviews drops one Level, a miss while Learned returns it to Learning; "fading" (recall under 0.80) is a Recall warning, not a Level; a mixed check-up about every three weeks can satisfy Mastered and its misses count as normal lapses. — [spec ENG-10](/docs/spec.md)
- **Performance is not learning.** Performance during training is often an unreliable index of whether learning occurred, and learners mistake current performance for lasting learning **[Strong; integrative review]** — [Soderstrom & Bjork 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25910388/).
- **Strict in-session criteria still overstate [Moderate].** Even with a retirement rule of 5 of the last 5 correct, all under 7 seconds, delayed-test accuracy gains were well below immediate gains (adaptive 0.85 immediate vs 0.52 delayed); in the first experiment a random schedule with about twice the trials showed larger raw delayed gains (0.76 vs 0.52) although the adaptive schedule was more efficient — [Mettler et al., CogSci 2020](https://cognitivesciencesociety.org/cogsci20/papers/0138/0138.pdf). On a one-week delayed test of 24 African countries, response-time-based adaptive scheduling (ARTS) scored 0.42 against 0.31 and 0.30 for fixed expanding and equal schedules, but 0.42 vs 0.326 against a yoked control was not significant (p = .16) — [Mettler, Massey & Kellman 2016](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2025/09/mettler_massey_kellman_2016.pdf).
- **Convergent practice.** ALEKS: "mastered" counts topics demonstrated in a Knowledge Check (after 20 topics learned plus 5 hours, after 10 hours, or after 60 days without one); a topic missed there moves back out of mastered or learned — [McGraw Hill support](https://www.mheducation.com/support/aleks-support-center/knowledge/why-did-my-number-of-mastered-or-learned-topics-go-down-after-a-knowledge-check.html), [UCSC explanation](https://mathcoach.sites-test.ucsc.edu/success/learned_and_mastered). Khan Academy Mastery Challenges: 6 questions over 3 skills, both correct levels up, both wrong levels down, unlocked at least 12 hours after the last — [Khan Academy Help](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/360037494231). RemNote requires two successful recalls for new cards and two in a row for "shaky" cards after an error — [RemNote](https://help.remnote.com/en/articles/9102040-understanding-the-exam-scheduler) **[Convergent practice / Vendor]**.
- **Mastery learning as a concept.** Slavin 1987 (17 studies of at least four weeks): median effect +0.04 on standardized measures and +0.255 on experimenter-made measures, retention effects "essentially zero", "mastery learning trades coverage for mastery" — [Slavin 1987](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/1987-slavin.pdf); Kulik et al. 1990 (108 evaluations): ES 0.52, larger for stricter criteria (91-100%: 0.80), 0.10-0.33 on standardized tests (figures from earlier-session notes) **[Strong; effects concentrated on criterion-referenced tests]** — [earlier notes](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Practice%20systems%20and%20learning%20science/science_of_practice.md).
- **Speed and accuracy decay at different rates [2024-26].** In procedural skills with motor components, half of the gains were lost after about 6.5 months (accuracy) and about 13 months (speed) **[Moderate; different scope]** — [Psychological Bulletin 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40455501/).

### Inferences
- Level does not change the FSRS schedule in the spec, so a mis-specified Level mainly risks false assurance or discouragement, not poor scheduling; that lowers the stakes.
- The delayed-and-mixed evidence requirement ("a success after 7 or more days at predicted recall 0.90 or lower" and "above exam tier or mixed check-up") is the part with convergent support (ALEKS, Khan, Soderstrom & Bjork, ARTS delayed gains). The count-based parts (two different days, three successful reviews) are conventional; RemNote's two-in-a-row is the closest analogue.
- The pace conditions are the weakest part; see Section 8. Because speed gains seem to persist longer than accuracy gains in other procedural domains (different scope), a Level with a pace condition may outlast Recall; treat as a hypothesis.
- **Recommendation.** Before adding criteria, test whether Level adds information beyond Recall: with logs from practice tests and real exams (STA-12), compare predicted success on exam-tier problems from (a) R alone, (b) R plus tier, (c) R plus tier plus Level; if (c) is no better, collapse Exam-level and Mastered. Show the evidence behind each Level on the skill page (the spec already intends this).

### Gaps
- No study validates a Level ladder against exam outcomes; ALEKS's own knowledge-check accuracy is vendor-reported; cumulative or mixed check-up evidence (how often, how many skills) was not retrieved; Bahrick's long-term mathematics retention work was available only through a news summary and is not used.

## 5. Core band, difficulty ladder, Elo difficulty model and cold start (ENG-4, ENG-5)

### Takeaway
An Elo-style rating for choosing practice items is well supported (basic Elo tracks IRT-style estimates at r = 0.97 and richer variants add little) and anchoring new items to their tier is the right response to Elo's need for about 100 learners per item; but the stability-to-band table (tiers 1-2 under 7 days, up to tiers 3-4 above 90 days), the 10% stretch cap, the tier anchors (-1.5 to 2.5) and the first-seen-versus-repeated threshold (15 points over 30 pairs) are design values with no direct evidence. Verdict: Elo method **Supported**; band table and anchors **Unsupported but testable**; overall **Weakly supported**.

### Cited Findings
- **What the spec does.** Only core-band problems produce FSRS events; band by stability: under 7 days tiers 1-2 (stretch 3), 7-30 tiers 2-3 (stretch 4), 30-90 tier 3 (stretch 4), over 90 tiers 3-4 (stretch 5 if Elite is on); stretch at most 10% of reviews; inside an exam window in-scope skills use tier 3. Elo: P = logistic(theta - d), theta updated by a/(1 + b n)(o - P) with a = 1, b = 0.05; difficulty starts at the tier value (-1.5, -0.5, 0.5, 1.5, 2.5), moves at most 0.05 per answer, and a run of surprising results flags a possible wrong tier; Elo never changes the FSRS schedule. Stretch is unrated (ADR-0014) and the skill's rated review is owed the next study day. — [spec ENG-4, ENG-5](/docs/spec.md), [ADR-0014](/docs/decisions/0014-honest-grading-and-queue-parity.md)
- **Elo is a sound item-selection model.** Elo shares the Rasch prediction function; the recommended uncertainty function is a/(1 + b n) with a = 1, b = 0.05 **[Moderate]** — [Pelánek 2016, Computers & Education](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf). Basic Elo matched joint maximum-likelihood IRT estimates (r = 0.97); richer prior-knowledge models gave only small RMSE gains (0.4142 basic, 0.4115 hierarchical, 0.4118 networked) — [Pelánek et al., UMUAI](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/umuai-adaptive-practice.pdf).
- **Cold start.** New items start at difficulty 0; "just 10 answers per student are sufficient to produce reasonable skill estimates" (r = 0.8 in simulation); "the system needs at least 100 students to get good estimates of item difficulty"; proportion-correct estimates are poor under adaptive selection; a single global skill can be too coarse — [Pelánek 2016](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf). Basic Elo is "probably not sufficient" for modelling prerequisites; Bayesian networks are suggested — [Pelánek et al., UMUAI](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/umuai-adaptive-practice.pdf).
- **Selection functions in practice.** Pelánek's fact-practice selection uses a target success of 0.75 with recency and count terms — [Papoušek, Pelánek & Stanislav 2014](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/EDM14-adaptive-facts.pdf); see Section 2 for target-rate evidence.
- **Learning curves can detect over-practice.** Using learning-curve analysis to stop assigning problems on already-mastered skills saved about 12% of time with no loss in post-test or retention **[Moderate]** — [Cen et al., LearnLab](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Using_learning_curves_to_optimize_problem_assignment).
- **LLM-simulated item difficulty [2024-26].** A 2026 preprint reports correlations of about 0.75, 0.76 and 0.82 between LLM-simulated and NAEP item difficulty estimates in three settings **[Weak, preprint]** — [arXiv 2601.09953](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09953v1).

### Inferences
- Elo is the right tool; the weak points are inputs. With one learner, difficulty cannot be learned (Pelánek's 100-learner figure), so the tier anchors carry all the item information; the "run of surprising results flags a wrong tier" rule is the right safeguard, and the spec's refusal to let one student's results move difficulty by more than 0.05 per answer is consistent with the literature.
- The band table's logic can be justified, not validated: Good/Again labels update stability, so a problem far easier than the skill's level makes Again nearly impossible (stability inflates), and one far harder makes Again spurious. Selecting problems so that predicted success is near the review's R (the ADR-0014 rule) addresses exactly this; the static table is then a coarse prior. A direct check is available from logs: calibration of predicted recall by tier (ENG-11 currently groups bins per course) should show tier-1 problems at stability above 30 days almost never failing if the table is too low.
- LLM-simulated difficulty could seed priors for new items, but the evidence is a 2026 preprint on large-scale assessments; at best give it a small weight.
- **Recommendation.** Keep Elo as specified; add per-tier calibration to STA; mark the band table as a parameter to evaluate with learning curves once enough reviews exist; keep stretch unrated.

### Gaps
- I found no source for the band-by-stability map (difficulty progression tied to memory strength), the 10% stretch cap, the tier anchors, or the 15-point first-seen versus repeated-form threshold; the 100-learner figure is Pelánek's statement for adaptive practice systems, not tested for step-tagged math problems; the LLM-difficulty result is unreplicated.

## 6. Prerequisite checks after a miss and partial credit through the graph, versus FIRe (ENG-6, ENG-7; ADR-0014 I9)

### Takeaway
Checking prerequisites after a miss is convergent practice (ALEKS, Math Academy, intelligent tutors) with only correlational support (prerequisite mastery predicts calculus grades) and no controlled test against alternatives; fractional implicit credit has only vendor support (FIRe) and no published validation, so keeping it in shadow mode is appropriate. However the rule that decides when credit goes On (credited log loss lower than plain on at least 200 held-out reviews, a point-estimate comparison in the code) is close to a coin flip: my calculation gives a 51% chance of switching on when credit is no better than plain, 13-30% when credit is biased or noisier, and only 68-84% when credit is really better. Verdict: ENG-6 **Weakly supported**; ENG-7 **Unsupported outside vendor claims**; the switch rule needs a margin and more data.

### Cited Findings
- **What the spec does.** A check is a tier 1-2 problem of the tagged skill and counts as a real review (pass Good, fail Again); when a foundational skill lapses, skills encompassing it with weight 0.5 or more get their next review pulled earlier, never their stability cut (ENG-6). Credit: S_B' = S_B + w(S_B^Good - S_B) at B's fractional-day recall, last-review time not reset (so early credit is tiny), no credit to skills learning, relearning, failed in 7 days or rated very difficult, forced explicit review when stability has doubled from credit, chain products under 0.25 ignored, credit never trains the optimizer, Shadow until "about 200 explicit reviews of credited skills show the credited predictions are at least as well calibrated" (ENG-7). ADR-0014 (I9): at most one credit per skill per study day; credit On only when `credit_switch` finds credited recalls beat plain ones on log loss over at least 200 held-out reviews. The code allows credit when n >= 200 and credited log loss < plain log loss, with no margin or significance test. — [spec ENG-6, ENG-7](/docs/spec.md), [ADR-0014](/docs/decisions/0014-honest-grading-and-queue-parity.md), [credit.py](/core/engine/diagnosis/credit.py)
- **Prerequisites matter (correlational) [Moderate].** In more than 6,000 college freshmen at 133 institutions, "a firm grip on precalculus subjects had twice the impact of a high school [calculus] course" on college calculus grade (ratio only; how mastery was measured is not stated) — [Harvard Gazette summary of Sadler & Sonnert 2018](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2018/07/masters-of-calculus-come-prepared-harvard-study-shows).
- **Practitioner views.** "If a step was the failure point, you'd probably benefit more from doing a completely separate problem" **[Practitioner]** — [Anki forum](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/questions-about-tuning-fsrs-for-math-logical-reasoning-problem-cards/70949). The confirmatory diagnostic set and the "probe low-recall prerequisites, deepest first" procedure behind ENG-15 and the post-confirmation checks were an earlier note's own proposals (tagged "Own proposal"), not published methods — [error_diagnosis_tagging.md](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Toishi%20gaps%20research/error_diagnosis_tagging.md).
- **Self-diagnosis is shaky.** In an algebra-based physics course, self-diagnosis scores on a hard quiz were 0.73 with a TA outline plus rubric, 0.57 with a worked example and 0.24 with only the final answer, notes and textbook; about 60% of students could not correctly apply principles they had invoked; "the SD grade does not necessarily imply meaningful SD" **[Moderate]** — [Mason et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1603.03070); weaker learners struggle to explain errors unless the wrong step is highlighted — [LearnLab, corrective self-explanation](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Corrective_self-explanation); bug-level diagnoses were unstable across items, skill-level diagnoses more stable **[Moderate]** — [Birenbaum, Kelly & Tatsuoka 1992](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/1992/hxjo.html).
- **FIRe.** An encompassing graph separate from the prerequisite graph; weights are the fraction of the simpler topic practiced, set explicitly only if nontrivial, not inferable from repetition flow, and close in the prerequisite graph; credit flows down, penalties flow up; weights multiply along chains (0.5 x 0.4 = 0.2), distant topics are zero; early repetitions are discounted; for low-speed topics incoming implicit credit is discarded and explicit reviews forced; a due repetition is not deferred to wait for an implicit opportunity; no formula for the discount and no validation are published **[Vendor]** — [Skycak, FIRe](https://www.justinmath.com/individualized-spaced-repetition-in-hierarchical-knowledge-structures/), [archived copy](https://gwern.net/doc/www/www.justinmath.com/9af2ce40376e836660c237a0e971d1896a163eee.html), [Hecker, part 7](https://frankhecker.com/2025/02/14/math-academy-part-7/).
- **Attempts elsewhere.** A forum user reports that the FSRS contributor Expertium tried raising a card's stability when a sibling was reviewed ("partial review"); no result was published — [Anki forum](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/interval-determination-of-siblings/49267) **[Practitioner, second-hand]**. DAS3H learns the contribution of each skill's practice history from data rather than asserting weights, and warns that very early reactivation can hurt long-term retention — [DAS3H](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.06873) **[Moderate]**. Gaps shorter than optimal cost more than gaps longer than optimal (consistent with ENG-7's "credit arriving early is tiny") — [Cepeda et al. 2008](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf).

### Inferences
- **ENG-6 tension with ENG-4 (spec-internal).** A check is a tier 1-2 problem that "counts as a real review", but for a skill whose stability is above 30 days the core band is tier 3 or higher, and ENG-4 says only core-band problems produce FSRS events. A pass on an easy check will inflate stability; a fail is informative. QUE-10 resolves the same issue for the Lighter set by counting successes as Hard; the same asymmetric rule (below-band passes count as Hard or have no effect, fails count fully) is the consistent fix.
- **The credit switch is underpowered (my computation).** Simulated paired log-loss comparison for reviews of credited skills (true success about N(0.85, 0.06); "credit exact" means the credited prediction equals the true probability and plain is lower by delta):

| Scenario | P(code rule passes), n = 200 | P(one-sided paired test p < .05), n = 200 | P(code rule passes), n = 800 | P(test passes), n = 800 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Credit exact, plain off by 0.02 | 0.68 | 0.16 | 0.80 | 0.23 |

- FIRe's design (discount early credit, discard credit on slow topics, force explicit reviews) is mirrored by the spec's safeguards; the safeguards are sensible hedges, but they are copies of an unvalidated design.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or independent test of fractional implicit repetition, or of post-miss prerequisite checks versus alternatives, was found; Math Academy's formula details and validation are unpublished; the sibling-credit experiment's outcome is unreported; the Mason et al. data are from physics courses, not calculus.

## 7. Exam boosters and projected exam-day recall (ENG-8, ENG-17; practice-test ladder)

### Takeaway
Reviewing with a specific test date in mind is supported in principle (the optimal gap scales with the test delay; overshooting costs less than undershooting; practice tests help), and the "0.4 x new stability" figure is an exact consequence of FSRS-6's curve at a 0.95 target (my check), but the 21-day window, the 0.95 target, the level-dependent targets, the priority formula and the accuracy of the projected exam-day recall have no outcome evidence. In a model-internal simulation, boosters bring about 97-99% of skills up to the target on exam day for 11-17% fewer total reviews than running at 0.95 all along, but they concentrate the cost in the last three weeks (about six times the window load in a mature course). Verdict: principle **Supported**; constants and projection **Unsupported**; overall **Weakly supported**.

### Cited Findings
- **What the spec does.** From 21 days before an exam, daily prediction of each in-scope skill's recall at exam time if not reviewed; skills already due before the exam are left alone; for one due after the exam whose predicted exam-day recall misses the goal's target, a booster is scheduled on "the latest day from which a Good review keeps exam-day recall at the target", spread evenly over the window; for a 0.95 target that is "within about 0.4 times the skill's new stability of the exam: 4 days for a 10-day stability, 16 days for 40"; a failed booster relearns and is re-planned next day; practice tests from 21 days out, graded at the end, their results count as reviews and every miss is Again (QUE-19); boosters are ordered by priority = w x (1 - projected exam-day R) x (1 + 30-day lapse rate) (ENG-17). Goal presets: A everyday 0.90 and exam-day 0.95; B 0.88 and 0.92; Keep 0.80 and none, "starting values, tuned from his own data after a semester". ADR-0006 resolves the wording: a booster's window runs from the first day a Good review keeps exam-day recall at the target to the day before the exam, boosters are load-balanced over their windows, ties go to the earlier day (room to re-plan after a failed booster), the plan is recomputed daily, and a custom goal's exam-day target is min(0.95, r + 0.05). — [spec ENG-8, ENG-17, goal presets](/docs/spec.md), [ADR-0006](/docs/decisions/0006-learning-engine-core.md)
- **Gap scales with the test delay.** Cepeda et al. 2008 (1,354 participants; gaps up to 3.5 months; tests up to a year): the optimal gap fell from about 20-40% of a 1-week test delay to about 5-10% of a 1-year delay; gaps too long cost less than too short **[Strong-ish; word facts]** — [Cepeda et al. 2008](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/).
- **Closed form.** FSRS-6: R(t, S) = (1 + F t/S)^(-w20), F = 0.9^(-1/w20) - 1, interval for retention r: I(r, S) = (S/F)(r^(-1/w20) - 1). — [awesome-fsrs wiki](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/The-Algorithm)
- **Practitioner advice.** "You shouldn't try to control your intervals based on an exam deadline"; raise desired retention "by a point or two" and use filtered decks near the exam — [Anki forum](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/exam-specific-add-on-or-help-max-review-date/67433/2). RemNote adds extra reviews alongside the main scheduler, a catch-up period that "proposes a temporary increase to your daily goal rather than silently rearranging", and a final review period **[Vendor]** — [RemNote](https://help.remnote.com/en/articles/9102040-understanding-the-exam-scheduler).
- **Testing effect.** Donoghue & Hattie 2021: practice testing d = 0.74 (374 effects), similar at all retention intervals; for mathematics alone, Murray et al. 2025 found testing vs restudy g = 0.18 with an interval crossing zero (7 studies, 32 effects), against a classroom math/statistics estimate of g = 0.433 [0.193, 0.674] from 27 effects (Yang 2021, earlier notes) **[Strong in general, contested for math] [2024-26]** — [Donoghue & Hattie](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/pdf), [Murray et al.](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/229807). Rowland 2014 (g = 0.50 overall, 0.73 with feedback) — [Rowland](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf).
- **Projection accuracy.** In a self-report survey of 399 USMLE Step 1 examinees, the best single practice tests had R-squared 0.66-0.68, the average gap between the best test and the real score was 6.9 points, and prediction errors on 19 held-out students ranged from -8.0 to +13.2 points **[Weak; self-report, medical exam]** — [Seal, Koek & Sharma 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7198101). No peer-reviewed evidence that practice-test scores predict real exam scores in college math was found — [earlier notes](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Practice%20systems%20and%20learning%20science/science_of_practice.md).
- **Practice tests and stress [Moderate, lab, critiqued].** Retrieval-practice learners showed no stress-related retrieval impairment, though a commentary notes they also performed worse overall and the finding needs validation with complex material — [Smith, Floerke & Thomas 2016](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aah5067), [Wolf & Kluge 2017](https://www.cog.psy.ruhr-uni-bochum.de/cog/mam/paper/2017/wolf_kluge_commentary-stress_retrievalpractice_frontbehavneurosci_2017_.pdf).
- **Mock-exam scoring is not a memory signal.** An earlier note cautions that FSRS rates single-skill recall while a mock exam mixes skills, speed and endurance, so mock results should drive diagnosis rather than the memory signal — [science_of_practice.md, Q4](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Practice%20systems%20and%20learning%20science/science_of_practice.md).

### Inferences
- **The 0.4 factor is correct (my computation).** With w20 = 0.1542, F = 0.9803 and I(0.95, S)/S = 0.4025, so a Good review leaves exam-day recall at 0.95 only if it happens within 0.4 times the new stability of the exam (4.0 days for S' = 10, 16.1 for S' = 40), matching the spec.
- **Wording.** The spec sentence "the latest day from which a Good review keeps exam-day recall at the target" is ambiguous: the feasible set is every day within 0.4 S' of the exam. ADR-0006 settles it (window from the first feasible day to the day before the exam, load-balanced, ties to the earlier day); the spec should say that instead, for example "a booster may be placed on any day from the earliest day on which a Good review still keeps exam-day recall at the target". My simulation always used the earliest feasible day.
- **Simulation (my computation).** Self-consistent FSRS-6 default learners, 300 skills, 30 seeds, Bernoulli(R) outcomes; booster policy as in ENG-8 (not-due-before-exam skills whose predicted exam-day R is below the target are reviewed on the first day a Good review would reach the target). |---|---|---|---|

| 0.95 all along | 2.62 | 0.977 | 97.8% |

  Exam on day 200 (mature course; skills learned over days 0-89; reviews counted from day 100):

  Reading: boosters deliver the target for about 97-99% of skills at roughly 11-17% fewer reviews than keeping 0.95 for the whole stretch, which is the efficiency the spec expects; the cost arrives in the last three weeks (about 6x in a mature course; 1.3x in a semester course whose skills were learned recently), so the goal preview should show the last-three-weeks load, and QUE-13's "propose a lighter goal" logic needs to apply to it. Results are cohort-dependent and assume the default parameters.
- **What cannot be defended.** The 21-day window, the 0.95 target, "0.92 for B", and min(0.95, r + 0.05) for custom goals are design values; the evidence that going from 0.90 to 0.95 on exam day changes exam scores is absent. The priority formula (weight x shortfall x error proneness) is a decision-theoretic heuristic: it ranks by expected points at risk, which is reasonable but untested.
- **Practice-test results as reviews.** If a mock-exam problem tests several skills, "every miss is Again" assigns lapses to the main tag without knowing which sub-skill failed, which adds noise to FSRS; the earlier note's caution applies. - **Projected exam-day recall.** It is a model output (R at exam time); no outcome study validates it, and the one external dataset (self-reported medical exams) shows single-test predictions miss by about 7 points. Keep the spec's range display and score the projection against actual exam results (STA-12) as DEV-16 plans.
- **Recommendation.** Reword ENG-8; show the last-three-weeks workload in the goal preview; keep the priority formula but log its inputs; treat the 0.95 target as a default to be tuned with real exam outcomes.

### Gaps
- No study ties a pre-exam booster schedule to exam scores in math; no retrieved evidence for 21 days or 0.95; cumulative-review (final-week) studies were not retrieved; Latimier et al. (2021) on spaced retrieval was not available; the simulation uses one parameter set and Bernoulli outcomes, ignores fuzz, and has no correlation between skills.

## 8. Target times and the slow-answer rule (ENG-13, ENG-14)

### Takeaway
Response time on correct answers carries information about memory strength (ARTS, adaptive fact learning, Pelánek's practice data), but adding it to a knowledge-tracing model improves fit only slightly, the evidence is from short items (seconds) rather than multi-minute worked solutions, FSRS itself uses grades and intervals only (and its documentation says Again/Good alone may fit better), and the 70%-of-median, floor times, 85%-of-exam-minutes and 2x/2.5x constants are the earlier note's labelled proposals, which the spec itself calls guesses. Verdict: RT as a signal **Weakly supported**; using time to assign Hard to schedule-relevant reviews **Unsupported**; speed in Level **Weakly supported** (convergent practice).

### Cited Findings
- **What the spec does.** Target time per skill and tier once Learned = max(floor 20/40/60/90/120 s, min(exam pace, 70% of the median of the first five correct answers after Learned)); exam pace is 85% of the exam's minutes spread over its problems, with single-skill tier 1-2 problems taking about a quarter to two fifths of an exam problem's time and tier 3-4 a half to four fifths; tightens by at most 10% per week; pace applies only when an exam length is entered (ENG-13). A correct answer counts as Hard only if its active time exceeds twice his median of log times for the skill and tier (2.5 times if recall at review was under 0.85) and exceeds the target; missing exam pace alone affects Level, not the schedule; raw times are stored so grades can be recomputed (ENG-14). — [spec ENG-13, ENG-14](/docs/spec.md)
- **Origin of the numbers.** The earlier research note tags the rule and its constants as "[Proposal]", states that all numbers "are starting guesses", and lists as gaps that no published FSRS benchmark tests response time as a feature or "slow-correct to Hard" against accuracy-only grading, that all RT-in-scheduling studies use short items (seconds), and that no research-based per-tier calculus time targets exist — [speed_mastery_goals.md, Sections 1 and 3](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Toishi%20gaps%20research/speed_mastery_goals.md).
- **RT as a strength signal.** ARTS treats RT as "a useful indicator of retrieval difficulty" and "at least a reasonable proxy for learning strength", treats RTs on incorrect answers as uninformative, and on a one-week delayed test scored 0.42 vs 0.31 and 0.30 for fixed schedules (not significant against a yoked control: 0.42 vs 0.326) **[Moderate; geography facts]** — [Mettler, Massey & Kellman 2016](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2025/09/mettler_massey_kellman_2016.pdf). In adaptive fact learning with 66 university students, the in-session estimated rate of forgetting (adjusted upward when a response was much slower than predicted) explained 62.4% of the variance in 3-day delayed recall, against 1.2% for working-memory capacity **[Moderate]** — [Sense et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2018.00112/pdf). In Pelánek's geography practice data, after a correct answer the chance the next answer was correct fell from about 95% (very fast) to about 80% (slow) — [Pelánek et al.](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/umuai-adaptive-practice.pdf).
- **But the incremental value is small.** Adding a learner's running median correct RT per knowledge component to Performance Factors Analysis raised cross-validated R-squared by only +0.0123 (multiple choice, 5 s limit), +0.0084 (short answer) and +0.0008 (statistics cloze) **[Moderate]** — [EDM 2023 short paper](https://educationaldatamining.org/edm2023/proceedings/2023.EDM-short-papers.22/index.html). Pelánek et al. found AFM a poor fit for programming because "response time carries information that binary correctness discards" — [AFM analysis](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/afm-analysis.pdf).
- **FSRS does not use time.** FSRS uses interval lengths and grades only; Hard multiplies the stability increase by w15 (< 1), so it still grows stability, more slowly **[Practitioner/technical]** — [Anki forum quoting FSRS FAQ](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/does-anki-take-into-account-average-and-total-time-for-each-card-for-scheduling/70714), [awesome-fsrs wiki](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/The-Algorithm). The py-fsrs optimizer's label is binary (Again = 0; Hard/Good/Easy = 1) and Anki's FAQ says FSRS "may even be more accurate if you only use Again and Good" — [py-fsrs optimizer](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-spaced-repetition/py-fsrs/main/fsrs/optimizer.py), [Anki FSRS FAQ](https://docs.ankiweb.net/faqs/frequently-asked-questions-about-fsrs). An Anki add-on that grades by answer time exists; its author no longer uses it and reads a linked thread as suggesting it is redundant with FSRS **[Practitioner]** — [Anki forum](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/pass-fail-review-automatic-difficulty-rating-based-on-answer-time-beta-ver-official-thread/53872.md).
- **Speed as a vendor practice.** Math Academy's FAQ: the system "will immediately assign review tasks on topics the student has answered incorrectly or exceeded the time limit"; yet Skycak's plan for version 2 states the current scheduler "relies on the first-order signal: accuracy" and will add timing later **[Vendor]** — [Math Academy FAQ](https://WWW.MATHACADEMY.COM/faq), [Skycak, Spaced Repetition 2.0](https://justinmath.com/the-vision-for-version-2-of-math-academys-spaced-repetition-system/).
- **Cautions.** Retrieval fluency during practice can mislead learners about later retention — [Benjamin, Bjork & Schwartz 1998](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Benjamin_RBjork_Schwartz_1998.pdf) **[Moderate]**; in a statistics course, fluency training beyond accuracy added nothing to retention (d = -0.02) **[Moderate; figure from earlier-session notes]** — [Péladeau, Forget & Gagné 2003](https://provalisresearch.com/Download/AERJ.pdf); harder but successful retrievals (for example after longer gaps) produced better final test performance, so a slow correct answer after a long interval can be a desirable difficulty **[Strong, secondary summary]** — [Pyc & Rawson via Matuschak](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z5zckzyAM1DwEQqt94qMpsd). NCTM (2023) says timed tests do not assess fluency and can harm students (mostly K-8) **[Practitioner]** — [NCTM](https://nctm.org/Standards-and-Positions/Position-Statements/Procedural-Fluency-in-Mathematics).
- **Speed gains can persist.** In Kellman's algebra module, recognition times fell about 29% from pre-test to post-test and about 36% to a 3-week delayed test, and solving-time gains were preserved **[Moderate]** — [Kellman et al. 2008](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2025/09/kellman_et_al_2008.pdf); this is the closest support for the "70% of own median" figure (a 30% improvement is in that range).

- **Cost of Hard (my computation).** With success drawn as Bernoulli(R) and slowness carrying no information about forgetting, grading a share h of passes Hard instead of Good (FSRS-6 defaults, desired retention 0.90, steady state, 16 seeds) changes workload and recall as follows:

### Gaps
- No FSRS benchmark with time-assigned Hard; no calculus-scale timing studies; no controlled evidence that timed practice transfers to timed exam performance for college calculus; the ARTS constants were not extracted; the Péladeau figures come from earlier-session notes; the Math Academy FAQ claim is a vendor statement.

## 9. Weak-spot finder (ENG-15)

### Takeaway
I found no published method or threshold for single-learner weak-spot detection; the rule's structure (30-day window, z of -2 or lower, at least five problems' worth, three misses on three problems and two days, then a 3-4 problem confirmation) is an earlier note's own proposal. Exact calculations show the screen is only weakly protective at five problems (z <= -2 is equivalent to three misses of five; the extra conditions add nothing there) and its realized false-flag rate varies from about 1% to 7% per evaluation, but the two-stage design limits the damage: in a 150-skill semester simulation about 16 false flags produced only about 2 false confirmations while about 75% of truly weak skills were flagged. A sequential confirmation rule has materially higher power for the same false-confirmation rate. Verdict: **Unsupported as specified (no evidence), but statistically workable with fixes**.

### Cited Findings
- **What the spec does.** Daily; compares each skill's last-30-day results with what the tiers predicted; blame from step taps or the weakest tagged skill, main tags 1.0, "uses" tags 0.4; flag at z <= -2 with at least five problems' worth of evidence, misses on three different problems and two different days; a flag schedules a diagnostic set of 3-4 fresh problems where the skill is the only main skill, at or below his level; 2 or more misses confirm (a lapse, then checks on low-recall prerequisites, deepest first), otherwise the flag clears; at most two sets a week. STA-8 reports how often diagnostic sets confirmed flags; DEV-16 tracks diagnosis quality. — [spec ENG-15, STA-8, DEV-16](/docs/spec.md)
- **Lineage.** The earlier note presents this as "Algorithm A/B [Own proposal]", writes that "with about 100 active skills, a one-sided z <= -2.0 screen would flag about 2 skills a week by chance alone (2.3% each)", calls that acceptable only because the confirmatory set is the real test, and records that "no published thresholds for minimum evidence in single-learner weak-spot detection were found" — [error_diagnosis_tagging.md, Section 4](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Toishi%20gaps%20research/error_diagnosis_tagging.md).
- **Population methods need many learners.** Elo item difficulty needs about 100 students — [Pelánek 2016](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf); in simulations with 2,000 students, AFM parameters are biased and "the best performing model is not necessarily the correct one", so flagging concepts from fitted parameters is risky — [Čechák & Pelánek, AIED 2021](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/aied21afm.pdf); in 90 simulated scenarios AFM had consistent problems accepting proposed skill-model refinements and PFA, though better, still struggled — [Goldin & Galyardt 2018](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/view/316) **[Strong, simulation]**.
- **Noisy evidence.** Slip estimates from future performance are noisy and tend toward extremes — [San Pedro, Baker & Rodrigo](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/AIED-D-13-00017_Revised%20v2.pdf); bug-level diagnoses were unstable while skill-level diagnoses were more stable — [Birenbaum, Kelly & Tatsuoka 1992](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/1992/hxjo.html); students' self-diagnosis depends on scaffolding (0.73 vs 0.24 in one physics course) — [Mason et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1603.03070); early detection of wheel spinning from few steps had under 20% precision and recall for the 3-in-a-row definition — [Zhang et al. 2019](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED594575.pdf) **[Moderate]**.
- **Step-level feedback matters in tutoring.** Answer-based tutoring systems had mean effect 0.31 against no tutoring, step-based systems 0.76, human tutoring 0.79 **[Strong]** — [VanLehn](https://quality.mit.edu/files/2012/01/Quality-Symposium-Kurt-VanLehn.pdf); the app's step taps are tags for blame, not step-by-step feedback.

| 7 | 3.3% | 7.4% | 2.6% |

| 10 | 3.3% | 5.0% | 7.0% |

- **Power per evaluation** (true success 0.60 or 0.50 against a prediction of 0.80): n = 5: 0.32 / 0.50; n = 6: 0.18 / 0.34; n = 7: 0.29 / 0.50; n = 8: 0.41 / 0.64; n = 10: 0.37 / 0.62; n = 12: 0.34 / 0.61; n = 15: 0.39 / 0.70; n = 20: 0.58 / 0.87; n = 30: 0.71 / 0.95. - **Confirmation by a fixed diagnostic set** (at least 2 misses): of 3 problems, P(confirm) = 0.104 / 0.028 for true success 0.8 / 0.9 and 0.352 / 0.500 for 0.6 / 0.5; of 4 problems 0.181 / 0.052 and 0.525 / 0.688. So a false flag survives confirmation 3-18% of the time and a true gap at 0.6 is confirmed only 35-53% of the time.
- **Sequential alternative (SPRT, standard method, no source retrieved).** Test H0: success 0.85 against H1: 0.55 with alpha 0.10, beta 0.20, at most 8 fresh problems, presented one at a time. The decision table is a lookup: confirm after 2 misses in the first 2 problems, or 3 misses with 1-2 correct, or 4 misses with 3-4 correct, or (at problem 8) 3 misses with 5 correct; clear after 4 correct with no misses, or 6 correct with 1 miss, or (at problem 8) 2 misses with 6 correct. Operating characteristics (P(confirm); expected problems 4.6-6.0):

| True success | 0.95 | 0.90 | 0.85 | 0.80 | 0.70 | 0.60 | 0.50 | 0.40 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SPRT, max 8 | 0.007 | 0.041 | 0.107 | 0.202 | 0.439 | 0.669 | 0.840 | 0.940 |
| Fixed 4-problem rule | 0.014 | 0.052 | 0.110 | 0.181 | 0.348 | 0.525 | 0.688 | 0.821 |
| Fixed 3-problem rule | 0.007 | 0.028 | 0.061 | 0.104 | 0.216 | 0.352 | 0.500 | 0.648 |

  At true success 0.85 the SPRT and the fixed 4-problem rule confirm equally often (10.7% vs 11.0%), but at 0.60 the SPRT confirms 67% against 53%, at the price of about 1.5 more problems on average. A variant with alpha 0.05 and at most 10 problems gives 0.151 / 0.275 / 0.547 / 0.766 / 0.900 at true success 0.85 / 0.80 / 0.70 / 0.60 / 0.50 (expected problems 4.8-7.6).
- **Semester simulation** (150 skills, 120 days, daily sliding 30-day window, at most 2 sets a week, about 4 problems per skill per 30 days, diagnostic set of 4): calibrated Elo and no truly weak skills: about 16 false flags (about 10% of skills) and about 2 false confirmations; Elo over-optimistic by 0.05 / 0.10: 19.8 / 21.1 false flags; with 8% of skills truly weak (true success 0.5 against a predicted 0.85): 9.3 of 12.4 weak skills flagged (75%), 6.4 confirmed, plus 12.6 false flags; at 2.5 problems per skill per 30 days only 4.2 of 11.6 weak skills were flagged (36%) with 4.8 false flags; at 6 problems per 30 days 10.1 of 11.9 flagged with 17 false flags. These simulations place each problem on its own day, so the "two days" condition is automatically met; if real problems cluster on one day, flag counts are upper bounds.
- **Consequences.** The two-stage structure, not the screen, protects students: false flags cost a 3-4 problem set each (about 60-90 problems per semester for a 150-skill course), false confirmations are rare (about 2), and the cap of two sets a week bounds the load. The weak point is power with few problems per skill, which is common in a 100-300 skill course. Because the z statistic depends on the Elo prediction, miscalibrated tier anchors (optimism of 0.05-0.10) raise false flags by roughly 25-30%.
- **Recommendation.** (1) Replace the normal-approximation z by an exact one-sided binomial tail (flag at P <= 0.025), or a beta-binomial tail that includes uncertainty about p0, so the nominal rate is the real rate; (2) replace the fixed 3-4 problem set by the SPRT lookup above (stopping early on clear cases) and report the confirmation rate (STA-8) as the monitor: a rate below roughly 15% of flags suggests miscalibrated predictions; (3) count evidence in distinct problems, not "problems' worth", for the three-misses condition; (4) keep the cap; (5) keep the rule's output as a hypothesis to test, since nothing external validates it.

### Gaps
- No published single-learner weak-spot method, minimum-evidence threshold, or comparison of such rules; Wald's SPRT, Benjamini-Hochberg control and beta-binomial shrinkage are standard statistical methods for which I retrieved no source in this session; the simulation assumes independent problems, constant true success, and Bernoulli outcomes; real Elo errors are correlated across a student's skills, which would cluster false flags.

## 10. Leeches at six lapses and leech repair (ENG-9)

### Takeaway
No external study supports six lapses (or Anki's eight); the number is a convention. A simple simulation shows six lapses is a reasonable but late detector: a skill whose true per-review success is 0.5 reaches six lapses in a median of 27 days (11 reviews), one at 0.8 does so in about a quarter of runs, and a normal skill (0.9) almost never. The repair content (worked example, prerequisite checks, textbook reference, fresh learning phase) is consistent with worked-example and prerequisite evidence but untested as a package. Verdict: number **Unsupported**; repair **Weakly supported**.

### Cited Findings
- **What the spec does.** A skill with 6 lapses (a setting) becomes a leech, is repaired not suspended: worked example, checks on every prerequisite, textbook reference, then a fresh learning phase; a flashcard leeches at 8 lapses and is flagged for rewriting (ENG-9). Per ADR-0014, a careless slip within the cap is Again flagged `slip` and the leech counter skips it (`leech_base` is raised by one); held misses that settle as excused also do not count. — [spec ENG-9](/docs/spec.md), [ADR-0014](/docs/decisions/0014-honest-grading-and-queue-parity.md)
- **Convention.** Anki's default leech threshold is 8 lapses, with warnings at half the threshold (ADR-0014 notes that Anki suspends leech cards, while this app repairs skills instead) **[Convergent practice]** — [Anki manual, leeches](https://docs.ankiweb.net/leeches.html).
- **Repair ingredients.** Worked examples g about 0.43-0.48 (Section 3) — [Barbieri et al.](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance); prerequisite mastery strongly associated with calculus grade (correlational) — [Harvard Gazette on Sadler & Sonnert](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2018/07/masters-of-calculus-come-prepared-harvard-study-shows); feedback after retrieval failure helps (Rowland: with feedback g = 0.73) — [Rowland 2014](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf); Math Academy retries lessons after unrelated work when too many answers are wrong **[Vendor]** — [Math Academy](https://mathacademy.com/how-it-works).
- **Repeated failure is not rare.** Wheel spinning (failing to master despite practice) affected 0.56% to 24% of student-skill pairs depending on dataset and definition; no remedy was tested — [Zhang et al. 2019](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED594575.pdf).

### Inferences
- **Threshold behaviour (my computation).** FSRS-6 defaults, desired retention 0.90, a constant true per-review success probability q (rather than the model's R), 400 days, 400 runs per cell; "lapses" are Again outcomes on reviews:

| True success q | Threshold 4: reach / median days / reviews | Threshold 6 | Threshold 8 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 94% / 20 / 7 | 91% / 27 / 11 | 92% / 28 / 15 |
| 0.6 | 80% / 25 / 8 | 81% / 49 / 13 | 78% / 56 / 18 |
| 0.7 | 63% / 58 / 10 | 57% / 73 / 16 | 59% / 90 / 23 |
| 0.8 | 36% / 124 / 12 | 26% / 148 / 20 | 25% / 148 / 27 |
| 0.9 | 7% / 141 / 13 | 2% / 229 / 24 | 1% / 171 / 33 |

  Reading: at six lapses a normal skill (q = 0.9) is flagged in 2% of runs, a mediocre one (0.7-0.8) in 26-57% over a year, and a hopeless one (0.5-0.6) in 81-91% within 1-2 months. Threshold 4 would flag 7% of normal skills; threshold 8 would delay flags for weak skills by about a month. Six is a sensible middle, but it needs about 11-16 reviews for a flag, which is slow for a skill that is plainly failing; Anki's practice of warning at half the threshold is a cheap complement.
- **Recommendation.** Keep 6 as a setting; add a soft warning at 3 lapses (offer a worked example and a prerequisite check, without the full repair); log lapses-at-flag, reviews-to-flag and post-repair success so the number can be tuned; keep the slip exclusion (otherwise careless errors would inflate flags).

### Gaps
- No external study on leech thresholds for skills or flashcards beyond Anki's convention; the repair package has not been tested; Kornell, Klein and Rawson (2015) on retrieval success could not be retrieved; the simulation assumes constant q, which understates real variation in skill difficulty over time.

## 11. Automatic parameter fitting (about monthly, from 300 long-term items) and per-domain parameter sets (ADR-0013, ENG-12)

### Takeaway
Benchmarks show that fitted FSRS-6 parameters beat the defaults for most users (84.3%) but lose more often when data are scarce, and Anki gates fitting with a health check above 300 items, which ADR-0013 copies together with a held-out adoption test and one-click revert; this is a conservative, defensible design, but every number behind it comes from flashcard data, nothing tests fitting on skills with fresh problems (whose labels are noisier), and per-domain parameter sets rest only on practitioner advice. The spec's ENG-12 text still says 512 reviews and a manual offer, which ADR-0013 superseded. Verdict: **Weakly supported**.

### Cited Findings
- **What the spec/ADR do.** ENG-12 (spec text): offer a manual "Fit my memory model" after 512 qualifying reviews in a domain (math, sciences, coding), fitter fsrs-rs-python 0.9.3. ADR-0013 supersedes it: `Engine.auto_fit()` fits each parameter set that is due, first at 300 long-term items, then every 30 days or when the items double; a candidate is adopted only if its recency-weighted log loss is lower than the current set's on the same items; above 300 items it must also win held-out on 5 time-ordered folds and pass Anki's health check (adjusted log loss <= 1.11 or adjusted RMSE(bins) <= 1.53); every attempt is logged and revertible; one parameter set per skill domain, one for flashcards, one per imported Anki deck group; the strict label (`strict_ok`) and failed learning-phase events are kept as prefixes; credited (partial-credit) events never train. — [spec ENG-12](/docs/spec.md), [ADR-0013](/docs/decisions/0013-fsrs-fitting-clock-and-memory.md)
- **Benchmark.** FSRS-6 log loss 0.3460, RMSE(bins) 0.0653, AUC 0.7034 without same-day reviews (FSRS-5: 0.3561 / 0.0742 / 0.7010); the averages are weighted by each user's review count; optimized FSRS-6 beats default parameters for 84.3% of users and the defaults win more often when data are scarce; absolute RMSE depends on binning and weighting **[Moderate; Anki flashcards]** — [srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark), [Expertium, Benchmark](https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html).
- **Library behavior at small sizes.** fsrs-rs returns the default parameters below 8 items, fits only the four initial stabilities (w0 to w3) below 64 items, and otherwise trains with a recency-weighted log loss, an L2 pull toward the starting parameters and clipping at every step; Anki's own adoption test is an in-sample comparison of recency-weighted log loss on all items, and its cadence advice is monthly or whenever the review count doubles **[Source code / Practitioner]** — [fsrs-rs training.rs](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs-rs/blob/main/src/training.rs), [gap-audit notes on Anki's fitter](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Spaced%20repetition%20algorithm%20gap%20audit/fsrs_in_anki.md).
- **Anki's guard rail.** The health check runs only above 300 training items, uses a 5-split time-series evaluation and the baselines `0.623 (4r(1-r))^0.738` for log loss and a retention-and-count-dependent baseline for RMSE — [Anki params.rs](https://github.com/ankitects/anki/blob/main/rslib/src/scheduler/fsrs/params.rs); forum guidance on the number of reviews conflicts (8/64 vs 100/400) — [Anki forum](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/how-many-reviews-for-accurate-optimization/53320); py-fsrs's optimizer returns the unchanged defaults below 512 logs — [py-fsrs tests](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/py-fsrs/blob/main/tests/test_optimizer.py); "RMSE is strongly correlated with the number of reviews, logloss is strongly correlated with retention" (Expertium, same forum thread). The older tutorial offers optimizing at 512, 1,024, 2,048 reviews as an alternative to fixed monthly optimization — [fsrs4anki tutorial](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/blob/main/docs/tutorial.md).
- **Separate parameters for different material.** Advice to put easy and hard decks in separate presets **[Practitioner]** — [Anki forum](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/fsrs-and-different-level-of-difficulty/48511); non-monotonic forgetting curves were attributed to mixing different materials (Jarrett Ye) — [LessWrong history](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/G7fpGCi8r7nCKXsQk); a subdeck's preset overrides its parent's — [fsrs4anki tutorial](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/blob/main/docs/tutorial.md).
- **Offline accuracy is not proof of benefit.** See Section 0: the Lucas critique preprint (Schill 2026) and MaiMemo's 2025 report that offline gains did not carry over to product outcomes **[Weak / Vendor] [2024-26]** — [Schill](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10548), [MaiMemo](https://memodocs.maimemo.com/docs/2025_experiment).
- **Synthetic check from the gap audit.** On synthetic data, 40 items changed no parameters, 200 items changed all 21, and 5.7k items cut hold-out log loss from 0.3631 to 0.3590 (the true-parameter model scored 0.3594) **[local simulation, not a published result]** — [gap audit notes](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Spaced%20repetition%20algorithm%20gap%20audit/fsrs_in_anki.md).
- **Do not tune by hand.** A forum volunteer advises "Do not tweak your parameters, or let an LLM tweak them for you" (practice-problem thread) **[Practitioner]** — [Anki forum](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/questions-about-tuning-fsrs-for-math-logical-reasoning-problem-cards/70949).

### Inferences
- The adoption gate (candidate must win on recency-weighted log loss; held-out folds and health check above 300 items; revert) is the right response to the two risks the evidence shows: overfitting at a few hundred items (the audit's synthetic run: all 21 parameters moved at 200 items) and offline-versus-online mismatch (Lucas critique, MaiMemo). It makes automatic fitting low-risk because a bad fit can only be adopted if it beats the current parameters on data the current parameters did not see, and can be reverted. This is stricter than Anki's own adoption test, which compares the two parameter sets in-sample and shows the health check as a message about fit rather than as a gate (gap-audit notes on `params.rs`).
- Skills with fresh problems add label noise (success depends on the problem's difficulty as well as memory), so a given amount of data should locate parameters less precisely than for cards; 300 items is therefore a floor from flashcards, not a measured requirement for skills. No source tests this.
- Per-domain sets split scarce data: each set needs its own 300 long-term items before its first fit (ADR-0013 maps courses to domains; courses not mapped share one "skills" set, which the legacy math parameters feed until that set is written), so a student with three courses in three domains may wait months for each fit. A hierarchical (partial-pooling) fit is the statistical alternative; I found no FSRS implementation or evidence for it.
- **Recommendation.** Keep ADR-0013. Update ENG-12's text (512 reviews, manual offer) to the ADR-0013 rule so the spec and the code agree. Consider adding a minimum improvement margin to the adoption rule so near-ties do not cause parameter churn (design option; no evidence), and report the held-out log loss gain to the student in plain words. Do not let the fitter train on Lighter-set events (see Section 12).

### Gaps
- No evidence on how many skill-level events are needed for stable FSRS parameters; no test of per-domain versus pooled sets; the benchmark is Anki flashcards; the forum thresholds conflict; the audit's synthetic numbers are local and not peer reviewed.

## 12. Daily load: caps, fair share across courses, interleaving and confusable-pair placement, triage of overdue skills, and the Lighter set (QUE-4 to QUE-13, QUE-10, QUE-17, session ordering, ENG-18)

### Takeaway
The daily-count cap with new skills gated by backlog is convergent practice (Anki's guidance and Math Academy), the weighted fair share across courses and the half-quota carry-over have no external evidence (they are design heuristics, plausibly sound), the interleaving evidence is moderate but contested with benefit concentrated in immediate succession, which the spec's "1 to 3 items apart" rule may not deliver, triage policy "highest recall first" rests on flashcard simulations and in my simulation gives the fewest lapses but 0.5 to 2 points lower mean recall than "oldest due first" (and QUE-4's 7-day promotion turns the default into near-"oldest due first" under a chronic backlog), and the Lighter set (tier 1-2 problems, successes counted as Hard) conflicts with the core-band rule and appears to feed the fitter. Verdict: caps **Convergent practice**; fair share **Unsupported**; confusable placement **Weakly supported (contested)**; triage **None (computed trade-off)**; Lighter set **Weakly supported with an internal conflict**.

### Cited Findings
- **What the spec does.** Default daily count 20; flashcards limit 50; up to 4 new skills a day, only while the projected 7-day average load stays at or under N (QUE-5); triage order 1-7 with order 6 "other due reviews, highest current recall first" and a pluggable policy recorded per session (QUE-6); no due skill waits more than 7 days past due (QUE-4); fair share across courses by goal (A 1.5, B 1.0, Keep 0.5) x exam urgency (1 to 3) x backlog (0.5 to 2) with a floor of 3 problems or 10% of the count, surplus handed back, courses mixed in proportion to quotas, shortfall carried over capped at half a quota, new skills only while a course's own backlog is under one day's quota (QUE-11 to QUE-13); session ordering: same skill never back to back, same section at least two apart, confusable skills one to three items apart, harder and easier alternate, skill names hidden until after the answer; Lighter set serves tier 1-2 problems, no new skills, successes count as Hard (QUE-10); ENG-18 logs whether a confusable skill appeared in the previous 3 items and reports strict success split by that flag after 1,000 rated reviews. — [spec QUE-4 to QUE-13, QUE-17, ENG-18](/docs/spec.md)
- **Caps and new-versus-review.** Anki's manual: "The review limit also applies to new cards, and no new cards will be shown" once it is reached (unless an option is set); with a backlog, "stop introducing new cards until you catch up with that backlog"; "Continuing to introduce new cards when you're already behind can make the backlog worse"; "If you are consistently learning 20 new cards a day, you can expect your daily reviews to be roughly about 200 cards/day"; and the "Deck" gather order "may stop before all subdecks have been checked" once a daily limit is reached, a direct starvation mechanism for later decks **[Convergent practice; vendor documentation, not research]** — [Anki manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html), quotes as recorded in the earlier [speed/mastery/goals notes](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Toishi%20gaps%20research/speed_mastery_goals.md). RemNote's exam scheduler adds an exam goal to the daily goal and proposes a temporary increase when the student is behind, rather than silently rearranging **[Vendor]** — [RemNote](https://help.remnote.com/en/articles/9102040-understanding-the-exam-scheduler). Math Academy's published load estimate is about 10 questions per lesson and 3 to 5 per review, with reviews growing to about 60 questions a day within months at 3 new topics a day, which is the case it makes for compressing reviews **[Vendor]** — [Skycak, FIRe](https://www.justinmath.com/individualized-spaced-repetition-in-hierarchical-knowledge-structures/).
- **Triage guidance conflicts.** Anki's manual lists "Due date, then random" as the default, recommended when up to date, and for a large backlog under SM-2 suggests "Relative overdueness" ("cards that you're more likely to have forgotten first"), whose FSRS equivalent it lists as "Ascending retrievability", that is lowest recall first **[Practitioner]** — [Anki manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html). Anki 24.11 then added "descending retrievability" (highest recall first) and said simulations favor it when there is a backlog; a 2026 thread calls it "the order which is usually recommended", with the rationale "you will be sacrificing some cards that already have lower R in order to rescue cards that still have higher R" (forum volunteer) — [Anki forum, thread titled "A situation in which descending retrievability may not be the most efficient review sort order"](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/a-situation-in-which-descending-retrievability-may-not-be-the-most-efficient-review-sort-order/68947); 26.05 made it "a pure R sort" — [Anki 26.05](https://github.com/ankitects/anki/releases/tag/26.05). An unvalidated forum proposal sorts by R_today - R_tomorrow, and no developer simulation was posted for it **[Practitioner] [2024-26]** — [Anki 24.11 notes](https://newreleases.io/project/github/ankitects/anki/release/24.11), [forum proposal](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/sorting-order-by-today-minus-tomorrow-retrievability-difference/70007). A sibling inventory records a 540-post forum thread on review order — [feature inventory, row A4](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Toishi%20full%20evidence%20review/anki_and_srs_feature_inventory.md). - **Interleaving evidence.** Brunmair & Richter 2019 (59 papers, 238 effect sizes, 8,466 participants): g = 0.42 overall; mathematics 0.34 [0.11, 0.57]; 0.29 after trim-and-fill; stronger when categories are similar; temporally spaced interleaving 0.22 (not significant) against 0.73 for immediate succession **[Moderate]** — [Brunmair & Richter](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf). - **Fair share has no direct evidence.** I found no study on splitting a fixed daily practice budget across courses; the only analogues are Anki's limits and RemNote's exam goal — [Anki manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html). QUE-11 to QUE-13 match an earlier research note's own "[Proposal]" almost line for line (goal factor A 1.5, B 1.0, Keep 0.5; urgency rising from 1 to 3 in the last two weeks; backlog pressure clamped to 0.5 to 2; floor of 3 or 10%; largest-remainder rounding; surplus handed back; shortfall carried over at up to half a quota; new skills only while the course backlog is at most one day's quota); that note states the design "comes from general scheduling practice (fair queueing, deficit round robin), not from learning-science studies, and was not sourced" — [speed, mastery and goals notes, section 7](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Toishi%20gaps%20research/speed_mastery_goals.md).
- **Return from a break.** The spec (QUE-17): on the first open after 3 or more missed study days, Home offers "Back on track by <date> at N a day" with paces N, 1.5N or 2N; new skills wait until that date; young skills (stability under 7 days) restart with a worked example. ### Inferences
- **Triage under a cap (my computation).** 300 skills learned over days 0-59, desired retention 0.90, FSRS-6 defaults, steady-state demand about 3.5 reviews a day; scenario A: a 15-day break (days 80-94) then a daily cap; scenario B: a persistent cap from day 60; 12 seeds.   Reading: order 6 alone (highest R first) produces the fewest lapses but the lowest mean recall among the first-order policies; "oldest due first" has the highest mean recall in all four settings at 25 to 60% more lapses; the gap is under 1 point after a short break and about 2 points under a persistent shortfall. - **Workload by goal (see Section 2).** The cap N interacts with desired retention: 0.88 needs about 20% fewer reviews than 0.90 and 0.95 about 57% more, so a fixed N makes a high-retention goal push the course into backlog sooner; QUE-8/13's proposal logic is the right place to say so.
- **Fair share.** The quota formula is a weighted proportional split with floors; its constants (1.5/1.0/0.5, 1-3, 0.5-2, 3 or 10%, half a quota) are starting values with no evidence. A principled alternative would rank all due reviews across courses by importance x expected recall lost per day and cut at N, but it is less predictable for the student; the quota approach is a reasonable trade of optimality for fairness and legibility (design judgment). Keep the constants as settings and test them by simulation (the spec already plans a three-course scheduler simulation) rather than by outcome.
- **Interleaving and placement.** The best-supported statements are: benefit is moderate on average (g about 0.3-0.4 for math), uncertain after a 2024 null and a pending 2027 replication, larger for similar categories, and concentrated where categories alternate in immediate succession. The spec's design is cautious in the right way (ENG-18 measures instead of assuming; authoring effort goes to confusable tables and which-method items; skill names hidden until after the answer, which forces discrimination). Two weaknesses: (a) "one to three items apart" is not immediate succession (whether it falls under the meta-analysis's "immediate" category is a judgment call), so the adjacency rule might forfeit part of the benefit; (b) ENG-18's observational flag ("confusable skill in the previous 3 items") is confounded by difficulty and by which skills the queue happens to place near each other. Cost-free fix: when two confusable skills are both due, place them adjacent (0 to 1 items apart) by default, or randomize adjacency (0-1 vs 4 or more apart) so ENG-18 becomes a within-student experiment. Power is low (confusable pairs are few), so use randomization to avoid bias rather than to detect a small effect.
- **Lighter set.** The spec's intent (an easy day should not overstate recall) is sound, and Hard is a mild penalty (it still grows stability, more slowly: w15 < 1), but tier 1-2 problems on a skill whose core band is tier 3 or higher are outside ENG-4's rule that only core-band problems produce FSRS events; passes are then weak evidence and failures are strong evidence. Cleaner rules: (a) Lighter-set passes do not update the schedule (log only), failures count as Again; or (b) keep Hard but mark Lighter-set events prefix-only so the fitter does not learn from them (as for stretch, settled and post-stretch events). I found no exclusion in the current fitter code.
- **Recommendation.** Keep caps, backlog gating and the pluggable, logged triage. Say plainly in the spec that QUE-4's 7-day promotion makes the default behave like oldest-due-first in a chronic backlog, and either keep that (it protects the most decayed skills) or make the 7-day rule part of the pluggable policy so the comparison in STA-4 is not blurred; offer "oldest due first" as the named alternative to "highest recall first" (in my simulation they differ by 0.5 to 2 points of mean recall and by 25 to 60% in lapses). Adopt adjacency or randomized adjacency for confusable pairs. Resolve QUE-10 as above. Record the fair-share constants as settings with no evidence behind them and test them in the planned scheduler simulation.

### Gaps
- No controlled evidence on daily caps, fair share, or triage policy for skill practice; Latimier et al. (2021) on spaced retrieval and Donoghue & Hattie's dependence on weak interleaving data limit the interleaving picture; the temporal-spacing moderator in Brunmair & Richter was read from a summary of numbers and its operational definition of "immediate succession" was not re-checked; the triage simulation uses a pure-Bernoulli learner with default parameters, equal skills, no correlation between skills and no new-skill intake during the backlog; the Lighter-set/fitter point comes from reading the code, not running it; no source for the 7-day figure in QUE-4 was found.

## 13. Placement: 25 questions with graph inference (onboarding "Placement check")

### Takeaway
A short adaptive check that infers many skills from a few answers is how ALEKS (cap of 30 questions, Bayesian over a knowledge structure) and Math Academy (20 to 60 questions, per-topic plus-minus evidence) place students (ALEKS with vendor-reported accuracy, Math Academy without published accuracy, neither independently validated), so the spec's idea (25 questions, graph inference, provisional states that the first real review confirms or demotes) is **Weakly supported**. But the algorithm as specified and implemented (`core/engine/placement.py`) asks the deepest skills first and propagates only passes: in my simulation it seeds only about a tenth of the skills a student who knows half the course actually knows (70 to 80% for a half-split adaptive order), and a single lucky pass on a deep skill can mark dozens of prerequisites known. The fix is small and standard: choose each question to split the unresolved skills, and let failures rule out dependents as passes rule in prerequisites.

### Cited Findings
- **What the spec and engine do.** One standard-tier problem per skill; order = deepest prerequisite chain first, then later sections first; a pass marks every transitive prerequisite provisionally known and skips it; a fail or "I don't know" changes nothing; stop after 25 questions per course; a skill passed directly starts in review with a provisional stability of 10 days, an inferred one with 2.3 days (the FSRS default first-Good stability), failed and "I don't know" skills start as new, the first real review on a fresh problem confirms or demotes each; "Placement only ever adds knowledge." The walk is implemented and tested in `placement.py`; I found no other caller. — [spec, Placement check](/docs/spec.md), [placement.py](/core/engine/placement.py)
- **ALEKS.** The initial assessment is capped at 30 questions (up to 29 adaptive plus one randomly chosen extra problem used only for evaluation); each question targets an item with likelihood near 0.5 of being in the student's knowledge state; each answer updates the probabilities of the candidate states, with separate update strengths for correct, incorrect and "I don't know" answers (values of about 35, 5 and 50 were relayed by a page summarizer, units unclear); items are classed in-state above 80% likelihood and out-of-state below 20%; the "I don't know" button exists because careless errors are possible and raises the probability of states without the item; the College Placement structure has about 10^23 states and 314 items, and the paper says exact identification could take about 77 questions in the worst case, which is why the cap exists. Accuracy on the extra problem: AUROC 0.875 (Sixth-Grade Math, N = 162,900), 0.863 (College Algebra, N = 174,073), 0.889 (College Placement, N = 2,775,432); accuracy 0.801 to 0.814; the paper reports no correlation with external tests **[Vendor]** — [ALEKS ESSA document](https://www.mheducation.com/content/dam/mhe/research/prek-12/essa-tier-4/aleks-practical-perspective-knowledge-space-theory.pdf). A secondary aggregator reports a half-split questioning rule (ask the item whose predicted P(correct) is closest to 0.5), a median tetrachoric correlation of 0.68 over 125,786 assessments and at most 35 items in an elementary domain **[secondary]** — [Emergent Mind](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/aleks).
- **Math Academy.** The book's diagnostic chapter: 20 to 40 questions for lower-grade courses and 40 to 60 for higher-grade ones such as Calculus, against 500+ without graph inference; evidence is a per-topic "plus-minus balance" in which a correct answer adds to the topic and its prerequisites and an incorrect answer subtracts from the topic and its post-requisites; a correct but excessively slow answer gets reduced weight; conflicts (advanced right, simple wrong, or right but slow) give low confidence and "conditional completion", meaning the student starts as though they know the topic and the system "falls backwards" at the first struggle; students are told to click "I don't know" if a problem is not solvable in about 3 minutes and not to guess ("a successful guess may cause you to be placed too far"); a handful of "supplemental diagnostic" questions patch the profile when the graph changes **[Vendor]** — [The Math Academy Way, ch. 30](https://justinmath.com/files/the-math-academy-way.pdf). The website adds about 30 to 45 minutes, splittable across sittings, and a conservative design — [Hecker, part 7](https://frankhecker.com/2025/02/14/math-academy-part-7/), [Math Academy](https://mathacademy.com/how-it-works). A reviewer calls the book "a sales pitch" in part; no independent study of the diagnostic's accuracy was found — [book review](https://ijfen.substack.com/p/book-review-the-math-academy-way).
- **Placement tests misplace people.** Scott-Clayton (community-college placement exams): in math, placement scores correlated r = 0.359 with first college-level grades (R-squared about 0.13); the severe error rate was 24.0% (5.8% severe overplacement, 18.3% severe underplacement); an index of high-school GPA and college-preparatory units alone cut it to 22.7% **[Moderate; different setting]** — [Scott-Clayton 2012](https://ccrc.tc.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/02/high-stakes-predict-success.pdf).
- **Prerequisite structure matters, correlationally.** Sadler & Sonnert 2018 (Section 6) — [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2018/07/masters-of-calculus-come-prepared-harvard-study-shows).

### Inferences
- **The structure is what shipped products do; the selection rule is not.** ALEKS picks the item nearest 0.5 (a half-split) and updates on both outcomes; Math Academy lets a fail subtract from post-requisites. The spec's walk asks the deepest skills first and never uses a fail to prune: the repository's cap test (`test_stops_after_25_questions`) answers "fail" to 25 skills in a row, which is the case where the check learns nothing.
- **Simulation (my computation; not a published result).** Synthetic layered prerequisite graphs (10 layers x 20 skills = 200 skills, and 15 x 13 = 195), each skill requiring 1 to 3 skills from the previous one or two layers; the student knows a downward-closed set with difficulty rising by layer; responses: a known skill is failed with probability slip, an unknown one passed with probability guess; 25 questions; 400 draws per cell. Policy A is the spec as implemented (deepest first, passes infer all transitive prerequisites, fails change nothing). Policy B is an illustrative alternative (ask the unresolved skill that maximizes the smaller of the number of skills a pass or a fail would resolve; a pass resolves its prerequisites as known, a fail resolves its dependents as unknown). Cells: share of truly known skills seeded as known / share of seeded skills that are truly unknown. Careful student (slip 0.05, guess 0.01), 10 x 20 graph:

| Share of the course the student knows | A: seeded and truly known | A: false-known | B: seeded and truly known | B: false-known |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10% | 15% | 17.5% | 37% | 5.2% |
| 25% | 13% | 11.2% | 61% | 1.0% |
| 50% | 10% | 4.2% | 70% | 0.4% |
| 75% | 29% | 1.1% | 73% | 0.1% |
| 90% | 70% | 0.2% | 72% | 0.0% |

  The 15 x 13 graph gives the same pattern (A: 13 to 27% sensitivity at 25 to 75% known; B: 78 to 80%). With a guessier student (slip 0.10, guess 0.10) A's false-known rate rises to 56% at 25% known and 27% at 50% known, B's to 11% and 2.9%. At 50% known, A leaves about 89 of roughly 100 known skills as "new" (each costs a learning phase) while B leaves about 30. Reason: with 25 questions and far more than 25 skills above a half-way student's frontier, A spends every question on skills that are failed, and an occasional lucky pass on a deep skill marks all its prerequisites known at once. Caveats: synthetic graphs, uniform prior, no item-level difficulty, real course graphs will have different shapes; the direction (A seeds only a small share of what a partial-knowledge student knows) follows from the ordering rule and does not depend on the exact numbers.
- **Error costs are cheap in both directions, which is the spec's real safeguard.** False-known skills (2.3 or 10 days of provisional stability) come up for review within about 2 to 10 days and are demoted on a fail; false-new skills cost a short learning phase. Neither error, however, is zero cost: a false-known cascade fills the first weeks' queue with reviews of things the student cannot do.
- **Recommendation.** (1) Replace the deepest-first order with a half-split rule (pick the unresolved skill that maximizes the smaller of the numbers of skills a pass and a fail would resolve), and let a fail mark the skill's dependents as skipped (they stay new); this is the ALEKS and Math Academy principle and a few lines in `next_skill` and `answer`. (2) Where a pass contradicts an earlier fail on one of its prerequisites, do not infer from it and ask a nearby skill instead (Math Academy's "conditional completion"). (3) Keep "I don't know" and tell the student not to guess (Math Academy's instruction). (4) Optionally treat a correct but very slow answer as an inferred-strength seed rather than a direct one (Math Academy down-weights slow correct answers; the spec already has target times). (5) Log, from the first real reviews, how often directly passed and inferred skills fail by graph distance, and use the rate to adjust the provisional stabilities after a semester. (6) Keep 25 as a cap with early stop when few skills remain unresolved; ALEKS uses 30 and Math Academy 20 to 60. (7) Re-run placement after long breaks as one option beside QUE-17's pace plan.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed validation of ALEKS accuracy against external outcomes was retrieved (the ESSA document is vendor-written and uses an internal extra-problem measure); the ALEKS update strengths are second-hand; no CAT or IRT sources were retrieved in this review, so the comparison with item-response-theory adaptive testing rests on the ALEKS and Math Academy descriptions; no study of placement for self-directed adult learners or of graph-based inference with single-problem items; Math Academy's diagnostic accuracy is unpublished; Scott-Clayton's results concern high-stakes college placement, not a practice app; the simulation uses synthetic graphs and a simple response model.

## 14. Skill granularity: "one thing a single kind of problem tests", about 100 to 300 per course (GRAPH-3, glossary, GRAPH-4/5/7, ENG-16)

### Takeaway
The knowledge-component literature says that the right grain is an empirical question settled by learning curves and model comparison, and that refining components can improve learning (a learning-curve-guided split of a geometry component produced d = 0.47 in a small tutor study), but those methods need many learners and are error-prone even then; the spec's definition ("one thing a single kind of problem tests", model-drafted and human-reviewed, with near-miss examples and separate method-choice skills) is plausible and in line with 2024-26 findings that LLM-proposed components need expert review. Nothing validates the number 100 to 300, which is at the low end of the products it cites. Verdict: **Weakly supported**.

### Cited Findings
- **What the spec does.** GRAPH-3: one skill is one thing a single kind of problem tests, roughly 100 to 300 per course (Math Academy runs about 300 topics per course); GRAPH-4: every skill has a name, section, textbook references, confusable skills, a worked example and a full pool (tier 1-2 need a template or 20 or more fixed problems; tier 3 at least 10); GRAPH-5: a model drafts skills and candidate edges, the student reviews them in order of confidence, a validator rejects cycles and missing prerequisites; GRAPH-7: each skill has a one- or two-sentence definition, two examples and one or two near-miss examples from its confusable neighbor; ENG-16: families of easily confused methods get their own method-choice skills, active once two prerequisites are learned. — [spec GRAPH-3 to GRAPH-7, ENG-16, glossary](/docs/spec.md)
- **Refinement can help.** A learning-curve-guided refinement (Learning Factors Analysis) split a circle-area component into forward and backward components; in a classroom experiment (115 students randomized, 91 analyzed) students on the refined tutor scored 0.85 against 0.73 on the post-test, d = 0.47, p = .027, with most of the gain on the square-root items that the merged component had hidden **[Moderate; small, one school]** — [Liu & Koedinger, JEDM](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/212/pdf_29). Using learning curves to stop over-practice saved about 22 minutes, 12% of tutoring time, with no significant difference in the post-test or a two-week retention test (a null result in a small study, not proof of no loss) **[Moderate]** — [Cen et al.](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Using_learning_curves_to_optimize_problem_assignment). A comparison of models with 1, 5, 39 and 106 skills for the same tutor data exists, but the abstract gives no accuracy numbers — [Pardos & Heffernan](https://www.sri.com/publication/education-learning-pubs/analyzing-fine-grained-skill-models-using-bayesian-and-mixed-effect-methods/).
- **Refinement is hard to do reliably.** In 90 simulated scenarios AFM had consistent accuracy problems when accepting proposed skill-model refinements, and PFA, though better, struggled in some cases, including telling apart two skills when error rates stayed high after much practice **[Strong, simulation]** — [Goldin & Galyardt 2018](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/view/316); with 2,000 simulated students the best-performing model was not necessarily the correct one, so flagging concepts from fitted AFM parameters is risky — [Čechák & Pelánek 2021](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/aied21afm.pdf). Pelánek: a single global skill can be too coarse, skill estimates for different continents correlated only about 0.6; the same source says about 10 answers per student give reasonable skill estimates but at least about 100 students are needed for good item-difficulty estimates — [Pelánek 2016](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf).
- **Product scale [Vendor].** Math Academy: about 300 topics per course, calculus "several hundred", "multiple thousands of interlinked topics" overall — [Math Academy, how our AI works](https://mathacademy.com/how-our-ai-works). ALEKS: College Placement covers 314 items; per-student course averages (2018) were 385.3 items (Sixth-Grade Math), 523.1 (Algebra I), 301.5 (College Algebra) — [ALEKS ESSA document](https://www.mheducation.com/content/dam/mhe/research/prek-12/essa-tier-4/aleks-practical-perspective-knowledge-space-theory.pdf).
- **LLM-drafted components [2024-26].** GPT-4 generating components for multiple-choice questions matched human-assigned components for 56% of chemistry and 35% of e-learning questions, and experts preferred the LLM's component in about two thirds of the disagreements **[Moderate; ACM L@S 2024]** — [Moore et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20526v1). Scaled generation alone was insufficient on 646 questions (RMSE 0.4285 against 0.4206 for an expert model, with 569 generated components against 101); merging similar labels at cosine 0.8 gave 428 components and 0.4259 **[Weak, preprint]** — [Wang, Lin & Koedinger 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09935). In a 2026 programming study, generated components matched or beat human ones (AUC 0.816 against 0.797; instructor-judged tag precision 93.2% against 92.5%; 89.3% inter-annotator agreement) **[Moderate; Findings of ACL 2026; programming]** — [KCGen-KT](https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-acl.1670.pdf). Free selection from a large fixed taxonomy is poor: in self-guided tagging of Common Core problems GPT-4 exactly matched the gold set only 4.8% of the time, over-predicted (3.05 against 1.66 tags) and usually landed on a neighboring standard, but narrowing one level at a time reached a mean per-level accuracy of 0.850 **[Moderate; K-12; EMNLP Findings 2024]** — [MathFish](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.323.pdf). Binary verification (does this problem need this skill?) approaches human level in K-3 (GPT-4o accuracy 88.4 against an expert's 91.8, but precision only 76.5, that is, over-tagging) — [MathKnowCT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.08406). Zero-shot Q-matrix coding of 20 reading items for 5 skills agreed with a validated matrix from kappa 0.12 to 0.63 across models and did not improve with newer ones (Gemini 2.5 Pro 0.63 in May 2025, Gemini 3 Pro 0.42 in January 2026) **[Moderate, preprint]** — [arXiv 2602.08796](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.08796). In a study of 26 items and about 614 students, LLM-proposed tags agreed only modestly with an expert matrix (high-confidence tags: recall 0.54, micro-F1 0.49) yet fitted student responses better (AUC 0.780 against 0.717) **[Weak, preprint]** — [arXiv 2604.16398](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.16398). I found no published accuracy for LLM tagging of calculus or other college-level problems against a fixed skill list — [earlier notes](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Toishi%20gaps%20research/error_diagnosis_tagging.md).
- **Method choice as a skill.** Interleaving's mechanism is choosing a strategy, not just executing it (Rohrer et al., d = 0.83), and schema-based instruction for word problems improved performance on a 9-week delayed test in a randomized trial of 806 seventh graders with math problem-solving difficulties (Jitendra et al. 2017) **[Strong]** — [Rohrer et al.](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2019-rohrer.pdf), [Jitendra et al.](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/a-randomized-trial-of-the-effects-of-schema-based-instruction-on-/). No study models method selection as a separate component and tests whether it improves prediction — [earlier notes](file:///home/claude/research_notes/Toishi%20gaps%20research/error_diagnosis_tagging.md).

### Inferences
- The spec's definition is operational ("one kind of problem") and matches the idea of a knowledge component that produces a smooth learning curve; its safeguards (near-miss examples for consistent tagging, human review of model drafts, method-choice skills for confusable families) fit the 2024-26 pattern that LLM proposals need expert review.
- It cannot be validated by the standard tools in a single-learner offline app: learning-curve analysis and Q-matrix refinement need many learners and are unreliable even then (simulation evidence). The spec's own Elo flag for wrong tiers and the first-seen versus repeated-form notice are the right local analogues; a third cheap one is a within-skill heterogeneity check: for each skill, compare success across problem forms after adjusting for tier; a bimodal pattern (some forms always solved, others never) suggests two skills.
- 100-300 per course is at or below both ALEKS (about 300 to 520 items per course) and Math Academy (about 300 topics per course); coarser skills mean fewer, noisier FSRS cards (a skill with mixed sub-skills has heterogeneous success, which FSRS cannot model) but less scheduling overhead. A cost-based argument for the low end is that more skills means more graph edges to review and more problems to author (GRAPH-4's pool targets).
- **Recommendation.** Keep the definition and the draft-then-review pipeline; treat 100-300 as a guide, not a constraint; add (a) the within-skill heterogeneity diagnostic, (b) a "split this skill" workflow that preserves history by assigning the old stability to both children, (c) a tagging-consistency check between two independent model drafts before human review (cheap, in the spirit of the kappa studies).

### Gaps
- The KLI framework paper and other KC literature (Koedinger) could not be retrieved in this session; no learning-curve or Q-matrix analysis exists for gaokao-level content; the LLM-tagging findings are mostly preprints with mixed results and none concerns college-level math; I found no evidence on the best granularity for FSRS-scheduled skills specifically; ALEKS "items" and Math Academy "topics" may not be the same grain as the spec's skills; the LFA classroom study is one school and 91 analyzed students, and its 2012 validation leaned on error rates by item type rather than on learning curves.
