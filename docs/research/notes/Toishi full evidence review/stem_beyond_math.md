## 1. PrairieLearn in depth: randomized variants, second-chance testing and mastery

### Takeaway
PrairieLearn (PL) separates a seeded generator (parse errors are not graded and cost no attempt) from assessment policies that turn variants and attempts into credit: in Homework a wrong answer yields a fresh variant and points only go up (mastery means working until streaks accumulate); in Exams one variant gets a few attempts at declining value. Illinois research finds parameter-style randomization produces small unfairness once outlier questions are trimmed, that variant isomorphism is a measurable design property rather than a given, and that frequent testing with second chances is associated with better results in quasi-experiments (with some evidence that frequency, not the second chance, does the work). For us the big lessons are: make variant quality measurable per instance, keep attempts per instance low, and keep "unreadable" separate from "wrong" (already in the spec).

### Cited Findings

**Generator, variant and grading mechanics (documentation)**
- A question's `server.py` runs `generate(data)` (sets `params` and `correct_answers`), then `parse(data)`, then `grade(data)`, which runs only if parsing produced no format errors and sets `partial_scores`, `score` and `feedback`. Each variant is seeded from `variant_seed`, so the same seed reproduces the same parameters and answers. — [PL server.py docs](https://docs.prairielearn.com/question/server/) [DOC]
- Unparseable or invalid input is a "format error": not graded and does not consume an attempt; an incorrect-but-valid answer is graded and does consume one. — [PL server.py docs](https://docs.prairielearn.com/question/server/) [DOC]
- Homework: unlimited attempts; after an incorrect answer "a new question variant is generated with new random numbers" (`triesPerVariant` defaults to 1). Exam: each question yields one random variant, and the student may make several attempts at that same variant when real-time grading is on. — [PL assessment docs](https://docs.prairielearn.com/assessment/configuration/) [DOC]
- Homework scoring: a fully correct submission earns `autoPoints`; each consecutive fully correct answer raises the value by `autoPoints` (3, 6, 9, 12) up to `maxAutoPoints` unless `constantQuestionValue` is set; partial credit counts only if it beats the current best; points never decrease. The docs state the mastery intent: "any student should be able to receive full points for a homework question if they work long enough on it." — [PL assessment docs](https://docs.prairielearn.com/assessment/configuration/) [DOC]
- Exam scoring: `autoPoints` can be a declining list such as `[10, 7, 5]`; partial credit is a percentage of that attempt's value; reattempting never loses points; submissions stop at 100% or after the last listed attempt. — [PL assessment docs](https://docs.prairielearn.com/assessment/configuration/) [DOC]
- `advanceScorePerc` (0 to 100) blocks later questions until the student's best autograded score meets the threshold, unblocks when attempts run out, and the docs "strongly discourage" it in high-stakes exams. — [PL assessment docs](https://docs.prairielearn.com/assessment/configuration/) [DOC]

**The 2015 PrairieLearn mastery paper (West, Herman, Zilles, ASEE 2015)**
- Questions are re-randomized on every attempt, students may attempt a question as many times as they like, and a practice submission grades the question without changing the score; answer formats already included vector drawing graded within a tolerance, numeric answers and multiple choice. — [ASEE 2015](https://peer.asee.org/24575.pdf) [DOC/OBS]
- Mastery Score = the average probability of answering a randomly chosen question correctly, from a three-parameter logistic IRT model (ability prior N(0,1), Bayesian updates); an "aging" term discounts recent repeated attempts to encourage spacing; points are adaptive (correct gains m⁺−m, incorrect loses m−m⁻) and the homework score is a constant times the Mastery Score, capped at 100%; recommended questions maximize expected utility (the derivative of the response function), favoring moderate success probability. — [ASEE 2015](https://peer.asee.org/24575.pdf) [DOC/OBS]
- Behavior, Fall 2014 (N=194): mean 6.0 attempts per question (SD 3.2); attempt count correlated slightly negatively with exam score (r = −0.14) while fraction correct correlated r = +0.51; attempts and fraction correct r = −0.29 (weaker students practiced more without reaching the same mastery); "few persisted beyond three incorrect attempts"; never-solved questions averaged 2.3 attempts versus 9.0 for eventually solved ones (the same summary also gives 7.9 attempts per question, which it does not reconcile with 6.0). — [ASEE 2015](https://peer.asee.org/24575.pdf) [OBS]
- Students ordered questions in varied, partly non-easy-to-hard ways (mean Kendall tau 0.65, SD 0.25 on Homework 4), read by the authors as spaced practice; the authors suggest detecting students about to give up and offering assistance. — [ASEE 2015](https://peer.asee.org/24575.pdf) [OBS]
- Perceptions, Spring 2014 (N=408): 80% liked online homework (4% disliked); 77% preferred it to written homework (10% not); 76% said it helped exam preparation (13% not) and 63% said it helped understanding (23% not); the same extract gives 33% (38% not) and 61% (14% not) for a non-adaptive system on those two measures, without saying whether the same students rated both. — [ASEE 2015](https://peer.asee.org/24575.pdf) [SURV]
- The paper does not describe exam or retake design. — [ASEE 2015](https://peer.asee.org/24575.pdf)

**Randomized variants: fairness, difficulty and taxonomy**
- Fowler et al. (SIGCSE 2022) studied PL exams with random question pools: CS0 Python (three semesters, about 550 to 650 students per exam, 20 to 30 pools per exam, 40 to 45 on finals) and a C++ Data Structures course (about 417 students per exam). Using a generalized partial credit IRT model and simulation (100 random permutations, 500 attempts per student): ability-to-score Spearman ρ 0.89 to 0.95 in CS0; mean absolute deviation (MAD) of a student's average score per variant was 4.65, 2.28 and 2.37 points across the three CS0 semesters (worst case just over 5 points); Data Structures averaged 2.77 points, but some programming exams with few questions reached about 19 points; the "SS ratio" (share of score variance due to chance rather than the variant received, as summarized) averaged 57.7% in CS0 and 90.6% in Data Structures. — [Fowler et al. 2022](https://zilles.web.engr.illinois.edu/papers/exam_variant_sigcse_2022.pdf) [OBS + simulation, two courses]
- Their recommendations: balance pools by difficulty and content; use historical data to remove outlier questions (CS0 improved after trimming); prefer parameterized item generators (analogous variants with different numbers showed lower permutation-driven variance); avoid exams with very few questions; aim for unfairness no worse than grading precision (one-third of a letter grade); pair randomized exams with second-chance and frequent low-stakes exams; keep tracking fairness. — [Fowler et al. 2022](https://zilles.web.engr.illinois.edu/papers/exam_variant_sigcse_2022.pdf)
- Chen, West, Zilles (L@S 2019): across ten course-semesters (CS and mechanical engineering, 3,784 enrollments, 1,065 questions, 256,168 submissions), a generator's exam correct rate averaged within about 5 percentage points of its last practice-attempt correct rate (equivalence test at δ=5% passed in 9 of 10 course-semesters). Within-generator variant variance was not measured, and 5 points is an average across generators, not a per-generator bound. — [Chen et al. 2019](https://zilles.web.engr.illinois.edu/papers/chen_predicting_difficulty_las_2019.pdf) [OBS]
- Sud, West, Zilles (ASEE 2019): an algorithm that builds exams from pools using historical performance roughly halves the standard deviation of generated exam difficulty on a synthetic example, with a "negligible" effect on cheating-cost measures. — [Sud et al. 2019](https://peer.asee.org/reducing-difficulty-variance-in-randomized-assessments) [abstract-level, simulation]
- Mahinpei, Xu, Wolfman, Moosvi propose six randomization levels built bottom-up from 186 questions in two courses, arguing "not all randomization techniques are equally effective in generating question isomorphs." (The PL research page lists this at SIGCSE 2024; a full-paper version appears in the ICER 2025 program.) — [ICER 2025 page](https://icer2025.acm.org/details/icer-2025-papers/21/A-Generalized-Framework-for-Describing-Question-Randomization); [PL research page](https://www.prairielearn.com/research) [DESC]
- For exam security, parameter randomization with pools of about four problems was enough to make collusion between early and late test-takers statistically insignificant (a cited study). — [Zilles et al. CBTF 2019](https://scitepress.org/Papers/2019/77533/pdf/index.html) [2nd]; same claim in [Zilles et al. FIE 2018](https://zilles.web.engr.illinois.edu/papers/zilles_cbtf_fie_2018.pdf) [2nd]

**Second-chance, frequent and mastery testing**
- Morphew, Silva, Herman, West (Applied Cognitive Psychology 2020): quasi-experimental, one undergraduate engineering course; frequent testing combining mastery testing with second-chance testing gave students 7 percentage points higher scores, twice as many As and half as many failing grades; the advantage held for multiple-choice and free-response and after controlling for ability, and women and underrepresented-minority students benefited equally. Sample sizes and second-chance rules are not on the abstract page; data are FERPA-restricted. — [Morphew et al. 2020](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/frequent-mastery-testing-with-second-chance-exams-leads-to-enhanc/) [QE]
- CBTF position paper: in mechanics of materials, replacing two long midterms with five shorter CBTF exams roughly halved D and F grades and doubled A grades on an identical retained final; second-chance exams are an "equivalent but different" exam replacing part of the grade; auto-grading "works best for building-block skills and structured tasks"; building generators takes "significant up-front effort" and deploying them as homework first helps test them. — [Zilles et al. CBTF 2019](https://scitepress.org/Papers/2019/77533/pdf/index.html) [position paper, OBS]
- Herman et al. (ICER 2025), quasi-experimental in a computer architecture course: 11 exams with no second chance versus 3 or 4 exams with second chances showed no significant difference on a common final; first-attempt exams were better under frequent testing; the least frequent regime found the course harder and more stressful; the authors conclude that adding second chances to frequent exams "does not improve exam performance, but may improve the overall student learning experience." — [ICER 2025 page](https://icer2025.acm.org/details/icer-2025-papers/9/Frequent-Testing-vs-Second-chance-Testing-An-Exploration) [QE; sample size not on page]
- Herman et al. (ICER 2020), one architecture course, no randomization: "90-cap" grade replacement (N=356 and 261) versus "90-10" (90% best plus 10% worst, N=353). Most students scored higher on the retake; 90-10 raised retake rates for A students (0.6% to 2.0%) and B students (16% to 32%); retakers added only about 15 to 20% more study effort; later-exam gains were small (Exam 4 d=0.28; final d=0.15); DFW 10.1% versus 11.9% (not significant); the policy did not help the weakest students. — [Herman et al. 2020](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10200288) [QE]
- A companion study of three second-chance policies found policy affected whether students retook, adding a second chance did not change first-exam performance or first-exam study habits, and retakers studied "an additional 60% of their original effort" (in tension with the 15 to 20% in the 2020 study; different courses and measures). — [Herman et al. 2019](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/second-chance-testing-course-policies-and-student-behavior/) [QE, abstract only]
- Emeka et al. (ASEE 2023): two sophomore engineering courses, one policy returning a modest share of lost points with insurance (grade cannot drop) and one returning most lost points without insurance; 513 students surveyed, 7 instructors and 11 students interviewed; preference "almost perfectly split" (insurance helps anxiety; no insurance pushes remediation). — [Emeka et al. 2023](https://peer.asee.org/determining-the-best-policies-for-second-chance-tests-for-stem-students) [QE + SURV]
- Emeka et al. (ASEE 2023, anxiety): 23 interviews and a 448-student survey across 7 courses; test anxiety fell by 30% with second-chance testing, and most students still worked hard on first attempts (no sign of complacency); no grade outcomes on the page. — [Emeka et al. 2023b](https://peer.asee.org/second-chance-testing-as-a-means-of-reducing-students-test-anxiety-and-improving-outcomes) [SURV]
- CBTF operations: most exams allow multiple submissions "with partial credit that decreases with each attempt"; at Fall 2017 scale 52,224 exams, about 6,000 students; under $2 per exam. — [Zilles et al. FIE 2018](https://zilles.web.engr.illinois.edu/papers/zilles_cbtf_fie_2018.pdf) [DESC]

**More tries are not more learning (physics online homework, same-variant retries)**
- Kortemeyer compared four semesters of calculus-based physics (typically over 500 students) allowing 10, 12, 20 and 99 tries with immediate correctness feedback and no penalty: solved 98.0%, 96.2%, 97.0%, 91.8%; given up 1.6%, 3.4%, 2.9%, 8.2%. Beyond the first one or two attempts, solved and abandoned counts decay exponentially; the author concludes students do not make productive use of later attempts and that "higher numbers of allowed attempts lead to less successful homework completion." Table 1 is not monotonic: the 10-try course had the highest solve rate, which the paper calls counterintuitive, and the share that ran out of tries was 0.4%, 0.4%, 0.1% and 0.0%. The introduction cites earlier work (Kortemeyer 2009) putting guessing at roughly 30 to 50% of submissions (not tested in this study). The excerpt has no exam or learning outcomes. — [Kortemeyer, arXiv 1407.2276](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1407.2276) [OBS, different semesters and instructors]

### Inferences
- Our build-time expansion is PL's randomization frozen into a bounded pool. Given FSRS spacing (a skill is reviewed days apart) and PL's observed median of about 6 attempts per question, 50 to 200 instances per template is plenty, but only if instances are real isomorphs: Fowler/Chen/Mahinpei imply isomorphism must be designed and measured, not assumed.
- Instance-level performance tracking (first-try success per `<template>--<seed>`) is the single-user analogue of the Illinois "trim outlier questions" practice; with sparse single-user data it only works pooled (per template across the user's history), so the build-time structural sweep (same step tags, same answer shape, no degenerate numbers) carries most of the weight.
- PL's two modes map onto ours: Homework ≈ learning phase and reviews (fresh instance after a miss); Exam ≈ practice tests (declining value per attempt resembles our rating map: first-try correct is Good, second-try is Hard). Second-chance exams ≈ "miss, see solution, retest on an equivalent but different instance," which is exactly what a bounded pool of isomorphs buys.
- Kortemeyer, the PL 2015 data ("few persisted beyond three incorrect attempts") and PL's own default (new variant per wrong homework attempt) all point the same way: our default of two attempts per instance then show the solution is well supported; unlimited same-instance retries invite guess-and-check.
- The second-chance evidence is policy-level and mostly Illinois engineering/CS; the ICER 2025 result suggests frequency of testing, not the second chance itself, may drive learning, which supports our daily-queue design more than it supports any special "retake" feature.
- PL's `rtol` default of 1% for numeric answers is 10 times looser than our 0.1% default for `number`; see Q2.
- PL checks symbolic equivalence with SymPy first and surfaces a timeout as an invalid submission; our numeric-first pipeline with an undecidable-to-self-grade fallback is more robust and should be kept.
- Unverified whether the 3PL "Mastery Score" of 2015 is still what PL runs; current docs describe streak-style points (not IRT), so treat the 2015 model as history.

## 2. Physics: Mastering Physics, WebAssign, Expert TA, Physics Classroom, Andes

### Takeaway
Mainstream physics homework systems grade numeric answers with a percentage tolerance (WebAssign default ±2%, Mastering typically 2 to 3%), make significant-figure and unit checking optional, and rely on author-written feedback for specific wrong answers. Documented pain points are typing math, all-or-nothing numeric grading, and hidden intermediate results. The strongest effectiveness evidence is not about any commercial product: it is that step-level feedback (Andes-style, d≈0.76) beats answer-only feedback (d≈0.31), that elaborated feedback beats right/wrong feedback, and that web homework is at least as good as paper.

### Cited Findings

**Tolerance, significant figures and units in vendor documentation**
- WebAssign: default numerical tolerance ±2% (also phrased "±2% or ±1 at the last significant digit"); tolerance cannot sensibly be 0 ("often marks correct answers wrong"; a tiny value such as ±0.01% is suggested); symbolic-mode questions set tolerance in question code; the page does not address intermediate rounding. — [WebAssign tolerance](https://help.cengage.com/webassign/instructor_guide/webassign/c_i_numerical_tolerance.htm) [DOC]
- WebAssign sig-fig checking applies only to questions showing a sig-fig icon, can be switched on or off per assignment, sets tolerance in units of the last significant digit (0 to 9 at class level), gives partial credit for too many sig figs and none for too few, encodes rules for add/subtract (fewest decimal places), multiply/divide (fewest sig figs), logs and antilogs, and recommends turning it off if the course uses different rules; if the key has one sig fig, tolerance is ignored. — [WebAssign sig figs](https://help.cengage.com/webassign/instructor_guide/webassign/t_i_setting_significant_figure_checking_options_for_assignments.htm) [DOC]
- WebAssign units: compatible units are accepted by conversion (miles key accepts yards, feet, kilometers; acres are rejected); partial credit applies only when units are compatible but the number is wrong; a numerically correct answer with missing or wrong units gets no partial credit; default class setting gives none. — [WebAssign units](https://help.cengage.com/webassign/instructor_guide/webassign/t_i_setting_until_partial_credit_for_classes.htm) [DOC]
- Mastering (Pearson): accepts a number within a percentage of the official answer, typically "between 2% and 3%" (no single default), does not grade significant figures, tells the student when an accepted answer was rounded differently, and advises that multistep solutions keep extra digits and round only at the end (example: 26 × 8.0 = 210, accepted range 206 to 214 at 2%). — [Pearson grading tolerance](https://help.pearsoncmg.com/mastering/instructor/standalone/Topics/grading_tolerance_and_sig_figs.htm) [DOC]
- PL `pl-number-input` default `rtol` 1e-2 (1%) and `pl-units-input` modes: see Q1. Our spec default is 0.1% for `number`. — [card-format.md](/docs/card-format.md)

**Feedback and hints**
- Expert TA separates hints (static, tied to the question) from feedback (a response to the student's specific wrong answer); it says its feedback was written from five years of student data from hundreds of schools, with instructor-authored Socratic wording; the post gives no outcome data, no description of how answers are matched to errors, and no answer-type list. — [Expert TA blog](https://blog.theexpertta.com/hints-and-feedback) [DOC]
- Andes (physics homework tutor): students draw vectors and axes, define variables, and enter scalar equations; each entry turns green or red immediately; equations are checked by substituting stored solution values after a dimensional check; blank fields or missing units trigger unsolicited slip messages; answer slots expect three significant figures while equations need about nine digits of precision "which discourages number-passing"; "What's Wrong Help" gives a pointing hint, a teaching hint and a bottom-out hint, and bottom-out hints cost points; syntactic "what's wrong" hints "did little good" and were replaced by knowledge-based error handlers; the authors "do not know which of the many features" drives its effect. — [Andes lessons learned](https://www.public.asu.edu/~kvanlehn/distrib/AndesLessonsLearnedForWeb.pdf) [DOC/DESC]
- Mastering Engineering's vector tool: a wrong answer triggers an explanation of why it is wrong without revealing the answer, and students can request declarative hints or Socratic hints that split the problem into sub-problems. — [Mastering Engineering transcript](https://pmark.pearsoncmg.com/northamerica/masteringengineering/vector-drawing-tool-video-transcript/index.html) [DOC]
- University of Sydney trial of MasteringPhysics (2004, about 250 students, 128 survey responses): hints and immediate feedback were the most praised features; hints carried a 3% penalty per section for wrong answers and a 3% bonus for not using them; attempts were raised from 4 to 6 after student feedback; hiding solutions made errors cascade through multi-part questions, so solutions were revealed; typing symbols, subscripts, exponents and matching brackets caused frequent problems (a start-of-semester tutorial and the "Display Math" button helped); "all or nothing" numeric questions were criticized; students reported correct answers marked wrong, but the author could not reproduce it; over 60% never used the system for private tutorials; students created fake identities to extract answers. Subjective, uncontrolled, different-cohort comparison. — [Sydney report](https://www.physics.usyd.edu.au/~obyrne/MPreport_v2.pdf) [SURV/DESC]
- Physics Classroom "Concept Builders" are "interactive questioning modules that target student understanding of discrete topics" (200+ builders in 18 topic areas, 74 of them chemistry) with Task Tracker progress tracking; the page gives no answer formats, feedback rules or evidence. — [Physics Classroom](https://www.physicsclassroom.com/Concept-Builders) [DOC]

**Effectiveness evidence**
- Step-based versus answer-based tutoring (VanLehn 2011 meta-analysis, effect sizes versus no tutoring): answer-based 0.31 (165 effects, 40% reliable), step-based 0.76 (28, 68%), substep-based 0.40 (26, 54%), human 0.79 (10, 80%); answer-based means the student works on paper and enters only a final answer; step-based means each step (an equation, a vector) is an interaction, as in Andes. In Andes, dialogue-style substep tutoring added no clear advantage; for quantitative physics Cordillera did no better than Andes (conceptual problems d=0.50). — [VanLehn slides](https://www.nectec.or.th/icce2011/speakers/ICCE2011_kurt_20111117.pdf) [MA, summarized in slides]; abstract: human d=0.79, intelligent tutoring systems d=0.76 — [ERIC EJ946764](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ946764) [MA]
- Andes versus paper homework: in the second Andes1 evaluation the post-test mean was "approximately 1 standard deviation higher" (Schulze et al. 2000, as reported by VanLehn et al. 2005); sample sizes not given in the text I could read. — [Andes IJAIED paper](https://www.public.asu.edu/~kvanlehn/ITScourse2009/Readings/IJAIED410VanLehnv7.pdf) [QE, 2nd within primary paper]
- Feedback type (van der Kleij, Feskens, Eggen 2015, 40 studies, 70 effects, computer-based learning): elaborated feedback 0.49, knowledge of correct response 0.32, knowledge of results (right/wrong only) 0.05; elaborated feedback was notably better for higher-order outcomes; delayed feedback had smaller effects (no significant timing-by-level interaction); effects were larger in mathematics than in science. — [van der Kleij et al. 2015](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/86y84/effects-of-feedback-in-a-computer-based-learning-environment-on-students-learning-outcomes-a-meta-analysis) [MA]
- Web versus paper homework (Bonham, Deardorff, Beichner, two quasi-experiments at NC State, about 230 and 120 students): no significant difference in conceptual gain (FMCE 18.9% versus 20.1%) or test averages in the calculus-based course (75.4 versus 73.3, p=0.24); the algebra-based web section scored higher on tests (84.2 versus 77.3, p=0.02) but homework method was not a significant predictor in regression; the authors conclude computer-graded homework has no significant pedagogical cost. — [Bonham et al.](https://projects.cos.ncsu.edu/physics_ed/Articles/HomeworkComparisonResearch.pdf) [QE]
- Attempt limits: see Kortemeyer in Q1 (more tries did not mean more solved problems). — [Kortemeyer](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1407.2276) [OBS]
- No independent controlled evidence on Mastering Physics, WebAssign or Expert TA was retrievable; the Sydney comparison links a liked system to higher exam marks (62.2 versus 56.1) but the authors note better students liked it more. — [Sydney report](https://www.physics.usyd.edu.au/~obyrne/MPreport_v2.pdf)

### Inferences
- A 0.1% default tolerance is a math default; measured physical quantities need roughly 1 to 3% (PL 1%, WebAssign 2%, Mastering 2 to 3%) because constants differ (9.8 versus 9.81 differ by 0.1%) and students round mid-calculation. The existing rule that every stored `wrong` answer must grade incorrect (MATH-13) is the natural guard against a tolerance that is too wide.
- Significant figures should be advisory by default (Mastering ignores them; WebAssign makes them opt-in per question): accept inside tolerance, show a "your answer has N sig figs" note, and offer a strict mode for courses that grade them.
- Units need three outcomes, not two: right dimension and value (correct), right dimension wrong value (incorrect, specific feedback), wrong dimension (incorrect, "units don't match"). Missing units on a numerically right answer is a design choice: WebAssign marks it wrong; Andes treats it as a slip prompt. Because a false "wrong" corrupts the schedule (spec principle), a non-consuming "add units" prompt is the safer default.
- Multi-letter and subscripted symbols (`mgh`, `v_0`, `a_x`, Greek letters) are normal in physics; the current rule that two letters never multiply needs a card-level declared-symbol table. Sydney's typing complaints show input friction is the main usability cost; the existing "read as" preview and math keyboard are the right mitigations.
- Step-level feedback is the best-supported lever (0.76 versus 0.31); our multi-part boxes and step-tagged solutions capture part of it cheaply. Do not hide the correct intermediate value after a part is finished or failed (Sydney: errors cascade).
- Our computed `wrong:` answers (per instance) are the equivalent of Expert TA and Mastering author-listed wrong answers, and better because values change per instance. Elaborated feedback is the evidence-backed form; bare "Not quite" on the first miss (our spec) is knowledge-of-results feedback by design, so the elaborated content should arrive no later than the second miss or the solution screen.

### Gaps
- Mastering Physics efficacy summary PDF redirected to a generic page (not read); no source on Mastering Physics's own graph, vector or FBD tools beyond the Mastering Engineering transcript; WebAssign's non-numeric question types not retrieved; Expert TA answer types and error-matching logic undocumented in retrieved pages; Andes per-year effect sizes not retrieved; Physics Classroom formats not retrieved; PER papers by Kortemeyer (other than the tries paper), Pritchard/MIT and others were not read.

## 3. Chemistry: ALEKS, Sapling/Achieve, Mastering Chemistry, OWL, structure drawing

### Takeaway
Chemistry adds three answer families beyond numbers: formulas and equations (a notation with charges, states and coefficients), drawn structures (Lewis, skeletal, mechanisms) and stoichiometry with units. Vendors handle formulas with restricted notations (ChemTeX) and sometimes plain exact-match (no equivalence), and handle drawings with licensed sketchers whose strictness the author configures (hydrogens, lone pairs, stereo). Structure answers are expensive and error-prone to author, even when reviewed. Open-source building blocks exist for an offline app (Ketcher, Apache-2.0, standalone WASM mode; RDKit.js, BSD-3), but they rely on WebAssembly, which touches our CSP rule. Independent effectiveness evidence for chemistry platforms was not found.

### Cited Findings
- ALEKS (vendor-affiliated paper): built on knowledge space theory (Doignon and Falmagne 1985), covers math, chemistry, statistics and accounting, about 4 to 5 million students a year; answers are open-ended rather than multiple choice so lucky guesses are "very unlikely", with an "I don't know" option; the initial assessment stops at 30 questions (one a random extra problem); learning mode picks from the "outer fringe" and scores an item +1 for a correct answer, +2 for a second consecutive correct, −1 for a wrong one, learned at 5; progress assessments re-test and can remove learned items; retention analysis on 8,352,006 extra problems from 6,701,233 students (2016 to 2019) shows correct rates plateauing near 0.6 for the innermost layer and about 0.8 for deep layers; assessment AUROC 0.86 to 0.89 (math courses, not chemistry-specific). — [ALEKS KST paper](https://www.mheducation.com/content/dam/mhe/research/prek-12/essa-tier-4/aleks-practical-perspective-knowledge-space-theory.pdf) [VND, OBS]
- Chemical equation input at Digital Ed (a separate vendor, shown as a documented example): keyboard syntax (`^` charges, `_` subscripts, `->`, `<=>`, states in parentheses); spaces ignored; "Doesn't accept equivalent responses ... must be an exact match", so species order and states must match, and the question text must tell students the expected order. — [Digital Ed](https://www.digitaled.com/support/help/admin/Content/INST-AUTHORING/QUESTION-TYPES/MATH-FORMULA/Author-chemical-equation-sub-type.htm) [DOC]
- Mastering's chemical formula answer type: authors write correct answers in ChemTeX (a restricted TeX subset), can mark both CH3OH and CH4O as correct, and can attach wrong-answer feedback per incorrect combination ("Incorrect; Try Again" then the feedback text, optionally with a linked hint). The formula evaluator compares formulas (whole answer, or the count of compounds matching) and supports skeletal and mechanism questions; its page does not say how ordering, balancing, phases or charges are handled. — [Pearson chemical formula](https://help.pearsoncmg.com/mastering/authoring/simple_editor/Topics/chemical_formula.htm); [Pearson formula evaluator](https://help.pearsoncmg.com/mastering/authoring/simple_editor/Topics/chem_formula_evaluator.htm) [DOC]
- Mastering's Lewis-structure grading parameters: hydrogens (default on, applies only to carbon atoms), lone pairs and radicals (default on), cis/trans (default off), chirality (default off; needs wedge bonds; incompatible with Fischer and Haworth), question type draw, select or both; the author sets the same parameters on any incorrect answers used for wrong-answer feedback; formal charges, resonance and partial credit are not addressed on the page. — [Pearson Lewis grading](https://help.pearsoncmg.com/mastering/authoring/simple_editor/Topics/grading_parameters_lewis.htm) [DOC]
- WebAssign's Marvin JS answer type "supports drawing and grading of Lewis structures, reactions and mechanisms," with optional partially drawn starting structures and a "Clean" tool; grading criteria are not described. — [WebAssign Marvin JS](https://help.cengage.com/webassign/student_guide/webassign/t_s_answering_marvinsketch.htm) [DOC]
- ALEKS's authoring page for typed balanced-equation answers only links a video; no grading rules are stated in text. — [ALEKS authoring](https://mheducation.com/highered/support/aleks/assignments/authoring-question-with-chemical-equation-answer.html) [DOC]
- Sapling Learning authoring study (Zurcher, Phadke, Coppola, McNeil, J. Chem. Educ. 2016, organic chemistry): 1,293 student-written questions, 1,114 passed review; 5,403 specific feedback responses across 1,053 questions (88% addressed a misconception, 11% hinted at a minor error, 1% missing or wrong), 97% of questions had a general hint; a random external review of 113 questions found 27% needed no edits, 55% had technical or formatting problems, 18% had content errors, and 16 of those 20 content errors were missing counterions or lone pairs (only 4 were wrong chemistry); benzene was programmed with one resonance form only; curved-arrow topics produced most missing solution explanations; learning effectiveness was not measured; authors were paid, self-selected students at one institution. — [Zurcher et al. 2016](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~bcoppola/publications/88-JCESapling.pdf) [DESC/OBS]
- Ketcher (EPAM): Apache-2.0; `StandaloneStructServiceProvider` runs Indigo as WebAssembly "without server dependency" and "provides offline capabilities" (needs WebAssembly); the alternative `RemoteStructServiceProvider` calls an Indigo Service over REST; reads MOL V2000/V3000, RXN, KET, SMILES, SMARTS, InChI, CML, CDXML and more, writes InChI Key, PNG and SVG; embeds as a React component or in an iframe; several methods (for example `getSmiles`) throw in macromolecules mode. — [Ketcher](https://github.com/epam/ketcher) [DOC]
- RDKit.js (MinimalLib): BSD-3-Clause, WebAssembly build with zero dependencies, can run in a Web Worker, no UI components included, needs `await initRDKitModule()` and bundler workarounds for the `.wasm` file; the retrieved page documents SMILES parsing and writing, atom counts and descriptors but does not list InChI, substructure or stereo support. — [RDKit.js](https://github.com/rdkit/rdkit-js) [DOC]
- ChemAxon (Marvin JS): forum threads from 2012 and 2016 show ChemAxon granting a "FreeWeb" license to free, non-commercial, no-login school sites on request; terms for embedding in an offline desktop application or redistributing are not stated. — [ChemAxon forum](https://forum.chemaxon.com/20/9125/freeweb-license) [DOC, dated]

### Inferences
- Formulas and equations can be graded better than the two vendor styles described: parse to a canonical multiset (element, count, charge, state), check an equation by atom and charge conservation, treat coefficient scaling and species order as equal unless the card asks for lowest integer coefficients or a given order. This is the chemistry analogue of the spec's "compare meaning, not text" rule, and it needs no external library.
- Drawn-structure answers are the costliest family: the grading knobs (hydrogens, lone pairs, charges, stereo) are many, and Zurcher's data show authors get lone pairs and counterions wrong even with review. A pack-build structure validator (valence, charge, lone-pair accounting) would be needed before any drawing item ships. SMILES and InChI do not carry explicit lone pairs, so Lewis-structure grading needs an electron-aware representation (design needed; no source found).
- Early chemistry coverage need not draw anything: number with units (quantity), typed formulas, balanced equations, choice, and "select the structure/atom" items cover most introductory stoichiometry, nomenclature and periodic-trend skills.
- Both Ketcher standalone and RDKit.js need WebAssembly; our CSP forbids `unsafe-eval`, and whether `wasm-unsafe-eval` is acceptable (and works in WKWebView) must be decided in an ADR. An alternative is Ketcher's remote mode backed by a loopback service in the Python core. Neither is a 1.0 concern.
- ALEKS's open-response default supports keeping multiple choice a minority answer type (guess resistance); its +1/+2/−1 learning score is a streak rule like PL's, and its progress checks are the analogue of FSRS reviews.

### Gaps
- OWL (Cengage): only product pages surfaced, no feature documentation read. Sapling/Achieve and Mastering Chemistry answer-type documentation and efficacy summaries (including the Pearson and McGraw Hill efficacy PDFs that appeared in search) were not read. No independent controlled study of online chemistry homework was retrieved. JSME, Kekule.js, OpenChemLib and ChemDoodle licensing and offline status were not checked. No source on how any vendor grades curved-arrow mechanisms. WKWebView/CSP behavior for WebAssembly not verified.

## 4. Statistics: WebAssign, MyLab Statistics, OpenIntro, guidance

### Takeaway
Little platform documentation and no effectiveness studies for online statistics practice were retrievable. What exists points to design needs rather than proven designs: tolerance and rounding policies that accept table-based and software-based answers, data and plots in context, built-in distribution calculators, and a stronger emphasis on interpretation and procedure choice than on table lookups (GAISE 2016).

### Cited Findings
- GAISE College Report (2016), six recommendations: teach statistical thinking; focus on conceptual understanding; integrate real data with context; foster active learning; use technology; use assessments. It says to "de-emphasize t-, normal and other probability tables" (p. 21), calls "drills with z-, t-, χ², and F-tables" "no longer necessary" (p. 24), advises "Perform most computations using technology" (p. 16), says calculators offer "limited functionality for smaller datasets" (p. 19), that "Being able to calculate a p-value is not enough" (p. 21), and recommends items on "choosing good interpretations of graphs or selecting appropriate statistical procedures" (p. 22) and minimizing hypothetical data (p. 17). — [GAISE College Report](https://www.amstat.org/docs/default-source/amstat-documents/gaisecollege_full.pdf) [guideline, expert consensus]
- A real table-versus-exact conflict: WeBWorK Open Problem Library bug 4100 (2018): a sample-size problem tells students to use z* from a z-table but keys 2.576; tables give 2.575; the exact value is 2.575829; students following the instruction get marked wrong; the reporter proposes a tolerance accepting all three. — [WeBWorK bug 4100](https://webwork.maa.org/mailman_archives/archives/public/webwork-bugs/2018-March/007061.html) [DESC]
- Rounding guidance from physics vendors applies equally: keep extra digits and round only at the end; typical tolerance 2 to 3% (Mastering) or ±2% (WebAssign). — [Pearson](https://help.pearsoncmg.com/mastering/instructor/standalone/Topics/grading_tolerance_and_sig_figs.htm); [WebAssign](https://help.cengage.com/webassign/instructor_guide/webassign/c_i_numerical_tolerance.htm) [DOC]
- MyLab Statistics (Pearson) page lists integrated StatCrunch (web-based statistical software with shareable data sets), Dynamic Study Modules, Integrated Review of prerequisites and an AI study tool; it names no graphing/drawing tools, algorithmic exercises, multi-part exercises or interpretation question formats, and cites no outcome data (it links to a separate "Product Evidence" page not read). — [MyLab Statistics](https://www.pearson.com/en-us/higher-education/products-services/mylab/statistics.html) [DOC/marketing]
- ALEKS includes statistics among its courses (same KST paper as Q3). — [ALEKS KST paper](https://www.mheducation.com/content/dam/mhe/research/prek-12/essa-tier-4/aleks-practical-perspective-knowledge-space-theory.pdf) [VND]
- OpenIntro's home page offers solutions and sample exams to verified teachers and mentions slides, videos, forums and a GitHub link; it does not describe student-facing quizzes, labs or auto-graded practice. — [OpenIntro](https://www.openintro.org/) [DOC]

### Inferences
- Statistics answers break the current `number` assumptions: probabilities from a rounded z-table and from software can differ by more than 0.1% relative (for example 2.575 versus 2.576 differ by 0.04%, but a table-based p-value after rounding z to two decimals can differ by 1% or more); small probabilities near 0 call for absolute tolerance; keys need explicit alternates (table path, technology path) or a card-level intermediate-rounding policy.
- Needed shapes: pairs and intervals for confidence intervals, `choice` for decisions (reject or fail to reject) and for "best interpretation" items (auto-gradable and aligned with GAISE), numbers with several accepted keys, and rubric-graded `self` for written conclusions.
- Needed content features: datasets shown as tables, pre-rendered histograms, boxplots and scatterplots (build-time SVG, since packs ship no code and `plot` today draws functions only), and an in-app distribution calculator as a declared tool (generalizing the calculator flag, MATH-16).

### Gaps
- No empirical study of online statistics homework found; WebAssign and MyLab statistics-specific question types not documented in retrieved pages; no source on how any platform grades free-text interpretation (keywords, rubrics); OpenIntro licensing text and any student practice tools not retrieved; no source for StatCrunch-style data entry design.

## 5. Programming and CS: CodeRunner, zyBooks, Runestone, LeetCode, Exercism, Execute Program, Parsons, tracing

### Takeaway
Execution-based graders (CodeRunner/Jobe, Exercism test runners) need an OS-level sandbox that fits a server, not a Mac app with no-eval rules; in-webview Pyodide is not a safe boundary (2026 disclosures). The formats that need no execution at study time are the ones with the best cost/benefit evidence: Parsons problems (graded by order/dependency, about equal learning in much less time in randomized studies, faded variants better for pattern learning) and code tracing / predict-the-output (a short structured strategy helped in a small randomized study, but tracing alone transferred poorly to writing). A fresh predict-the-output instance can have its answer computed at build time, which fits the pack rules.

### Cited Findings

**Execution-based graders and sandboxing**
- Jobe (CodeRunner's sandbox): Linux only (Apache, PHP, C toolchain); runs each job as a low-privilege user through `runguard`; defaults CPU 5 s, memory 400 MB (higher for Python), 20 processes, 2 MB stdout; uses cgroups since 1.9.0; "it does not restrict any system calls"; does not isolate the network (admins must firewall); must run on a dedicated, firewalled server, with "CAVEAT EMPTOR" for open exposure. — [Jobe](https://github.com/trampgeek/jobe) [DOC]
- CodeRunner is a Moodle question type that "runs student-submitted program code in a sandbox to check if it satisfies a given set of tests" (v5.10.6 needs Moodle 4.3+, PHP 8.1+); the repo page describes no Parsons-style types. — [CodeRunner](https://github.com/trampgeek/moodle-qtype_coderunner) [DOC]
- Exercism: a test runner is "software that runs an exercise's tests against a student's code", one per language track, orchestrated by the website; the retrieved page does not document isolation. — [Exercism](https://exercism.org/docs/building/tooling/test-runners) [DOC]
- Pyodide-based sandboxes (Cyera, disclosed at DEF CON 34, August 2026): seven products that ran untrusted Python in Pyodide with import denylists were escapable via `ctypes` to Emscripten exports (for example `emscripten_run_script`) into the host JavaScript runtime; CVEs include n8n CVE-2025-68668 (CVSS 9.9), Grist CVE-2026-24002 (9.1), cohere-terrarium CVE-2026-61522 (9.3), smolagents CVE-2026-10613 (8.3); recommendation: treat Python-level restrictions as one layer, and run untrusted code in a separate process or container with minimal permissions; WebAssembly memory isolation does not stop calls the host deliberately exposes. The page gives inconsistent dates for smolagents and does not analyze desktop webviews. — [Cyera](https://www.cyera.com/research/sandcastles-not-sandboxes-how-one-architectural-flaw-exposed-seven-products) [DESC, security research]
- Hidden tests matter: Faded Parsons researchers added "at least three hidden test cases" after the study to catch hard-coded answers. — [Weinman et al. CHI 2021](https://acelab.berkeley.edu/wp-content/papercite-data/pdf/parsons-chi2021.pdf)

**Parsons problems (scrambled lines, no code execution needed to grade order)**
- Systematic review (Ericson et al., ITiCSE working group 2022): 141 papers included (a prior review had 34 primary studies); Parsons problems date from 2006; distractors appear in 18.8% of studies with identifiable features, adaptive in 5.8%, faded in 4.3%; feedback is either line-based or execution-based; median study size 102; threats to validity were the least-met quality criterion; only 10 papers carry a cognitive-load tag. The 2023 update added 20 papers; a multi-institution replication on Runestone (reported as 13 institutions in 8 countries, though the table lists 11) had no outcome data in the text I could read. — [Parsons SLR](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf); [ITiCSE 2023 WG report](https://falmouth-test.eprints-hosting.org/5216/1/ITiCSE_2023_WorkingGroup_Parsons_Report.pdf) [MA/SLR]
- Ericson's randomized experiments, as summarized by Poulsen et al.: in one, students learned equal amounts from Parsons problems, fixing code and writing code from scratch; in a second, students who did Parsons and adaptive Parsons problems learned the most. — [Poulsen et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09609) [2nd]
- Weinman et al. cite Ericson et al. for Parsons problems taking about 70% of the time of other exercises; the ITiCSE 2023 working-group text I read could not confirm that figure. — [Weinman et al. 2021](https://acelab.berkeley.edu/wp-content/papercite-data/pdf/parsons-chi2021.pdf) [2nd]; [ITiCSE 2023 WG report](https://falmouth-test.eprints-hosting.org/5216/1/ITiCSE_2023_WorkingGroup_Parsons_Report.pdf)
- Hou, Ericson, Wang (ITiCSE 2023), 89 students (41 with an on-demand adaptive Parsons help, 48 without; randomization not stated): practice score 20.98 versus 10.83 (p=0.003, f²=0.11), efficiency 1.34 versus 0.81 (p=0.041), posttest 15.15 versus 11.51 (p=0.349, not significant). — [Hou et al. 2023](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10510493) [QE/RCT unclear, small]
- Faded Parsons (Weinman, Fox, Hearst, CHI 2021; lines to arrange plus blanks to fill; CS1; participation was extra credit): Study 1 (n=50) pattern acquisition 55.3% faded Parsons versus 20.7% writing (tracing 44.0%; the source table notes a non-significant difference from tracing without saying which condition it compares) and code-writing transfer 85.3% versus 74.7% (tracing 55.3%); Study 2 (n=43, randomized) acquisition 43.3% versus 33.7% (p<.01) and writing transfer 27.7% versus 28.7% (no difference); when free to choose, students chose faded Parsons 77.6% of the time; on one object-oriented pattern (OOP1) writing did better, though not significantly. — [Weinman et al. 2021](https://acelab.berkeley.edu/wp-content/papercite-data/pdf/parsons-chi2021.pdf) [RCT, small]
- Proof Blocks (Parsons-style proofs; Poulsen et al., randomized lab study, 332 analyzed of 451): 11.1 minutes versus 43.4 for written proofs (about four times faster) with equal posttests (51.6 versus 50.3; Kruskal-Wallis p=0.76); feedback names the first incorrect line; three tries before the example solution; no no-activity control group. — [Poulsen et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09609) [RCT]
- Distractors: add time without clear learning gains (cited as refs 69, 70 in Haynes-Magyar 2025) — [Haynes-Magyar 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.22407) [2nd]; reported to raise cognitive load and time and lower success, recommended against in summative assessment, with a 576-student CS1 study finding a large time cost; visually paired distractors are easier than mixed ones (Denny et al.). — [ITiCSE 2023 WG report](https://falmouth-test.eprints-hosting.org/5216/1/ITiCSE_2023_WorkingGroup_Parsons_Report.pdf) [2nd]
- The cost of building: PL's `pl-order-blocks` grades order by dependency graph without running code (see Q1), supports indentation, paired distractors and first-wrong-line feedback. — [pl-order-blocks](https://docs.prairielearn.com/elements/pl-order-blocks/) [DOC]

**Code tracing and predict-the-output**
- Xie, Nelson, Ko (SIGCSE 2018), 24 CS1 students block-randomized (13 strategy, 11 control), 5 to 10 minutes teaching a "memory table" tracing strategy: 30.00 versus 24.55 of 36 on six predict-the-output problems (p=0.049, d=0.72); midterm 91.5 versus 84.5 (p=0.083, self-reported grades for 17 of 24, 3 to 6 days later); tables were untested for arrays and objects; low performers without the strategy often skipped steps or gave up. — [Xie et al. 2018](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Xie2018TracingStrategies.pdf) [RCT, very small]
- In the faded-Parsons study, code tracing gave "100% pattern exposure but 0% active exposure" and transferred poorly to writing (55.3% versus 85.3% for faded Parsons), so it was dropped from Study 2. — [Weinman et al. 2021](https://acelab.berkeley.edu/wp-content/papercite-data/pdf/parsons-chi2021.pdf) [RCT, small]

**Platforms**
- Execute Program (spaced-repetition programming lessons in TypeScript, Python, JavaScript, SQL, regular expressions): per Andy Matuschak's notes, readers answer prompts by running code ("no prompts that can't be executed"), each prompt is both practice and recall, lessons are short, a lesson unlocks only after prerequisites are reviewed successfully, and a missed review answer is not penalized unless the learner gives up; how exercises vary is not described. The vendor's page contains only marketing text. — [Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Execute_Program); [Execute Program](https://www.executeprogram.com/spaced-repetition) [secondary notes, DOC]
- zyBooks in an introductory circuit analysis course (ASEE 2025, N=345, non-randomized, one instructor, seven semesters): auto-graded end-of-chapter-style problems with multiple answer fields, randomized values on each attempt and a full solution after an incorrect attempt; when activities counted for points, A to C grades rose from 45% to 57% (p=0.005) and activity time rose about five-fold; confounded by changes in paper homework and semester mix. — [zyBooks circuit paper](https://www.zybooks.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Student_Performance_Improvement_in_an_Introductory_Circuit_Analysis_Course_when_Interactive_Web-Native_Textbook__zyBook__Activities_are_Assigned_for_Points.pdf) [QE, weak]

### Inferences
- A tiered coding ladder fits our constraints: (T0) choice and cloze over code; (T1) Parsons with dependency-graph grading, no distractors by default (or paired), no execution; (T2) trace and predict-the-output, where the pipeline runs a reference interpreter at build time to compute each instance's answer (the pack ships code as text, not as something the app runs, consistent with PACK-3); (T3) spot-the-bug (pick a line) and complexity answers; (T4) write-code with hidden tests, later, in a separate sandboxed process.
- T4 needs its own ADR because it executes student-written text (AGENTS.md rule 3 is about the math parser, but its spirit applies) and because Pyodide-in-webview is rejected by the Cyera findings: our webview hosts a JavaScript bridge to the core, so an escape would reach the database. A separate OS-level process with no access to the profile folder is the direction the sources support; no macOS-specific sandbox source was retrieved. The scheduled C++ environment makes this decision unavoidable but not urgent.
- Tracing evidence is real but thin (n=24); the better-supported move is to pair tracing with Parsons or fill-in items, because tracing alone does not transfer to writing.
- Execute Program's design (executable prompts, prerequisite gating, forgiving misses) is a useful comparator for skill-per-card spaced repetition of code idioms, but no outcome data exist.

### Gaps
- LeetCode, Runestone and Exercism isolation details were not retrieved; no evidence found specifically on spaced repetition of programming concepts; Lister/Lopez/Venables tracing-and-writing studies were not read; ICER 2024 "Scaffolding Novices" Parsons paper only seen as a search hit; no macOS sandbox documentation; Execute Program's intervals and variation not documented; Ericson's primary papers not read (secondary summaries only, including the 70%-of-the-time figure, which rests on one secondary citation).

## 6. Engineering: statics and circuits

### Takeaway
Statics needs force and moment vectors with sign conventions, free-body-diagram (FBD) drawing, and reactions/member forces; circuits need complex phasors, SI-prefixed units, expressions in s or jω, and schematics. The documented tools are bespoke: PL's `pl-drawing` grades placed vectors and components by tolerance, Mechanix recognizes sketches, Mastering Engineering has a vector tool. Controlled evidence is sparse, with one randomized PL study showing an FBD-specific benefit. Typed or choose-from-options alternatives cover a lot before any drawing exists.

### Cited Findings
- `pl-drawing` objects include vectors and loads (`pl-vector`, `pl-double-headed-vector`, `pl-paired-vector`, `pl-arc-vector`, `pl-distributed-load`), mechanics (`pl-rod`, `pl-fixed-pin`, `pl-roller`, `pl-clamped`, `pl-spring`, `pl-pulley`), circuits (`pl-capacitor`, `pl-resistor`, `pl-battery`, `pl-switch`, `pl-inductor`) and plot sketching (`pl-axes`, `pl-graph-line`). Grading: position tolerance `tol` defaults to 0.5 × grid size, `angle-tol` to 10 degrees; a point is correct inside a tolerance square; a vector needs its anchor inside an aligned box and the angle within `angle-tol` (`disregard-sense` accepts either direction); extra objects are penalized by default; blank submissions are not graded; no freehand lines (use `pl-sketch` for gradable curve sketches or `pl-excalidraw` for manual grading). A truss object was not visible in the retrieved portion. — [pl-drawing](https://docs.prairielearn.com/pl-drawing/) [DOC; last ~36k characters unread]
- Randomized comparison of a PL paired-vector object for exploded frame FBDs (240 students in one course): final-exam FBD item median 100% versus 60% (p=0.0012); midterm 87% versus 80% (p=0.1255, not significant); some rubric items improved significantly (two-force member 84.4% versus 64.9%, p=0.008); the intervention group also did one more practice problem (confound); 2D frictionless pins only. — [PL paired vector](https://peer.asee.org/improving-understanding-of-reaction-forces-in-free-body-diagrams-using-a-paired-vector-object-in-prairie-learn.pdf) [RCT, confounded]
- Mechanix (sketch-recognition statics tutor, IUI '20 companion): recognizes hand-drawn trusses and force arrows, accepts reversed arrows with the sign flipped, force sums are entered with the student's own variable names; about 350 students at five universities (2018 to 2019); homework scores not significantly different from WileyPlus (56 students, p=0.47); truss recognition F-score 0.994 on 3,000 sketches (1,482 of 1,500 trusses labeled correctly, no non-trusses accepted); scope excludes multi-body problems, distributed loads and applied moments. — [Mechanix](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10166442) [DESC, preliminary]
- Mastering Engineering's vector tool lets students choose a named force from a dropdown and draw it; moments and support reactions are not mentioned. — [Mastering Engineering transcript](https://pmark.pearsoncmg.com/northamerica/masteringengineering/vector-drawing-tool-video-transcript/index.html) [DOC]
- A JSXGraph-conference abstract says interactive JSXGraph input can be evaluated in STACK but "this doesn't apply to free body diagrams," which need a more involved input and evaluation solution. — [JSXGraph conference abstract](https://jsxgraph.uni-bayreuth.de/conf/program/kraska) [DESC]
- Circuit analysis with zyBooks: auto-graded multi-field problems with randomized values and a solution after an incorrect attempt (details and weakness of the evidence in Q5). — [zyBooks circuit paper](https://www.zybooks.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Student_Performance_Improvement_in_an_Introductory_Circuit_Analysis_Course_when_Interactive_Web-Native_Textbook__zyBook__Activities_are_Assigned_for_Points.pdf) [QE, weak]
- Engineering students were less enthusiastic about the CBTF mainly "because auto-graders give no partial credit for correct setup work," and exams with multiple dependent parts "often don't translate well to computerized formats." — [Zilles et al. CBTF 2019](https://scitepress.org/Papers/2019/77533/pdf/index.html) [SURV, position paper]
- Electrical-engineering conventions are visible in PL: `pl-symbolic-input` accepts complex numbers with `i` or `j` and has `imaginary-unit-for-display`; `pl-number-input` has `allow-complex`; `pl-units-input` uses Pint. — [pl-symbolic-input](https://docs.prairielearn.com/elements/pl-symbolic-input/); [pl-number-input](https://docs.prairielearn.com/elements/pl-number-input/); [pl-units-input](https://docs.prairielearn.com/elements/pl-units-input/) [DOC]
- Andes (physics) has students draw vectors and axes and define variables before writing equations. — [Andes](https://www.public.asu.edu/~kvanlehn/distrib/AndesLessonsLearnedForWeb.pdf) [DOC]

### Inferences
- Statics before any drawing tool: numeric/quantity vectors for reactions and member forces with a declared sign convention (counterclockwise-positive moments, tension-positive), and choose-the-correct-FBD from pre-rendered options. Both are auto-gradable with existing machinery; the PL RCT suggests drawing FBDs adds real value later, especially for paired reactions, but it is confounded by extra practice.
- Circuits: quantities with SI prefixes and compound units; complex phasors in rectangular or polar form (tolerance on magnitude and angle); transfer functions and impedances as expressions in `s` or `jω` (numeric sampling at complex points); Boolean expressions and truth tables (set or matrix types). Schematics can be pre-rendered per instance at build time; student-drawn circuits are out of scope.
- The "no partial credit for setup" complaint is a design signal for multi-part engineering problems: score and show each setup step (free-body equations, node equations) as its own part, which is what the spec's per-part boxes already allow.

### Gaps
- No controlled evidence on circuit auto-graders; no SPICE-based grading source; truss support in `pl-drawing` unconfirmed (page truncated); Mastering Engineering grading criteria not described; JSXGraph/STACK FBD concept details not retrieved.

## 7. Cross-cutting: diagram answers, multi-step problems, "show your work", feedback

### Takeaway
Every auto-graded diagram system found is geometry-with-tolerance on a constrained canvas (typed objects, grid snap), with per-problem checkers; freehand is manually graded or special-cased. Step-level checking is the best-supported way to give multi-step feedback, and cheap approximations exist (parts with intermediate answers, revealed correct intermediates). "Show your work" cannot be auto-graded; vendors use uploads with manual grading, and self-assessment is least accurate for the weakest students.

### Cited Findings
- Diagram grading in PL: see Q6 (`tol` default 0.5 × grid, `angle-tol` 10 degrees, extras penalized). PL also offers `pl-sketch` (auto-gradable curve sketches), `pl-excalidraw` (free-form, manual), `pl-image-capture` (capture handwritten work) and `pl-file-upload`. — [pl-drawing](https://docs.prairielearn.com/pl-drawing/); [PL elements index](https://docs.prairielearn.com/elements/) [DOC]
- WeBWorK GraphTool: the author defines a target object (for example `{circle, solid, (h, k), (h + r, k)}`); the graph is "graded based on appearance"; each object type needs its own custom checker; the circle example compares squared distances with `==` and no tolerance (student points snap to the grid); wrong objects get a message naming which object is wrong. — [WeBWorK GraphTool](https://webwork.maa.org/wiki/GraphTool) [DOC; one example only]
- Mastering Lewis structures offer a "select" question type (choose part of a given drawing) alongside "draw"; WebAssign Marvin JS offers partially drawn starting structures. — [Pearson Lewis grading](https://help.pearsoncmg.com/mastering/authoring/simple_editor/Topics/grading_parameters_lewis.htm); [WebAssign Marvin JS](https://help.cengage.com/webassign/student_guide/webassign/t_s_answering_marvinsketch.htm) [DOC]
- Step-based versus answer-based tutoring: see Q2 (0.76 versus 0.31; smaller steps make negative feedback actionable: "debugging short chain is easy" versus "where in long chain was the mistake?"). — [VanLehn slides](https://www.nectec.or.th/icce2011/speakers/ICCE2011_kurt_20111117.pdf) [MA summary]
- Cascade and rounding in multi-part questions: hidden solutions made errors cascade (Sydney); keep extra digits until the end (Pearson); Andes demands about nine digits in equations to discourage "number-passing." — [Sydney report](https://www.physics.usyd.edu.au/~obyrne/MPreport_v2.pdf); [Pearson](https://help.pearsoncmg.com/mastering/instructor/standalone/Topics/grading_tolerance_and_sig_figs.htm); [Andes](https://www.public.asu.edu/~kvanlehn/distrib/AndesLessonsLearnedForWeb.pdf)
- PL multi-answer questions: each answer gets a `partial_scores` entry with optional `weight` and `feedback`, and the question score is their combination. — [PL server.py docs](https://docs.prairielearn.com/question/server/) [DOC]
- Elaborated feedback beats right/wrong feedback (0.49 versus 0.05) and knowledge of the correct response (0.32). — [van der Kleij et al. 2015](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/86y84/effects-of-feedback-in-a-computer-based-learning-environment-on-students-learning-outcomes-a-meta-analysis) [MA]
- Auto-grading "works best for building-block skills and structured tasks. Open-ended work should be graded manually, using a blended approach." — [Zilles et al. CBTF 2019](https://scitepress.org/Papers/2019/77533/pdf/index.html) [position paper]
- Self-assessment accuracy (indirect): in a University of Kentucky biology course (n=310), students were "significantly miscalibrated" on the first exam, with the lowest performers least accurate, and calibration improved over exams; a following-semester intervention (instruction on overestimation plus retrieval practice; n=290 versus 251 control, different instructor) improved calibration and first-exam performance. This measures predicted exam scores, not self-grading against a key. — [Osterhage et al. 2019](https://uknowledge.uky.edu/biology_facpub/174) [QE, confounded by instructor]
- Our spec ships "AI-original proof or olympiad style" items self-graded with a rubric, and lists "rubric-scored long answers in tests" as a later feature. — [spec.md, PACK/Later](/docs/spec.md)

### Inferences
- Cheap diagram substitutes, in order of cost: (1) choose-the-graph/FBD/figure from options rendered from parameters; (2) place-a-point on a snapped grid (reuses the `point` checker with a tolerance); (3) order or dependency blocks; (4) label-the-parts by dragging labels to fixed anchors (graded as set equality); (5) typed features (already the spec's stance on curve sketching); only then (6) a typed-object drawing canvas with PL-style tolerances. The spec's candidate plotting library (JSXGraph) is a plausible base for (2) and (6).
- Multi-step support, cheapest first: independent parts each with its own checked answer (exists); reveal the key's intermediate value when a part is finished or failed; optional "error carried forward" (re-evaluate later parts from the student's earlier answer) for later versions; step-tagged solutions and the error picker (exists) for diagnosis.
- Self-graded items are the least reliable input to the scheduler; prefer an auto-checkable "final answer plus checkpoints" proof of work, and when self-grading is unavoidable use rubric lines that are objective, ask for a confidence rating before the key appears, and keep the "I was right" style disputes out of FSRS until reviewed (the spec already treats rubric maps carefully).

### Gaps
- No evidence found on the accuracy of self-grading against a rubric in STEM problem solving; no source on error-carried-forward auto-grading in practice systems; no accuracy data for curve-sketch recognition; no PER evidence on whether requiring shown work in online systems changes learning.

## 8. Concrete requirements for our answer types, card format and grader (1.0 versus later)

### Takeaway
For 1.0 (Calculus I) the work is architectural seams, not new STEM features: a grader contract that returns a structured verdict, an open answer-type registry that degrades gracefully, pluggable template-function libraries, per-instance quality tracking and room in the card and pack formats for figures, data, tools and tolerance/rounding policy. Units, significant figures and the physics answer family belong to 1.1 (as the spec already says). Statistics, chemistry, programming and engineering extensions are course-pack work, each with one or two decisions (WebAssembly for structure editors, code execution sandbox, drawing canvas) that need ADRs before their packs.

### Cited Findings
- The spec already defers units and significant figures to 1.1 and drawing input beyond 1.0, keeps instance data static, bans eval on user input, and keeps packs free of executable code. — [spec.md](/docs/spec.md); [AGENTS.md](/AGENTS.md)
- Evidence anchors for each requirement below are in Q1 to Q7; the strongest are: step-level feedback (0.76 versus 0.31, [MA](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ946764)), elaborated feedback (0.49 versus 0.05, [MA](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/86y84/effects-of-feedback-in-a-computer-based-learning-environment-on-students-learning-outcomes-a-meta-analysis)), parse-versus-grade separation ([PL docs](https://docs.prairielearn.com/question/server/)), isomorph quality ([Fowler 2022](https://zilles.web.engr.illinois.edu/papers/exam_variant_sigcse_2022.pdf)), attempt limits ([Kortemeyer](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1407.2276)), Parsons efficiency ([Poulsen et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.09609), [Weinman et al.](https://acelab.berkeley.edu/wp-content/papercite-data/pdf/parsons-chi2021.pdf)) and sandbox risk ([Cyera](https://www.cyera.com/research/sandcastles-not-sandboxes-how-one-architectural-flaw-exposed-seven-products)).

**C. Grader behaviors that generalize**
- C1 (1.0 seam). Keep the layered shape-then-exact-then-semantic-then-form pipeline for every type; "can we read this?" stays separate from "is it correct?". Each type's semantic step: numeric sampling (expressions), unit conversion (quantity), atom conservation (equations), topological validity (blocks), string equality after normalization (trace output).
- C2 (1.1). Generic error-class diagnostics computed from the key and the wrong answer feed wrong-answer feedback and the "What went wrong?" picker without per-card authoring: sign flip, factor of 10, 100 or 1000, unit-prefix slip, degrees versus radians, forgot a factor of 2 or π, rounding-only difference. Authored `wrong:` lists remain the primary source (Expert TA, Mastering and Sapling all rely on authored wrong answers; 88% of Sapling's specific feedback addressed a misconception).
- C3 (1.0). Golden suites and property tests per type: unit conversions invariant under any compatible unit, equation checks invariant under coefficient scaling and species order (when allowed), all topological orders of a block set pass and adjacent swaps that break dependencies fail.
- C4 (1.0). Prefer to turn timeouts into "undecidable, self-grade" (spec) rather than PL's student-visible "did not converge."
- C5 (before any code answers). Never execute student-supplied code in the core or web process; an ADR chooses an out-of-process sandbox. Do not use in-webview Pyodide.
- C6 (before any structure or WebAssembly editor). An ADR on `wasm-unsafe-eval` versus a loopback structure service; verify behavior in the actual WKWebView.

**D. Content and pipeline**
- D1 (1.0). Pipeline sweeps check that all instances of a template share one solution structure (extends the 1,000-seed sweep), not only that keys are right.
- D2 (later). Domain wrong-answer generators (physics: sign, unit conversion, g value, degrees; chemistry: coefficient and subscript slips; statistics: one- versus two-tailed, wrong table; CS: off-by-one) analogous to the calculus "known wrong answers".
- D3 (later). Per-domain "required reading" for authors: Zurcher's Sapling data show even reviewed authors err on lone pairs and counterions, so structure-answer packs need an automated validator and sampled human review.

**E. Rollout ordering note**
- Planned course order after Calculus I: Calc 2 and 3, Linear Algebra, Statistics, physics, chemistry. The spec itself names Physics I as the first post-calculus pack (1.1). That order means quantity/units/sig figs (B2 to B6) are needed first, statistics (B7) next, chemistry and CS later. No source decides the order of those later packs.

### Gaps
- Several design choices above rest on inference and engineering judgment, not retrieved evidence: default quantity tolerance (1% versus 2%), missing-units handling, the declared-symbol mechanism, reveal policy for intermediate values, the CSP/WebAssembly question and the macOS sandbox choice. No source quantified how often generic error classes (sign, power of ten) account for wrong answers in physics practice; that would help prioritize C2.
- No comparative usability data on typed-unit entry versus unit pickers; no data on how students interpret "advise" sig-fig notes.
- The evidence base is dominated by Illinois (PL) and US introductory courses; transfer to a single-user, offline, self-study app is inferential.
